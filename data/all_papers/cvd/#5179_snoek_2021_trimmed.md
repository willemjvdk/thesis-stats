# Methods
## Study design

The TeleCaRe study is an investigator-initiated, prospective, single-centre, randomised controlled trial, which was conducted between September 2014 and August 2016. The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by Isala Medical Ethics Committee (reference number: NL48475.075.14) and registered in the Dutch Trial Register: NL4140. Informed written consent was obtained from all participants. Details of the study and the objectives have been published previously.18

## Population and randomisation

We enrolled 122 patients for participation in the outpatient CR programme of the Isala Heart Centre (Zwolle, the Netherlands). Patients were eligible for inclusion if they had a minimum attendance of 80% in CR and at least one of the following indications for CR: acute coronary syndrome (ACS), percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) within three months prior to the start of the CR programme. The exclusion criteria were a contraindication to CR according to the Dutch national guideline,19 mental impairment leading to inability to cooperate, severe impaired (musculoskeletal) ability to exercise, signs of cardiac ischemia and/or positive exercise testing on cardiac ischemia, insufficient knowledge of the Dutch language, no access or no availability or insufficient knowledge of a computer with Internet or an implanted cardiac device (pacemaker). Patients were randomised in blocks of n 4 with a 1:1 ratio to the intervention group with telemonitoring and telecoaching (TELE) or to the control group with a traditional follow-up programme (CON) by means of a computerised allocation system applying an algorithm that prevented the care providers or the investigators from predicting the outcome of the randomisation process.

## Intervention group

The intervention group has been previously described.18 In short, patients in the intervention group were equipped with a smartphone (Samsung Galaxy Ace GT-s5830i, Korea) and Bluetooth-connected heart

rate belt (Zephyr, Annapolis, USA). The device registered training mode, training time and training intensity (determined by heart rate). Subjects were advised, according to international guidelines on physical activity,20 to exercise at least 30 min a day for a total of five days a week, with at least a moderate intensity. Moderate intensity was defined as the intensity above the first ventilatory threshold.21 Patients were free to choose which type of exercise (i.e. walking, cycling, etc.) they would perform to meet the recommendations and were advised to use the device when performing moderate-to-vigorous-intensity exercise. Patients had insight into their training history through an individual webpage and on their smartphone. They were contacted weekly by telephone for supportive guidance in the first month, every other week in the second month and from then on monthly until six months. Recorded physical activity patterns were discussed by telephone and motivational interviewing was used to motivate and stimulate the patient.22 Specialised nurses in CR were trained in motivational interviewing skills. Furthermore, a standard operating procedure, composed with a psychologist, was used for the individual coaching in order to ensure a similar mode of operation. Patients were seen by their cardiologist around three and 12 months after completion of the CR programme, and contacted every month by telephone to register additional care utilisation and adverse events. An overview of the study procedures is presented in supplemental Figure S1.

## Control group

Similar to the intervention group, control patients were seen by their cardiologist around three and 12 months after completion of the CR programme. Furthermore, a research nurse contacted patients in the control group every month by telephone to register care utilisation and potential adverse events. No questions were asked and no advice was given on physical activity. No additional visits were scheduled for control patients.

## Measurements

Cardiopulmonary exercise test. The primary endpoint was the comparison of both groups with respect to changes in peakVO one year after the start of the study. Peak oxygen consumption was assessed during a maximal cardiopulmonary exercise test on an electromagnetically braked cycle ergometer (Lode Corrival, Lode, Groningen, the Netherlands) upon enrolment in the study (after completion of traditional CR) and after a six- and 12-month follow-up. Oxygen consumption and CO production was measured breathby-breath by ventilatory gas exchange (Metalyzer 3b, Cortex Biophysics GmbH, Leipzig, Germany).

All patients started with 1 min of rest followed by 2 min of unloaded cycling. Subsequently, an individualised ramp protocol was performed in order to reach peakVO2 between 8 and 12 min. Identical protocols were used in all three tests. Patients were verbally encouraged to exercise until levelling off of VO2, despite an increase in workload and/or a respiratory exchange ratio > 1.10. Subsequently, an active recovery period of 4 min of exercise with minimal workload was performed. A 12-lead electrocardiogram was recorded continuously. PeakVO2 was determined as the mean oxygen consumption during the final 30 seconds of the exercise test.

Assessment of physical activity. Physical activity was assessed by the international physical activity questionnaire (IPAQ, long form).23

QoL. QoL was measured by a validated Dutch version of the Quality of Life after Myocardial Infarction questionnaire.24

Cardiovascular risk factors. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight over height squared. Waist circumference (cm) was measured end-expiratory at a level midway between the lowest rib and the iliac crest. Blood pressure was determined in seated position, with manual auscultation in both arms after a 5-min rest. Venous blood samples (12 mL) were obtained after an overnight fasting period. Total cholesterol was analysed by the biochemical laboratory (ISO certified) using standard procedures for the analysis.

Care utilisation. The utilisation of care (cardiac-related hospitalisation, outpatient clinic visits, procedures and general practitioner (GP) visits for cardiac complaints or other GP visits that resulted in interrupting the exercise programme) was collected by a (monthly) telephone call with the participants throughout the study period and the evaluation of patients’ electronic medical files. Cardiac-related hospitalisation was defined as all planned hospitalisations for cardiovascular-related reasons. Standard outpatient visits as part of regular care after CR were omitted in total outpatient clinic visits; only additional outpatient visits were recorded. Cardiacrelated procedures were considered relevant as both diagnostic and therapeutic. Consultations with patients’ GPs about cardiovascular disorders and consultations that prohibited patients from exercising were recorded as relevant care utilisation.

MACE. The occurrence of MACE (cardiovascular mortality, near-sudden cardiac death, ACS and cardiovascular emergency visits) was likewise collected by a (monthly) telephone call with the participants

throughout the study period and the evaluation of patients’ electronic medical files.

Emotional and social functioning. Emotional functioning was measured with the patient health questionnaire \left( \mathrm { P H Q - 9 } \right) ^ { 2 5 }and the hospital anxiety depression scale (HADS).26 Social functioning was tested with the multidimensional perceived social support scale (MPSS).27

## Statistical analysis

All parameters were analysed on the intention-to-treat principle, according to initial randomisation. Distribution of the data was assessed with the Shapiro– Wilk test. Continuous variables are presented by mean  standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables by count and percentage. Analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Sample size calculation suggested that for an expected difference in pea \chi \mathrm { V O } _ { 2 }of 3.0 mL kg1 min1 between the control and intervention group with an estimated SD of 4 . 8 \mathrm { m L } { \cdot } \mathrm { k g } ^ { - 1 } \mathrm { m i n } ^ { - 1 }, assuming 90% power and 5% significance level ( \alpha = 0 . 0 5 ), a total of 108 patients were required to detect a beneficial effect of telemonitoring. These numbers are based on a previous study that shows the effects of 52 weeks of CR with combined supervised and unsupervised exercise sessions.28 To account for a loss of 10% follow-up, we randomised 61 patients in each group.

Differences in baseline characteristics were tested by an independent samples t test or a Mann–Whitney U test as appropriate for continuous variables, and by Fisher’s exact test for dichotomous variables. Between-group differences in changes in \mathrm { p e a k V O } _ { 2 }from baseline to 12 months were tested with an independent samples t test. Differences in care utilisation between both groups was tested with Fisher’s exact test. In addition, linear mixed-model analysis was used to assess time-dependent changes at baseline, 6 and 12 months for primary and all secondary outcome parameters. Time was used as a fixed factor. No random factor was used. All tests were two-sided and a p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

# Results
Between September 2014 and August 2015, a total of 299 eligible patients were referred for CR. A total of 122 patients consented to participate and were randomised to CON with standard follow-up, or to TELE with telemonitoring and telecoaching guidance for six months. A flow chart of the trial is presented in Figure 1. Patients were predominantly male, younger than 65 years and were overweight (Table 1). No significant differences were found in patient

characteristics, medication use, referral reason for CR and exercise capacity between the TELE and CON group. Four patients (3.3%) dropped out during the study: three patients in TELE and one patient in CON. In both groups, one patient had musculoskeletal problems, causing an inability to perform the last exercise test. The other dropouts in the intervention group decided to stop in the first week due to limited time (n 1) and the inability to perform the last cardiopulmonary exercise test due to a second opinion for different treatment options for stable angina pectoris (n 1).

TELE patients (n 59) completed a total of 10,821 exercise sessions during the six-month intervention, resulting in 1 8 3 \pm 1 4 7sessions per patient. A total of 5781 sessions (53%) were longer than 30 min ( 9 8 \pm 6 8sessions/patient) and time spent in light-to-moderate, moderate-to-high and high-to-extreme zones was 47%, 46% and 7%, respectively. The mean number of weeks patients reached their exercise goal of 30 min of moderate-to-high intensity exercise, five days a week, was 3.8 (SD 5.9).
