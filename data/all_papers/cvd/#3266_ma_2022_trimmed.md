# 2. Methods
## 2.1. Study design

This study was a single-blinded, two-arm randomised controlled trial with a repeated-measure design to examine the effects of an SNS intervention amongst Chinese hypertensive patients. This study was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR-IOR-17014227).

## 2.2. Participants

The participants were recruited from two community health service centres in Xi'an, China, from March 2018 to June 2018. The city of Xi'an is comprised of 16 districts, and there are around 8 to 20 service centres in each district. The researcher randomly selected these two centres, which are located in the largest district of the city. Each of these two centres was serving about 50,000 Chinese residents, 7000 of whom were hypertensive patients (around 30% of the hypertensive patients had been scheduled for regular follow-ups). There were about 50 healthcare professionals in each healthcare centre, the majority of them (75%) general medical practitioners and nurses. There were also several traditional Chinese medicine practitioners and health counsellors and educators. These two centres were similar to centres in all of the districts of Xi'an in organisational structure, service provision, clinical (hypertension) treatment and care protocols, staff-patient ratio, and types of professionals available.

To identify potential participants, the staff of the centre attended a brief training session on the purpose of the study and the inclusion criteria. Those patients who attended the centres and who self-reported that they had been clinically diagnosed with essential hypertension by a physician had their blood pressure and medication regimen checked by the staff of the centre. The staff asked those with uncontrolled blood pressure and/ or who were regularly taking antihypertensive medication(s) whether they used a smartphone and were interested in participating in the study, after explaining the purpose and procedure of this study to them. Five hundred and forty-eight out of 4000 people with hypertension (i.e., around 29% of the total number of hypertensive people attending the two centres) were assessed by the staff of the centre and found to be eligible (i.e., those with uncontrolled blood pressure, who were taking antihypertensive medications, and who had a smartphone). If those patients agreed to provide their phone number to be further contacted by the researcher, the staff would then refer them to the researcher to be screened for eligibility and possible recruitment. The researcher approached those patients by phone to introduce the study, ask them about their interests, and make a face-to-face appointment. During the face-to-face interview, the purpose, procedure, and potential benefits and risks of the study, as well as the participants' rights and confidentiality were explained to

each participant. Once a patient was found to meet the selection criteria, had signed the consent form, and was enrolled in the study, the researcher reviewed that patient's medical record for verification.

Eligible participants were Chinese residents who: (a) were aged 18 years or above and had been clinically diagnosed with essential hypertension by a physician, (b) taking at least one type of prescribed anti-hypertensive medication, (c) had a smartphone on which they could access the Internet and a smartphone application, (d) were able to understand Chinese/Mandarin and give their informed consent to participate in the study, and (e) were willing to provide their contact telephone number. Excluded were hypertensive patients who were pregnant and who had a limb movement or cognitive function disorder, and were presenting with a severe complication of hypertension, such as endstage renal disease or cancer and/or had been involved in another hypertension-related self-care or illness management programme in the recent 6 months.

## 2.3. Sample size estimation

Sample size estimation refers to the total standardised mean difference in SBP (Hedges' g = 0.59) and DBP (Hedges' g = 0.46) in two similar randomised controlled trials of electronic health interventions amongst hypertensive people at 3 months after starting the intervention in the community health service centres and hospital (Moore et al., 2014; Or and Tao, 2016). These two RCTs (Moore et al., 2014; Or and Tao, 2016) were conducted in similar settings, where multidisciplinary health care and general hypertension-related education were provided to outpatients or community patients with hypertension. A total of 152 hypertensive patients (76/group) were required to detect the conservative effect size of 0.46, with a study power of 80% at a 2-sided 5% level of significance. Accounting for a drop-out rate of 16% (Margolis et al., 2013), 182 (91/group) participants were required for this study.

## 2.4. Randomisation and allocation

The target sample size was 182 patients (91 for both the intervention and control groups), and 15 to 20 subjects were enrolled per week from two community health service centres. Block randomisation at a 1:1 ratio was performed, with each block of 10 containing varying sub-blocks of four and six. Using a uniform block size likely increases the possibility of anticipating the randomisation sequence; therefore, varied blocks of unequal lengths (e.g., at least two or more different block sizes) were used to minimise selection bias (Starkings, 2007; Doig and Simpson, 2005; Schulz and Grimes, 2002).

The randomisation sequence was generated with an online randomisation program (http://www.randomization.com/) by a research assistant who was not involved in the study. A labelled card indicating the random group allocation (1, intervention group; 2, control group) was placed into sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes by the research assistant. Each participant was given an envelope on the basis of their sequence of entry into the study.

## 2.5. Usual care

The participants in the intervention and control groups received the usual care provided by the community health service centres, including medical follow-ups and health education. A medical follow-up was conducted every 3 months, and participants received one medical followup during the study period. During a medical follow-up, community healthcare professionals would examine a participant's BP level, body weight, and heart rate, and communicate face-to-face in the community health service centre with the participant about his/her health status and lifestyle. A monthly health education session was provided in a group format to promote knowledge on hypertension and healthy lifestyles amongst participants (National Health Commission of the People’s Republic of China, 2017).

## 2.6. SNS intervention

Apart from the usual care, the participants in the intervention group received a 12-week SNS intervention. This intervention involved six weekly individual education and consultation sessions and a series of smartphone-enhanced self-care activities on such topics as healthrelated decision-making, self-monitoring of BP, weight control, reducing sodium intake, healthy diets, actively engaging in physical activities, smoking cessation, reducing alcohol consumption, and emotion control (Table 1 and Appendix). Smartphone-enhanced self-care activities were conducted via a self-developed smartphone application, which was available on iOS or Android. A trained research nurse could track the health status and self-care activities of the participants and upload the educational materials to the application.

Individual education and consultation sessions were provided to the participants in the first 6 weeks, consisting of two face-to-face sessions followed by four phone sessions. They were conducted by the researcher and a trained research nurse. The sessions (30 min to 50 min per session) were aimed at helping the participants use the smartphone application and improving their self-care ability. The Middle Range Theory of Self-care of Chronic Illness was used to guide the development of the sessions. As shown in Table 1, the topics and delivery methods of the six individual education and consultation sessions were as follows: (1) face-to-face training on the use of the smartphone application, (2) face-to-face self-measurement of BP levels, (3) advice on healthy diets by phone calls, (4) advice on active engagement in physical activity by phone calls, (5) emotion and stress management by phone calls, and (6) advice on healthy lifestyles by phone calls. In these six sessions, based on the health information uploaded in the smartphone application, the interveners communicated with the participants about their self-care behaviours (self-care maintenance) to help them recognise healthy and unhealthy behaviour and the relationship between behaviour and changes in health status (self-care monitoring). They then provided suggestions and recommendations (self-care management). The interveners helped the participants to make health-related decisions by completing individual self-care planning using the smartphone application.

Smartphone application-enhanced self-care activities. After completing the installation of the smartphone application and user training in the first education and consultation session, the participants were asked to use the password-protected smartphone application to facilitate their self-care activities for 12 weeks. Four key components of the smartphone application were identified to facilitate self-care activities. The first key component was ‘health education’, whose content was developed following international and national guidelines on hypertension management (Writing Group of 2018 Chinese Guideline for the Management of Hypertension, 2019; Williams et al., 2018; Whelton et al., 2018). Nine educational articles covering the health topics of hypertension, antihypertensive medication, and self-care behaviours were provided to participants to meet their knowledge acquisition needs.

The second key component was ‘individual self-care planning’, which allowed participants to set their target BP level and body weight by inputting detailed values and to make a diet and physical activity plan by selecting preferred options. The options were designed on the basis of the Chinese hypertension guideline, culture, and social context (Writing Group of 2018 Chinese Guideline for the Management of Hypertension, 2019; Chinese Nutrition Society, 2016). The third key component was ‘daily record’, which allowed participants to input and record their BP level, body weight, and self-care behaviours (e.g., medication adherence, diet, physical activity, stress, sleep quality, and defecation condition). Participants were required to record their BP level at least three times a week, and other items (body weight and BMI, medication adherence, diet, physical activity, and other life content) at least once a week. Otherwise, the interveners would send a reminder message to them via the short messaging service. The fourth key component was ‘automated weekly health report’. The smartphone application automatically generated a health

report weekly based on the individual's daily records. The health report displayed changes in health status (e.g., BP and body weight) using line charts, frequency of self-care behaviours (e.g., medication adherence, diet and physical activity) using doughnut or bar charts, and the mean scores of individuals on stress, sleep quality, and frequency of defecation.

The following strategies were used to maintain the fidelity of the intervention. The intervention was delivered by two interveners, who were the researcher (MY) and a trained research nurse with a master's degree in nursing and 5 years of clinical experience in hypertension management. Both interveners had access to the tracking system to track the participants' performance of self-care behaviours. The researcher conducted a one-day training session for the registered nurse to ensure that the intervention was delivered accurately, confidently, and skilfully. Included in the training was an explanation of the study and the intervention, knowledge on hypertension and self-care, and case scenarios. Weekly face-to-face or phone call communications between the two interveners were conducted to ensure the consistency of the intervention. Furthermore, a standardised checklist was used to randomly check the implementation of the intervention.

Using a standardised validation form, the content of the educational materials was examined by a panel of seven experts, including one academic expert on self-care of hypertension, two clinical experts (senior registered nurses with more than 15 years of clinical experience in CVDs), and four physicians (with more than 10 years of clinical experience in CVDs). The educational materials were relevant, adequate, and accurate for this intervention. In addition, three laymen with a low level of education (less than 9 years of formal education) were invited to read the educational materials and use the smartphone application. The educational materials were found to be easy to read and understand, and the smartphone application was user friendly. A pilot study was conducted amongst 30 hypertensive patients (n = 15 per group) to test the feasibility of the intervention in terms of implementation, adherence, and data collection.

# 2.7. Data collection and outcomes
Two trained outcome assessors, who were masked to the group allocations, completed the collecting of data face-to-face at baseline (T0), on the 6th week after starting the intervention (T1), and on the 12th week after starting the intervention (T2) in the community health service centres. The primary outcome was BP control, including SBP, DBP, and the proportion of participants with a BP level of below 140/90 mmHg. BP was measured three times at baseline and at each follow-up appointment. An automated BP measurement device (Omron U30) was used and the measurement was made while the individual was in a seated position and after having rested for at least 5 minutes. The same arm and cuff size was used each time (Writing Group of 2018 Chinese Guideline for the Management of Hypertension, 2019). The mean of the three measurements was used in the primary outcome (Writing Group of 2018 Chinese Guideline for the Management of Hypertension, 2019).

Secondary outcomes were anthropometric parameters, including body weight, waist circumference, BMI, and the proportion of participants with normal BMI (18.5 ≤ BMI < 24.0 kg/m2 ); and self-care, including behaviour, motivation, and self-efficacy. Body weight was measured using a digital scale (Omron HN-289). Waist circumference was measured using a non-elastic tape (cm) at the midpoint between the lower margin of the last palpable rib and the top of the iliac crest (hip bone) at the end of normal expiration. Self-care was measured using the self-reported Chinese version of the Hypertension Self-Care Profile (Ma et al., 2020). The Chinese version of the Hypertension Self-care Profile is a reliable and valid instrument for evaluating hypertension self-care in Chinese patients. It includes three scales, namely, a behaviour scale (Cronbach's alpha = 0.86), a motivation scale (Cronbach's alpha = 0.94), and a self-efficacy scale (Cronbach's alpha = 0.93). Each scale has 20 items, and is rated using a four-point Likert scale. A high score indicates good behaviour, motivation, and self-efficacy in hypertension self-care.

A 2-hour training session for two outcome assessors was conducted by the researcher to decrease the risk of bias and ensure the quality of the collected data. Before the commencement of the study, the interrater reliability of the two assessors was examined using six hypertensive patients, and they were found to be satisfactory in all measurements. The weighted kappa statistics of the behaviour scale, motivation scale, and self-efficacy scale of the Chinese version of the Hypertension Self-Care Profile ranged from 0.72 to 1.00, 0.73 to 1.00, and 0.73 to 1.00, accordingly. The two outcome assessors were registered nurses with an undergraduate nursing degree and experience in conducting quantitative and anthropometric data measurements.

## 2.8. Ethical considerations

The design and procedure of this controlled trial followed the Declaration of Helsinki (General Assembly of the World Medical Association, 2014). Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Joint Chinese University of Hong Kong—New Territories East Cluster Clinical Research Ethics Committee (CREC Ref. 2017.391). Written informed consent to participate in this study was obtained from each participant before the baseline measurement was conducted and group assignments made. Participants had the right to withdraw from the study at any time, with no impact on the treatment or services that they receive. The participants' right to privacy was ensured by the maintenance of strict confidentiality of data. All non-anonymous personal information and study data were kept in a password-protected USB flash disk and safely stored in a locked cabinet.

## 2.9. Statistical analysis

The intention-to-treat principle was adopted in the data analysis to preserve the integrity of the randomisation process. The generalised estimating equation (GEE) model with an autoregressive correlation structure was used to assess differential changes in the outcome variables between the two groups from baseline to 12 weeks (interaction effect of group*time). Six variables with a p-value of less than 0.25 were identified as potential covariates in adjusted models of the GEE analysis. This was done to minimise the discrepancies caused by noncomparable parameters, ensure the statistical power of the effects of the intervention, and decrease bias (Bursac et al., 2008; Tabachnick and Fidell, 2013). The six variables were: age (p = 0.18), monthly income ( p = 0 . 0 7 ), status of alcohol consumption (p = 0.18), family history of hypertension ( p = 0 . 1 3 ), classification of anti-hypertensive drugs ( p = 0 . 1 6 ), and hypertension duration ( p = 0 . 1 8 , \mathrm { T a b l e } 2 ). The effect sizes of the continuous outcome variables were estimated using Cohen's d statistic on the basis of between-group mean differences (Cohen, 1988). A GEE analysis was performed to calculate the odds ratio [OR] and indicate the effect of the intervention on categorical variables, which included the proportion of participants with BP < 140/90 mmHg and those with normal BMI (18.5 ≤ BMI < 24.0 kg/m2 ) (Ellis, 2010). The results of the GEE model were represented by three major indicators and their regression estimates (β): (1) group – indicating the differences between the intervention and control group at baseline; (2) time – indicating the effect of time on the control group, with T0 as reference; and (3) group*time – indicating the interaction effect of group and time, which implied the additional effect of the intervention on the intervention group at the corresponding point in time compared with the control group. When the overall treatment effect of the continuous outcomes was significant in the GEE analysis, pairwise contrast tests using the estimated marginal means were performed to determine whether there were any significant differences in the mean scores of each continuous outcome between groups at T1 and T2 (Tabachnick and Fidell, 2013). All statistical analyses were two sided and performed using IBM SPSS 25.0 software. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

# 3. Results
In the two community health service centres a total of 548 patients with essential hypertension were screened for eligibility to take part in the study. Of these, 210 were found to be eligible. They provided their informed consent to participate in the study and were randomly assigned to either the intervention (n = 105) group or control group ( \mathsf { n } = 1 0 5 )). Of the 210 participants, 191 (91%) completed all three instances of data collection. The drop-out rate of the intervention group was 10% (n = 11) and that of the control group was 8% ( \mathtt { n } = 8 ). All 210 participants were included in the GEE analysis (Fig. 1). Of the 105 participants in the intervention group, 98 (93.3%) attended all six individual education and consultation sessions.

As shown in Table 2, the mean age of the participants was 60.57 years (SD = 9.99), ranging from 26 to 73. More than half were female (52.9%). Most had received a senior high school education (12 years of formal education) or below (87.2%). Eighty-seven per cent of the participants were married; 84.3% were being cared for by their spouse or children, and 81.9% had medical insurance. Less than half were employed (47.6%) and had a monthly income of between RMB 1000 and 3000 (US$148–445; 47.6%). More than 80% of the participants did not smoke or drink alcohol. The average duration of hypertension was 9.72 ± 8.65 years. Half (51%) had chronic comorbidities, with the common types of comorbidities being heart disease (24.8%), diabetes (15.2%), and arthritis (9.0%). The participants in the two groups were comparable at baseline (Table 2).

No adverse events or intervention-related safety issues were recorded during the study. Each variable had 630 observations, and 26 of them (4%) were missing. The few missing data points (5% or less) were regarded as less serious problems, which had no significant effect on the results (Tabachnick and Fidell, 2013). As shown in Table 3, the statistically significant effects of the Group*Time interaction between the two groups were found in SBP (T1: β = −7.29, T2: β = −11.07, p < 0.0001), DBP (T1: β = −4.80, T2: β = −7.50, p < 0.0001), body weight (T1: β = −1.01, T2: β = −1.71, p < 0.0001), BMI (T1: β = −0.35, T2: β = −0.59, p < 0.0001), the proportion of participants with a normal BMI (T1: OR = 1.18; T2: OR = 1.29, p < 0·05), waist circumference (T1: β = −2.63, T2: β = −3.19, p < 0.0001), self-care behaviour (T1: β = 4.51, T2: β = 7.77, p < 0.0001), self-care motivation (T1: β = 5.16, T2: β = 6.61, p < 0.0001), and self-care self-efficacy (T1: β = 4.76, T2: β = 6.74, p < 0.0001) at 6 weeks (T1) and 12 weeks (T2) after starting the intervention. The proportion of participants with BP < 140/90 mmHg showed statistically significant results at T2 (OR = 2.69, p = 0.003).

Pairwise contrast tests indicated that the two groups differed significantly in changes in BP (Table 4), with a greater reduction in the intervention group than in the control group at T1 (SBP: MD = −7.97, DBP: MD = −4.15, p < 0·0001) and T2 (SBP: MD = −11.74, DBP: MD = −6.85, p < 0·0001). Participants in the intervention group had a significantly lower BMI and waist circumference than those in the control group. The between-group mean difference in BMI was 0.92 kg/m2 (p = 0.023) at T2. The waist circumference was significantly lower in the intervention group than in the control group, with a between-group difference of 3.30 cm (p = 0.04) at T1 and 3.86 cm (p = 0.014) at T2. The self-care (behaviour, motivation and self-efficacy) of the participants in the intervention group improved significantly compared with that of the control group at T1 (behaviour: MD = 3.12, p = 0.017; motivation: MD = 4.41, p < 0.001; self-efficacy: MD = 5.15, p < 0.001) and T2 (behaviour: MD = 6.38, p < 0.001; motivation: MD = 5.85, p < 0.001; self-efficacy: MD = 7.13, p < 0.001).
