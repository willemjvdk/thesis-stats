## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# Methods
## Study design

The study was designed as a single-centre, prospective, parallel-group, randomised (2:1), controlled trial of

home-based telemonitored NW training (training group (TG)) vs usual care (control group (CG)) in HF patients.

## Participants

For this study, we recruited consecutive patients hospitalised between December 2009–June 2012 in the Department of Cardiac Rehabilitation, Institute of Cardiology, Warsaw, Poland. We included patients: of either sex with any aetiology of left ventricular systolic HF (as defined in the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines)1 diagnosed at least for three months; with a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF)-40% on echocardiography; classified as New York Heart Association (NYHA) class II or III; who were clinically stable and receiving an optimal and stable medication regimen for at least four weeks before enrolment; who had no contraindications to exercise training, and who were able to undergo home-based cardiac telerehabilitation. The exclusion criteria were: unstable angina; a history of an acute coronary syndrome within the last month, coronary artery bypass grafting within the last two months, or initiation of CRT-P or CRT-D within the last six months, or implantation of a pacemaker and/or ICD within the last six weeks; symptomatic and/or exerciseinduced cardiac arrhythmia or conduction disturbance; valvular or congenital heart disease requiring surgical treatment; hypertrophic cardiomyopathy; severe pulmonary hypertension or other severe pulmonary disease; uncontrolled hypertension; anaemia (haemoglobin - 10.0 g/dl); acute and/or decompensated noncardiac disease; physical disability related to severe musculoskeletal or neurological problems; acute or chronic inflammatory disease; severe psychiatric disorder; and patient refusal to participate. The study was approved by the local Ethics Committee. Each patient gave written informed consent.

## Intervention

All patients underwent the following assessments at entry and after completing the eight-week programme: clinical examination, two-dimensional echocardiography, six-minute walking test (6-MWT), cardiopulmonary exercise treadmill test (CPET), and quality of life (QoL).

Echocardiography. Two-dimensional echocardiography was performed using standard parasternal, apical, and subcostal views (VIVID 4 GE Medical System with a 2.5 MHz transducer). LVEF was calculated from conventional apical two-chamber and four-chamber images using the biplane Simpson technique.

6-MWT. The 6-MWT was conducted using a standardised protocol between 11:00 and 14:00 after taking usual medication.11 Patients were required to perform a six-minute shuttle walk test with markers placed at 25 m.

Cardiopulmonary exercise treadmill test. The symptom-limited CPET according to a ramp protocol12 and ESC guidelines13 was performed using a Schiller treadmill (Carrollton, USA) which was connected to a computerised breath-by-breath spiroergometry system (ZAN 600, ZAN Messgera¨ te GmbH, Germany). Oxygen consumption (VO2) was measured continuously using breath-by-breath analysis and used as an index of exercise capacity. VO peak was defined as the highest oxygen uptake level achieved during the final 30 s of CPET.

Assessment of QoL. The Medical Outcome Survey Short Form 36 (SF-36) questionnaire was used to assess QoL. In the Polish version of the SF-36, lower scores are indicative of better QoL.14

Exercise training. Exercise training was planned in line with the published guidelines for HF patients.15 The chosen workload reflected individual effort tolerance with regard to: perceived exertion according to the Borg scale16 and the training heart rate (HR) range established individually for each patient. The training HR was calculated using the method known as HR reserve. This method uses a % of the difference between the maximum HR and the resting HR rate, and adds this value to the resting HR. The target training HR was 40– 70% of the HR reserve. Following the baseline evaluation, patients underwent some (3–6) monitored educational exercise training sessions. Subsequently, patients underwent an eight-week home-based telerehabilitation programme. The training session consisted of three parts: a warm-up lasting 5–10 min (breathing and light resistance exercises, calisthenics), a 15–45 min NW training, and a five-minute cool-down. The initial duration of the aerobic NW training depended on the functional capacity in baseline CPET. If baseline peak VO2 was <14 ml/kg/min, we started at 10 min/NW training/day, if baseline peak VO2 was 14–20 ml/kg/min, we started at 15 min/NW/day; and if baseline peak VO2 was >20 ml/ kg/min, we started at 20 min/NW/day. Later on we tended to gradually prolong the time allotted to the training session so that the final period of time would reach 45–60 min. In order to achieve this optimal prolonged training duration usually two weeks needed to elapse. Patients trained five times a week. The exercise training effectiveness was assessed by changes – delta (-) – in the duration of the workload, VO2peak in CPET, 6- MWT distance resulting from the comparison of these

parameters from the beginning and the end of the programme. The methodology of telerehabilitation was described previously.17,18 In brief, all patients in the TG received a special remote device for telemonitored and telesupervised exercise training – telerehabilitation set (Pro Plus Company, Poland), which consists of: EHO mini device and blood pressure measuring and weighing machine. The EHO mini device enabled the electrocardiogram (ECG) data from three pre-cordial leads to be recorded and transmitted via a mobile phone to the monitoring centre. Before the beginning of a training session, patients used the mobile phone to answer a series of questions regarding their present condition, including fatigue and medication taken. After that, patients transmitted their resting ECG, blood pressure and weight data to the monitoring centre. If no contraindications to the training were identified, patients were given permission to start the training session. The device had training sessions preprogrammed individually for each patient (defined exercise duration, breaks, and timing of ECG recording). The previously planned training sessions were executed thanks to the sound and light indications produced by the device. The timing of automatic ECG recordings corresponded to peak exercise. If the training session was completed uneventfully, the patient transmitted the ECG recording via mobile phone to the monitoring centre immediately after the end of every training session. Patients could also transmit an ECG recording at any other moment, e.g. if they experienced worrying symptoms. The data were analysed at the monitoring centre, and the safety, efficacy and accuracy of a particular patient rehabilitation programme were assessed. Telephone contact was also used for psychological support. Additionally, to ensure the safety of the exercise training conducted in patients with an ICD and CRT-D, maximal training HR was set at 20 bpm, lower than the defibrillator discharge threshold. In patients with a pacemaker, CRT-P, CRT-D, the rate–response function was switched on, enabling HR adjustment to the physical effort, which facilitated reaching the desired training HR. Patients were advised to be accompanied during the training (usually by a family member), who had been provided training in first aid procedures by the monitoring centre, and who, in case of emergency, would call for a health professional and would be able to provide first aid until medical help arrived.

Usual care. Patients in the CG receiving ‘usual care’ were not provided with a formal exercise training prescription and did not perform supervised rehabilitation. All patients, regardless of the treatment group, received recommendations for suitable lifestyle changes and self-management according to ESC guidelines.1

Assessment of the adherence to home-based telemonitored NW training. Adherence during the NW training was assessed basing on the daily telephone contact with the monitoring centre, which was required to obtain the necessary permission for the training. Adherence was defined as the percentage of patients who carried out the prescribed exercise training. According to the recommendations, in terms of their adherence, the patients were divided into three groups: the first category were adherent patients, i.e. patients who adhered both to the number of training sessions prescribed and to the duration of the prescribed cycle by at least 80%; the second group consisted of non-adherent patients, who adhered <20% to the prescribed number of training sessions and their duration. The third group corresponded to the partially adherent patients who carried out the prescribed exercise, yet tended to omit some of them or did not carry them out for the prescribed duration (i.e. who adhered 20% and <80%).6

Assessment of the acceptance of home-based telemonitored NW training. At the end of the study, patients filled out a 12-item questionnaire in order to assess their acceptance and satisfaction of home-based telemonitored NW training. The questions are presented in Table 2.

# Outcomes
The primary end point was functional capacity assessed by VO2peak. Secondary end points included: extended assessment of the effectiveness of rehabilitation (workload duration in CPET, 6-MWT distance and QoL), safety and adherence to NW training. In addition, acceptance of telemonitored NW training was evaluated. Measurements were obtained at baseline and at the end of an eight-week intervention period.

## Sample size

Estimation of sample size. According to the mean values and standard deviations of VO2peak, and under the following assumptions: significance level ¼ 0.05, power ¼ 0.80 and differences of this parameters over eight weeks ¼ 15%, the sample size ¼ 32 is satisfied.

## Random allocation to treatment and blinding

Following the baseline measurements, participants were randomly assigned (in a 2:1 ratio), according to simple randomisation procedures (a random number table), to the TG or CG. The study was not blinded

(blinding for patients and investigators was not possible due to the nature of the exercise training intervention). Only CPETs were conducted by technical staff blind to treatment allocation.

## Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.2; Cary, North Carolina, USA). The normality of distributions was verified using the Shapiro–Wilk test. To compare normally distributed continuous variables, the paired and unpaired t-tests were used. To compare changes of variables in time between training and control groups the general linear model with repeated measures adjusted for age, LVEF, aetiology of HF and the duration of effort was used. The signed-rank test (for changes over time) and the Wilcoxon test (to compare groups) were used for continuous variables that were not distributed normally. The chi-squared or Fisher exact tests were used for categorical variables. Values of p < 0.05 were considered significant.

# Results
Of the 227 patients screened for the purpose of this study, 44% were excluded because of fulfilled exclusion criteria and 7% refused to participate. Of the 111 patients randomised, 77 were assigned to the TG and 34 to the CG. All patients in the TG completed an eight-week rehabilitation programme, yet two patients did not undergo the final clinical examinations (one of them was awaiting an elective orthotopic heart transplantation, the other suffered from an exacerbated comorbidity). None of the patients dropped out of the programme due to complications caused by the NW training or technical difficulties with operating the telerehabilitation set. In the CG, 33 out of 34 patients completed the eight-week observation. One patient dropped out due to logistics-related problems. Another patient, however available for final examination, did not undergo it because of injuries of the lower extremity. Eventually, the remaining 75 patients in TG and 32 patients in CG were included in the analysis (Figure 1). The clinical characteristics of these patients are described in Table 1. The groups were comparable in terms of demographic data, baseline clinical parameters and pharmacotherapy except for the following parameters: age, LVEF, aetiology of heart failure, number of patients with ICD and workload duration in CPET. All TG patients achieved the individually established training HR and/or moderate rating of perceived exertion according to the Borg scale; mean one exercise session duration was 45  5 min.

## Safety of home-based telemonitored NW training

Neither death nor other major events including the need for hospitalisation due to HF exacerbation occurred as a result of participating in the homebased telemonitored NW training. We did not observe worrying signs or symptoms and episodes of exerciseinduced malignant arrhythmia or myocardial ischaemia. No patient developed musculoskeletal injuries related to the NW training. No need to discontinue or urgently stop the rehabilitation cycle was observed in any patient. No adverse events caused by the upper

extremity movements during the NW training in CIEDs either locally, close to the can, or resulting from the electrode dysfunction or damage were observed. No intervention from CIEDs during the remotely supervised telemonitored NW training has been reported. One intervention during an unsupervised patient activity in TG and two interventions in CG took place. After eight weeks of NW, CIEDs were controlled in all patients and found to function correctly. There were few minor events related to telerehabilitation, though 5.3% patients reported minor skin reactions due to the electrodes.

## Adherence to home-based telemonitored NW training

All patients completed the proposed model of cardiac rehabilitation. The adherence to home-based telemonitored NW training was very high. There were no nonadherent patients. Altogether 94.7% of the patients were adherent and only 5.3% patients were partially adherent.

## Acceptance of home-based telemonitored NW training

All TG patients filled in the patients’ acceptance and satisfaction questionnaire. Each patient controlled the telemonitoring device unaided and almost 99% of the patient group reported that the device was ‘very easy’ or ‘easy’ to use. About 90% of the patients had no problems coordinating the exercise with the instructions from the device. A great number of patients undergoing the NW training reported that everyday contact with the monitoring centre stimulated them to undertake exercise. Moreover, they felt safer during telemonitored training than when they did exercises at home without being supervised remotely. Most of the patients reported that their everyday physical and mental activities improved. The details of the results are shown in Table 2.
