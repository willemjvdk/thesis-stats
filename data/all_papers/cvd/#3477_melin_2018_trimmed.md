# Methods
## Study Design

Patient Centered Management of Heart Failure, as previously reported,21 was a prospective, multicenter randomized controlled trial involving 3 university hospitals in Stockholm, Sweden. Patients hospitalized for HF were enrolled in the study during their stay in the hospital for an acute HF episode or on a follow-up visit at the hospital within 4 weeks after discharge. The patients were enrolled either by a specialist or by a registered nurse. The study was not blinded, and either the patient received a tablet computer or not; however, all data processing was performed by investigators without knowledge if the patient was in the intervention group (IG) or in the control group (CG). All patients continued to be in the study even in the case of rehospitalization. The patients were randomized to receive standard care or standard care plus the OPTILOGG system, and after randomization, the patients were all referred to primary care.

## Intervention

OPTILOGG consists of a tablet computer locked to a custom software and is wirelessly connected to a weighing scale. The patient is instructed to step on the weighing scale every morning. The weight is wirelessly transferred to the tablet and based on 2 predefined levels unique to each patient and the weight trend; the current day’s dose of diuretics was displayed on-screen. The weight level for each patient is defined by the specialist and registered nurse who enrolled the patient. The patient is also given a short tip on how to improve life with HF. The education module includes information on the importance of taking the prescribed medications, lifestyle changes, recipes for healthy food, facts about HF, and symptoms. Every 5 days, the patients input their symptoms (fatigue, shortness of breath, and swelling/edema). The patients are encouraged to use the system daily. For interested patients, there is an information library where they can search for information,

and all weights, doses, and symptoms are available to the patient on a ‘‘History’’ tab.

The patient could be enrolled in hospital or at a clinic visit. Upon leaving the hospital or the outpatient clinic, the patient was visited by an engineer, who installed the system in the patient’s home and showed the patient how to use it. The theory was that patients could be coached to a better self-care behavior with the aid of an easy tablet computer. When the system detected a deterioration in health status, an onscreen alert was presented, urging the patient to contact the hospital and the HF specialist nurse at the recruiting clinic during business hours.

## Patient Recruitment and Follow-up

Patients older than 18 years hospitalized for HF were enrolled in the study during their stay in the hospital or follow-up visit at the hospital within 4 weeks after discharge. Exclusion criteria were limited to cognitive impairment if deemed severe enough to preclude them from understanding fundamental instructions, being referred to a nurse-led HF clinic, life expectancy of less than 6 months, or current participation in another study that might affect their treatment. The patients were followed for 6 months after randomization. All patients signed a written informed consent form. The study was approved by the regional ethical review board in Stockholm, Sweden (Dnr: 2012/2176-31/1).

## Sample

Data analysis was performed per intention to treat with the modification that those patients in the IG who actively withdrew their consent immediately (before leaving the hospital) and without ever being exposed to the intervention were not included. However, any patients withdrawing their consent after the first exposure were included in all analyses, so none of these patients was excluded, and they were analyzed according to the randomization scheme for the purposes of intention-to-treat analysis. A retrospective, perprotocol analysis based on the level of exposure to the intervention was also performed. Eighty-two patients were randomized, and 72 were thus included in the final study.

## Data Collection

As previously described,21 data were collected from the 9-item European Heart Failure Self-Care Behavior Scale,22 the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire23,24 with 6 domains and 2 summary scores, the Swedish version of the Health Survey25 with 8 domains summarized in a physical score and a mental score, and the Dutch Heart Failure Knowledge Scale, which consists of 15 multiple-choice items (0Y15).26 Data

were collected at baseline and after 6 months. We also made a compilation of HF-related hospital days during the same period using information collected from the medical records.

## Statistical Analysis

The primary end point was self-care behavior, and no previous data were available on how a home-based system affects self-care. Because daily weighing is an integral part of the intervention, we chose study weighing frequency as reported in the literature and what effect size would be reasonable given a certain intervention. StrPmberg et al27 have reported the proportions of weighing frequency between the CG and the IG around 30% versus 70% for a similar intervention. With an estimated compliance of 30% in the CG and an estimated compliance of 80% in the IG, 30 + 30 patients would be required to reject the null hypothesis with 80% power and a type I error probability of 5%. Because of potential attrition, the target for enrollment was set at 40 + 40 patients. Power calculations were performed by ‘‘KnowIt Decision’’ consulting agency on December 2012, as previously described.21

The baseline characteristics were analyzed using a \chi ^ { 2 }test and a t test for independent samples. The total number of hospital days per patient and 6 months were analyzed using generalized Poisson log-linear regression, and because we were interested in estimating the net/marginal incidence rate ratio of inpatient hospitalization, absorbing competing events (eg, death) was handled by censoring. The other outcome variables (9-item European Heart Failure Self-Care Behavior Scale, Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire, Short Form (36) Health Survey, and Dutch Heart Failure Knowledge Scale) were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. P values of less than .05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical calculations were performed in R, version 3.0.1.

# Results
## Baseline Characteristics

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are reported in Table 1.21 Of 82 randomized patients, results are based on 72 patients (IG, n = 32; CG, n = 40). Ten patients were excluded from the analyses because of their withdrawal of consent immediately after randomization and thus before the system was installed. These patients did not significantly differ from other patients in any registered aspect. No significant differences were shown between the IG and the CG regarding baseline characteristics, except for atrial fibrillation (AF), which was more common in the CG (73% vs 47%, P G .05).

## Deaths and Missing Data

Five patients in the CG and 3 patients in the IG died. Three patients withdrew their consent in the CG before the 6-month follow-up was to take place. Two patients in the IG withdrew their consent and had their OPTILOGG system uninstalled.

Nine patients in the CG (23%) did not complete the questionnaires for the 6-month follow-up. The corresponding number in the IG was 5 patients (16%). This means that, in total, 58 of the 59 available (alive and had not withdrawn consent) patients’ data were collected (98%) or 81% of all 72 patients in the study. According to the US Food and Drug Administration recommendations, all data collected up to the point of the withdrawal of consent should be used in the analyses.

## Effect on Self-care

At baseline, there were no differences between the 2 groups (median IG, 25.5 [interquartile range {IQR}, 14.5Y30.2], versus median CG, 23.0 [IQR, 14.8Y31.3]; NS). After 6 months, the IG displayed better self-care behaviors, as indicated by a lower 9-item European Heart Failure Self-Care Behavior Scale score (median IG, 16.5 [IQR, 12Y22], versus median CG, 23.5 [IQR, 18.8Y30.0]; P G .05) (Table 2).

We also studied the effect on ‘‘consulting behavior.’’ Consulting behavior shows the patients’ ability to contact the healthcare system in case of worsening symptoms and is a validated subscale.28 After 6 months, the IG displayed better consulting behaviors, as indicated by a lower 9-item European Heart Failure Self-Care Behavior Scale score (median IG, 10.5 [IQR, 6Y16], vs median CG, 12.5 [IQR, 6.8Y17]; NS) compared with 6 months (median IG, 6.5 [IQR, 5Y10.3], vs median CG, 11 [IQR, 8Y16]; P G .05).

## Effect on HRQoL and QoL

The disease-specific health related quality of life was measured by Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire. At baseline, there were no differences between the groups in clinical mean summary score (median IG, 44.8 [IQR, 22.7Y62.1], versus median CG, 43.0 [IQR, 28.1Y53.0]; NS). After 6 months, the IG had a significantly higher score (median IG, 72.7 [IQR, 50.8Y87.9], versus median CG, 51.8 [IQR, 40.9Y62.8]; P G .05) (Table 2). Quality of life as measured by the Short Form (36) Health Survey was not different between the 2 groups regarding mental and physical scores at baseline and after 6 months (Table 2).

## Effect on Disease-specific Knowledge

Mean knowledge score on baseline was comparable for both groups with mean values of 11.6 T 1.8 for IG versus 12.2 T 2.1 for CG (NS). After 6 months, the IG showed a significantly improved level of knowledge of HF (IG, 12.8 T 1.7, versus CG, 12.1 T 1.9), with an 11% increase in IG and a 1% decrease in CG (P G .05) (Table 2). BecausetheeducationmoduleoftheOPTILOGG system is an integral part of the intervention and the systems were collected, we could also analyze what information the patients read, which is not possible with handouts or other forms of education. One hundred

percent of the patients actively chose to seek out more information in addition to the daily tip that was always provided in the tablet. On average, the patients chose to read more once every 2 weeks, with large interpatient variability. The analysis showed that patients tended to actively choose to read about topics that were perceived as positive (such as ‘‘food and drinks,’’ with 31% of all selections), whereas disease-oriented information (such as ‘‘symptoms,’’ with 17% of selections) were less likely to be studied.

## Effect on Heart Failure Hospitalization

There was a reduction in hospital days in IG (relative risk, 0.72; 95% confidence interval, 0.61Y0.84; P G .05), a difference corresponding to a reduction of 2.7 days per patient over 6 months. The number of hospital days due to HF was logged during the 6 months for all patients and analyzed as ‘‘number of days in the hospital per patient over 6 months.’’ A total of 221 HF hospital days were logged for the IG (n = 32), and 385 HF hospital days were logged for the CG (n = 40). This means that, for the IG, there were 6.9 HF hospital days per patient and 6 months and, for the CG, there were 9.6 HF hospital days per patient and 6 months (Figure 1).

There was a total of 28 hospitalizations in the IG and 38 hospitalizations in the CG, and 44% of the patients (n = 14) were readmitted at least once in the IG compared with 40% of the patients in the CG (n = 16) within 6 months. These numbers were not significantly different. There was, on average, 1.0 admission per patient in the CG and 0.9 admissions per patient in IG (NS).

## Effect on Adherence

Adherence to use the system was high, with a median of 85% (IQR, 71%Y93%) (Figure 2). The system registered all interactions with the system, and the adherence was defined as the number of days the patient interacted with the system in any way divided by the number of days the patient has been equipped with the system. Thus, each patient will yield 1 adherence frequency between 0% and 100 %.
