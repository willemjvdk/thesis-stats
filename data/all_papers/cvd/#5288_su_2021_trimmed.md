Background: The uptake of and adherence to cardiac rehabilitation remain suboptimal despite its apparent health benefits in modifying risk factors and slowing disease progression. eHealth refers to the use of information and communication technologies for health-related purposes. It is a promising approach for improving participation in cardiac rehabilitation by enabling instant contact, hypermedia information delivery, technology-monitored functionalities and individualised progress monitoring.

Aims: To evaluate the effects of a nurse-led eHealth cardiac rehabilitation (NeCR) system on health behaviours, cardiac self-efficacy, anxiety and depression, health-related quality of life, risk parameters and unplanned use of care services for people with coronary heart disease.

Design: A single-blinded randomised controlled trial design was used.

# 3. Methods
## 3.1. Study design

The study was a single-blinded, randomised controlled trial (registration number: ChiCTR1800020411), conducted at four cardiology units in Tongji Hospital, Wuhan, China, from March to October 2019. Eligible participants were randomly allocated to receive NeCR intervention or usual care, with block sizes of four, six and eight. Random numbers and group allocation codes were sealed in opaque envelopes. The outcome variables were measured at baseline and 6 and 12 weeks post-intervention. Single-blinding was maintained as research assistants performing data collection did not have access to information about group allocations.

## 3.2. Participants

In the study setting, patients were treated with medication and percutaneous coronary intervention and discharged with regular clinical follow-up. No cardiac rehabilitation or structured service was offered for disease maintenance. Participants were recruited through study posters, word of mouth by the researchers, and hospital staff referrals. Researchers screened medical records and

conducted an interview to determine the eligibility of individuals who expressed interest. Eligible patients were adults who had been hospitalised for an initial diagnosis of CHD based on angiography or the exacerbation of CHD in previously diagnosed cases, were discharged, could read and speak Chinese, had an education background at the primary level or above, were able to use a computer and/or smartphone to access the Internet and had no prescribed physical activity restrictions. Patients with a life-limiting condition, acute psychotic disease, absolute or relative contraindications to exercise testing and training, a high risk of exercise prescription based on the American Association of Cardiovascular and Pulmonary Rehabilitation guidelines or visual, fine motor, auditory or ambulatory disorders were excluded.

## 3.3. Sample size estimation

A meta-analysis on the effects of telemedicine-based cardiac rehabilitation on health outcomes reported effect sizes on promoting a healthy lifestyle of 0.42 to 1.29 (Rawstorn et al., 2016). A medium effect size of 0.5 (with 80% power and 0.05 significance) was adopted in this study. The estimated sample size was 146, which accounted for an expected attrition rate of 12%, based on similar studies with post-test time points of 6–12 weeks (Widmer et al., 2017; Zutz et al., 2007).

## 3.4. Study intervention: nurse-led eHealth cardiac rehabilitation

## 3.4.1. Development of the NeCR programme

As the primary aim of the NeCR programme was to enhance behavioural risk factor modification for CHD patients, its development was guided by social cognitive theory, which postulates the importance of optimising cognitive (i.e., knowledge), behavioural (e.g., self-efficacy, skill mastery) and environmental factors (e.g., peer support, resource-related barriers to action) in enabling behavioural changes (Bandura, 2004). Therefore, empowerment strategies through nurses and peer patients were deliberately adopted when designing the NeCR programme to optimise these three factors (Zimmerman, 1995). More specifically, the NeCR programme incoproated a goal-setting cycle, through which the nurse first increased the patients’ awareness of their rehabilitation needs and then supported them to set consensus-based goals and actions to modify behavioural risk factors (i.e., physical inactivity; unhealthy lifestyle including unhealthy diet, stress and poor psychosocial adaptation; and smoking). Each goal had three levels of upward adjustment to enable patients to realise small successes and develop self-efficacy (cognitive and behavioural factors). Online platforms, namely the website and social media platform, were used to empower patients to proceed in the goal-attainment process in the following four ways: i) by enriching cognitive factors by providing resourceful information related to CHD self-care (cognitive factor); ii) by enhancing skill mastery for risk factor modification through video-based education (behavioural factor), iii) by supporting the monitoring of goal attainment by automatically transforming behaviour-tracking data as a percentage of goal attainment and adjusting the system to show upward goal progress accordingly (behavioural factor) and iv) by optimising partnerships with professionals and peer patients in motivating goal attainment and brainstorming strategies to overcome barriers on the social media platform (environmental factor). Details of the NeCR have been published elsewhere (Su and Yu, 2019), and a brief outline of the overall process is described below. Fig. 1 shows a graphical presentation of the NeCR programme.

## 3.4.2. Description of NeCR

3.4.2.1. Preparatory nurse-led goal-setting phase. The preparatory phase was an in-hospital phase during which a registered nurse

with experience in cardiovascular nursing first conducted an individual asseesment of CHD disease management and provided a brief counselling session to create awareness of the effect of physical activity, dietary habits, smoking status, stress management practices and social adaptation to the disease process. The nurse also assessed the exercise capacity of the patient with a 6- mintue walking test to allow the tailoring of exercise goals. Based on the assessment data, the nurse and patient developed goals and action plans for lifestyle behavioural change and uploaded this information to the patient’s account on the NeCR e-platform. A group-based session (approximately 5–8 patients) was held by the nurse to orientate the participants to the NeCR e-platform and pedometer-based monitoring. The nurse also invited the patients to join the chatroom on WeChat (group size 12–16) for subsequent real-time interactions and mutual support.

3.4.2.2. Empowerment-based action phase. The ICT-based CR components were delivered through an account consisting of a password-protected website and a telecare platform. The website consisted of three platforms: a goal-attainment interface, an experiential learning platform and a health dialogue forum. The goal attainment interface presented behavioural goals for participants to upload goal-attainment data weekly or daily with graphical visualisation and motivational feedback. The participants were encouraged to indicate the frequency, intensity and duration of their exercise and the number of steps/day; complete a dietary checklist; mark their stress score; and indicate their smoking status (if relevant). A traffic light system with pre-determined and automated motivational messages was used to reflect the percentage of goal attainment after uploading their data. The experiential learning platform presented comprehensive details of the core CR components. The role of each CR component and the associated health benefits were briefly introduced, following which behavioural change action advice was presented. Various learning opportunities were provided, such as case scenarios, videos, pictures and comics, to engage participants. The health dialogue forum enabled participants to access recent CR news reports posted by the nurse. The nurse also initiated health dialogues based on the news. Patients were also encouraged to post their inquiries or questions on the forum for nurse and/ or peer responses.

The nurse collected health data uploaded by participants on the self-monitoring platform and shared goal-attainment progress via the WeChat group. Reminders for participants to upload the data were sent to participants weekly. Based on the trend analysis, the nurse encouraged participants to share their experiences in goal attainment, verbalise their concerns and provide advice to one another. The nurse also resolved any barriers encountered by the patients.

## 2.5. Usual care

Participants in both groups received usual care, which was a 10 min didactic session on medication usage and lifestyle changes (physical activity, diet and smoking cessation) delivered by staff nurses of the study hospital. For data collection, the researcher taught the control group participants how to use a pedometer.

## 2.6. Measurements

A self-designed demographic questionnaire was used to collect baseline sociodemographic data, including age, sex, marital status, education, employment status, living conditions and Internet use. Clinical data, including the diagnosis, treatment received, the diseased vessel and comorbidities, were retrieved from the medical records.

# 2.6.1. Primary outcomes
The primary outcome focused on lifestyle behavioural changes, namely increased physical activity, health-promoting lifestyle habits and smoking cessation. Physical activity comprises body movements that use energy. The level of physical activity was measured using a pedometer (Mi Band; Xiaomi, Beijing, China), which recorded the number of steps on three weekdays (El-Amrawy and Nounou, 2015). Pedometer measurements correlate strongly with the measurements of different accelerometers (median r 0.86) and the time spent in observed activity (median r 0.82) (Tudor-Locke et al., 2002). The International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), comprising 27 questions, was used to measure the self-reported physical activity level by assessing the time spent walking; undergoing rigorous, moderate, or light activities; and sitting. The IPAQ has acceptable test–retest reliability, with intraclass correlation coefficients of 0.74 to 0.97 for each sub-domain amongst Chinese adults (MacFarlane et al., 2011).

Health behaviours were measured using the Health-Promoting Lifestyle Profile II (HPLP- II) questionnaire (Walker et al., 1987). The HPLP-II questionnaire measures six behaviour domains related to exercise, health responsibility, nutrition, stress management, interpersonal support and self-actualisation. It has suitable inter-

nal consistency, with a correlation coefficient of 0.92. The questionnaire uses a Likert scale of 1–4, representing always to never, with a higher score indicating better health behaviour. The HPLP has been translated and validated for Chinese older adults, and the Chinese version has a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.63–0.81 (Cao et al., 2012).

Smoking status was assessed by asking (1) what is your current smoking status? (nonsmoker, ex-smoker, current smoker) and (2) how many cigarettes do you smoke per day (if applicable)? (Global Adult Tobacco Survey Collaborative Group, 2011).

# 2.6.2. Secondary outcomes
The secondary outcomes focused on changes in cardiac selfefficacy, health-related quality of life, psychological wellbeing, and cardiac physiological risk factors. Cardiac self-efficacy was measured using the Cardiac Self-efficacy Scale (CSES), which assesses an individual’s confidence in maintaining their function and controlling their symptoms (Sullivan et al., 1998). It uses a 5-point Likert scale, in which a higher score indicates greater self-efficacy. The CSES has good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.90 (original version) and 0.926 (Chinese version) (Zhang et al., 2018).

Health-related quality of life was assessed using the Mac-New Heart Disease Health-related Quality of Life (MacNew) questionnaire, which evaluates the influence of CHD on individuals’ physical, emotional and social wellbeing. The MacNew questionnaire has 27 items scored from 1 (low quality of life) to 7 (high quality of life), with internal consistency and intraclass correlation coefficients ≥ 0.73 (Hofer et al., 2004). The questionnaire has been translated and validated for Chinese CHD populations, with intraclass correlation coefficients ranging from 0.88 to 0.93 (Thompson et al., 2009).

Psychological wellbeing was measured using the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale 21 (DASS-21) with 21 items assessing three domains (Lovibond and Lovibond, 1995). The DASS-21 assesses the presence of symptoms over the past week using a 4-point (0–3) Likert scale, with higher scores indicating more prominent symptoms. The DASS-21 shows a good comparative fit index value (0.93), with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.95, 0.93 and 0.90 for anxiety, stress and depression, respectively (Crawford and Henry, 2003). This instrument correlated strongly with hospital anxiety ( r = 0 . 8 8 )and depression (r  0.93) scales at each time point (Sukantarat et al., 2007). The DASS-21 has been translated and validated in Chinese populations, with Cronbach’s alpha 0.80 (Chan et al., 2012; Zuo and Chang, 2008).

Cardiac physiological risk parameters, namely blood pressure (HEM-7124; Omron, Osaka, Japan), body mass index (BMI; RGZ-120-RT; Xiheng, China) and waist circumference (flexible tape), were assessed using standard approaches.

Unplanned use of health services included cardiac-related hospital readmission, re-vascularisation and emergency department visits. China does not have an electronic system to track the use of services. Therefore, the patients were reminded to call the outcome assessor whenever they were admitted to the hospital. To reduce recall bias, the outcome assessor called participants monthly to assess their unplanned use of medical services.

## 2.7. Procedure and ethics considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Joint Chinese University of Hong Kong – New Territories East Cluster Clinical Research Ethics Committee (No. 2018.469). The hospital also granted permission to conduct the study. The purpose and process of the study were explained to potential participants by the researcher. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant, and they were assured of their right to withdraw at any time without prejudice. Participants were also assured of the confidentiality of their health data and use of the e-platforms. Two trained research assistants collected baseline data after obtaining informed consent at the hospital. Block randomisation, using block sizes of four, six and eight, which were generated by random allocation software, were used to allocate the participants to the NeCR or usual care control groups. The group assignments were placed in an opaque sealed envelope in the sequence generated by the above method. The research assistant opened the envelope for the participants after baseline data collection. Block randomisation was used to equalise the rate of recruitment between the two study groups in order to recruit an adequate number of patients to form the WeChat groups. Post-test data were collected at 6 and 12 weeks post-intervention via face-to-face interviews conducted by research assistants who had no information of group assignment.

## 2.8. Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS v25 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the participants’ characteristics. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using skewness and kurtosis statistics. A t-test or chi-square

test was used to determine any significant differences in demographic, clinical or outcome variables between the two groups at baseline. The generalised estimating equation (GEE) model was used to examine changes in the outcome variables between the two groups across the study endpoints. The GEE model can handle missing data mathematically and accommodate group∗time interaction effects, thus making the estimation highly indicative of the intervention effect. Any variables with p \ < \ . 2for betweengroup differences at baseline were regarded as covariates and adjusted for in the GEE model. A chi-square test was used to compare the smoking cessation percentage between groups. Survival analysis was performed to assess the time between entering the programme and utilising unplanned health services. The level of significance was adjusted using Bonferroni correction, as the study involved multiple outcome comparisons (Ludbrook, 1998). Effect size estimates were calculated for all mean differences at 12 weeks using Hedges’ g, relating the mean score differences to the pooled standard deviation.
