## Study design

The study design was a randomized multicentre clinical trial to compare the results of the HCAD intervention at home (intervention group) for one month with a usual care and general exercise group (control group).

The research flow chart is shown in Figure 1. The study was approved by the local ethics committee of each clinical centre. The physicians of the three rehabilitation centres included patients following the jointly defined inclusion and exclusion criteria. After subjects passed the screening criteria, they signed an informed consent to participate in the study and baseline measurements were performed. After baseline assessments (T ), the subjects were randomly assigned to the intervention or control group.

Randomization was organized in such a way that it fitted into the clinical working practice. Each time HCAD was available, three patients were randomly assigned: two to the intervention group and the other one to the control group. The randomization was performed at each individual clinical centre.

A second assessment (T1) was performed after the month of usual care (for intervention as well as control group) and the third measurement (T ) was performed after the month of HCAD intervention for the intervention group and for the control group two months after baseline. All assessments were performed at the centre where the patient had received their treatment.

## Treatments

## Intervention group

The intervention group underwent one month of usual care. The duration and the type of therapy were recorded in a diary by the therapists. The month of usual care was followed by approximately four training sessions with the HCAD system in the hospital. The actual intervention with the HCAD system at home consisted of one month, whereby the patients had to perform at least one training session a day for five days a week with an average duration of 30 minutes.

The HCAD system comprised a hospital-based server and the portable unit which was installed at the patient’s home. The portable unit consisted of seven sensorized tools: a key, light bulb, book, jar, writing, checkers and keyboard (Figure 2). With this portable unit a set of exercises could be performed that summarized the movements for correct functional activity of the upper limb such as reaching, grasping, lateral pinch, pinch grip, holding, manipulation and finger dexterity. The portable unit was also equipped with two webcams, which allowed videoconferencing and recording. The videos and the results of the exercises were uploaded to the hospital server where all the data and videos were collected. The therapist used this information for the weekly videoconference with the patient.

## Control group

Subjects in the control group received usual care and generic exercises prescribed by their physicians. The

therapists completed a diary which contained the exercises performed by the patients and the treatment received.

# Outcome measures
The primary outcome measures were the Action Research Arm Test (ARAT) and the Nine Hole Peg Test (NHPT). The ARAT is an observational test consisting of 19 items focusing on grasping objects of different shapes and sizes, and gross movements in the vertical and horizontal planes (score range 0 to 57).13,14 The total score of all tests together for the affected arm (if both arms are affected, the dominant hand was used) was used in the analysis. The NHPT measures manual dexterity (and reaching and grasping).15 The total time needed to move the nine pegs with the affected arm (if both arms are affected, the dominant hand was used) was used as an outcome measure. When the patient was unable to move all nine pegs, a total time of 180 seconds was assumed.

## Data analysis

Baseline characteristics of the two groups were compared to evaluate the success of randomization. Mean and SDs were used to summarize scores on the ARAT and the NHPT.

To investigate whether the patients in the intervention group (after the month of using the HCAD system) performed at least as well as the usual care group on arm/ hand function the 90% confidence intervals for the changes on the main outcome measures between T and T were calculated for an effect between the groups (HCAD and usual care). Ninety percent confidence intervals were used because the tests were two-sided. These analyses were conducted separately for every diagnosis group (stroke, TBI and MS). To be able to decide whether the changes for the two groups (HCAD versus usual care) were equal, cut-off points were defined beforehand. The cut-off points were defined as the minimum clinically important difference (MCID) for each primary outcome measure. The cut-off points for the ARAT were an improvement or decrease of 5.7 points, which was considered as clinically equal.16,17 For the NHPT a decrease or increase of 18 seconds was taken as equal, which is 10% of the total time of 180 seconds – this seems appropriate, but was chosen quite arbitrarily. These clinical equality bounds were compared with the calculated confidence bounds in order to conclude whether the HCAD intervention was at least as good as usual care.

For both the HCAD and the usual care group the number of patients showing a clinical improvement on the primary outcome measures was calculated. The cut-off points used to classify a patient as improved or not were chosen as 10% of the total range of the scale.16 The baseline characteristics of the patients showing an improvement were compared to the baseline characteristics of the patients showing no clinical improvement.

The user satisfaction was asked by using a VAS. The VAS was turned into a numeric scale from 0 to 100. VAS scores of 30 mm or less were categorized as not satisfied; those from 31 mm to 69 mm as average scores (socially desirable answers); those with scores of 70 mm or more were categorized as satisfied.

For all analyses an intention-to-treat analysis, including patients with protocol deviations was performed. The SPSS (version 11.5) statistical computer program was used for the

statistical analyses. The Shapiro-Wilk statistic was used to test for normality.

# Results
## Subjects and loss to follow-up

Forty-seven men and 34 women were included in the study (average age 48 years, range 19–79 years). There were 16 stroke patients, 30 TBI patients and 35 MS patients. The average time since the start of the diagnosis was 10 years (range 1–35 years). Information about inclusion rate and number of patient data available at each measurement point is shown in Figure 1. The reasons for exclusion of the patients were most often insufficient or no impaired upper limb function; patients could not move one peg of the NHPT within 180 seconds or the patient’s arm/hand function was too good; they were able to perform the NHPT in less than 25 seconds. Some patients had medical complications and some refused to participate in the study. Of the 81 patients who were included in the study, 11 were lost during follow-up (14%). No difference was found in loss to follow-up between the groups: 13% (7 patients) in the intervention group versus 15% in the control group (4 patients).

## Baseline characteristics

There were no significant differences in subject characteristics at baseline (Table 1) which means that randomization was performed successfully.
