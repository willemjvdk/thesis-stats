# 2. Methods
## 2.1. Study design, setting and participant

The study design was a prospective randomized controlled clinical trial (Phase 3) with two groups, interventional vs. non-

interventional. In this monocentric study observer was blinded. The full protocol, including detailed descriptions of the randomization and intervention and statistical analysis plan, has been registered at http://ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT01822938) and was published previously [17]. This study was done in Limoges (France) and participants were recruited from the physical medicine and rehabilitation services at the Hospital Center. All procedures followed the recommendations of the institutional ethical committee as per the Good Clinical Practice Guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. This trial adheres to the CONSORT 2010 statements for randomized trials of nonpharmacologic treatments [18]. This project received authorization from the ethics and individual protection committees (IPC) (RCB: 2012- A01456-37) and. All people with a first stroke who were admitted to the physical medicine and rehabilitation services were screened as potential participants. The inclusion criteria were: age - 18 years; first ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke; stroke within < 6 months; Modified Functional Ambulatory Categories ( \mathrm { m F A C } ) \geq 2 ;registered with the French social security system.

The non-inclusion criteria was: physical disorders limiting gait skills before or after stroke; cognitive disorders impeding comprehension of PA education – i.e., aphasia, as indicated by a Boston Diagnostic Aphasia Examination (BDAE) score < 2; uncontrolled hypertension; inability to complete questionnaires; cardiopulmonary pathology preventing effort completion; involvement in other research; under legal guardianship; and pregnancy.

All eligible subjects gave written informed consent according to the protocol. They were randomly assigned (blocks of variable size) to an intervention group receiving regular individualized coaching on PA (EG), or to a control group receiving standard care (CG). The randomization process was ensured using a secure internet connection with access by individual user ID and password and encrypted data transmission to the randomization platform of the Clinical Research Unit of the Limoges University Hospital. The adverse events monitoring was carried out in accordance with current French law [17].

## 2.2. Interventions

After inclusion, the PA therapist designed and implemented an individualized PA program for all eligible participant. In this program the PA therapist explained the PA recommendations of the French Society of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation and the French Neurovascular Society and the benefits of an active lifestyle in preventing stroke recurrence, in accordance with the French guidelines on stroke treatment. He included an educational diagnosis, selection of physical activities, and definition of goals [19].

After hospital discharge, all the eligible participant received the usual care for 12 months which may have include outpatient therapy, medical appointment at 1, 6 and 12 months.

The EG participants underwent the individualized coaching program. This phase included monitoring, weekly phone calls, and a home visit every 3 weeks for 6 months. An activity tracker (AT) (SenseWear armband, BodyMedia, Pittsburgh, PA, USA) was used to monitor PA at home. The participant was asked to wear the AT when they woke up and remove it before going to bed. We chose this sensor because it is regularly used in the analysis of activity in participants and it has the advantage of starting only when it is in contact with the person’s skin. Therefore, the data we will get corresponds to the AT wearing time. As part of the protocol the EG were routinely monitored.

 each day to complete a simple chart to measure subjective perception of PA for the therapist to compare with the objective values of the PA;

After the 6 months coaching program, EG received only the usual care for 6 months.

The CG was not monitor their PA and was not receive home visits or telephone calls. Also, no further coaching about PA was provided during the 12 months follow-up.

## 2.3. Measurements

For outcome measurements, all subjects were evaluated after hospital discharge (T0), again immediately after the 6-month intervention (T1), and again after the 6-month follow-up (T2). All assessments were conducted by the same medical doctor and professional PA therapist in the hospital.

The primary outcome was the difference in the 6MWT between the two groups at T1. The 6MWT was conducted as one trial and administered on a 20 m segment of a quiet corridor. The final score was the distance walked during the 6MWT.

For the other outcomes, the two groups were compared in terms of independence, motricity, ambulatory ability, body composition and lower limb strength at T1 and T2. Activities of daily living were assessed by the Barthel index (BI) [20]. The motricity index(MI) was used to measure motor function [21]. Ambulatory ability was rated using the mFAC [22]. The Zmetrix (Biopharom1, Challes Les Eaux, France) bioelectrical impedance analyzer was used to evaluate the percentage of fat and muscular mass. Maximal strength knee extension was measured by the Dynatrac2 dynamometer (YSY-Medical, Gallargues-le-Montueux, France). All participants completed the following questionnaires during the medical appointment at 1, 6 and 12 month before the other evaluation of the study: The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale(HADS) [23] and the multidimensional fatigue inventory questionnaire(MFI-20) [24]. No formal monitoring of adherence was undertaken in this study.

## 2.4. Statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated using the Kamps et al. study to have a 30% difference in the distance on the 6MWT between the two groups after intervention (mean  SD, 237.8  115.7 m for interventional group vs. 185.3  88.3 for interventional group) [25]. Thirty-six participants were needed in each group in order to achieve 80% power with a two-sided 5% significance level. To account for an expected drop-out rate of 15%, 42 participants were included in each group.

All participants are included in the analysis. The data were analyzed on the basis of intention to treat. Quantitative variables were described according to mean and standard deviation or median, interquartile range (IQR). The normality of the variables analyzed was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test.

# 2.4.1. Primary outcome
6MWT comparisons between the two groups at T1 (6 months) was made using a statistical test, such as the Student or Wilcoxon t-test.

# 2.4.2. Other outcomes
For all other the variables, comparisons between the two groups at T1 and T2 were made using a statistical test, such as the Student or Wilcoxon t-test. Finally, we compare the primary outcome

between T0, T1 and T2 in both groups. Then, a complementary perprotocol analysis was made. We tested a group effect, a time effect, and the interaction between the two. Repeated data analysis used SPSS PROC MIXED. The analysis was based on the data itself (if the normality of the variables was met), or on its rankings if it was not.

For the EG, the activity level is represented per week with an error bar graph, with a 95% confidence error bar for the mean. The change in the level of physical activity during follow-up was assessed based on a Friedman test.

The P-value considered statistically significant was P < 0.05. Data were analyzed with SAS1 9.3 (SAS Institute Cary, NC).

# 3. Results
Fig. 1 shows the participant flow in the trial. As screening data were not collected in this trial, the estimated number of individuals assessed for eligibility is based on data from one department, in which 15% of patients admitted for cerebrovascular disease were enrolled in the trial. A total of 162 stroke patients were eligible and 84 met the inclusion criteria, agreed to participate, and were randomized and included from April 2013 to January 2017. One participant decided to withdraw written consent; for this reason, the statistical analysis was done with 83 participants. Ten participants dropped out after randomization. In cases of drop out/intervention discontinuing, we were able to collect their outcome data and they were included in the final analysis (except for your withdrawal). There were no clinically relevant differences between group variables or outcome measures at baseline. Table 1 shows the demographic information and characteristics of stroke patients included in the study. No adverse events or safety issues occurred during the course of our study.

For all variables at T0, the Shapiro-Wilk test has a p-value less than 0.05. We therefore rejected the null hypothesis that our sample follows a normal distribution. The following analyses were carried out using non-parametric tests.
