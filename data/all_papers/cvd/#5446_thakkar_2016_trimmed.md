## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# Methods
The present analysis was a secondary analysis of the TEXT ME study. The methods and text-messages development process are detailed elsewhere22–24 and briefly described here. Adult participants with CHD from a large university-affiliated tertiary level hospital in western Sydney, Australia, were eligible if they had English language proficiency sufficient to read text messages and a personal mobile phone. Patients were recruited while in the hospital or soon after hospitalization in clinics. CHD was defined as prior myocardial infarction, percutaneous coronary intervention, coronary bypass surgery or coronary angiogram with greater than 50% stenosis in at least one major epicardial artery. Participants in the intervention arm received text message support in addition to usual care whereas the control arm received only usual care. Randomization was in a uniform 1:1 ratio with a block size of eight and allocation was concealed from the study staff. Traditional exercise based cardiac rehabilitation programme was offered to all patients with CHD as a standard hospital policy as part of usual care. Those participants that accepted the cardiac rehabilitation program, attended this either preceding (30%) or in parallel (70%) with the TEXT ME intervention. The text messages (semi-personalized, non-repetitive, 1/ day, 4/ week, on random weekdays, between 10:00 h and 16:00 h) offered information on four major secondary prevention areas including physical activity, diet, smoking cessation and general cardiac education. The programme was designed to deliver at least one physical activity message (one for each major secondary prevention area) per week. Illustrative physical activity messages are listed in Table 1. Patients provided written informed consent and ethical approval was obtained from the Western Sydney Local Health Network Human Research Ethics Committee.

## Assessment of physical activity domains

Physical activity was assessed at baseline and follow-up using the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ).11 The GPAQ is an interviewer-administered questionnaire and was developed by the World Health Organization (WHO) for population based physical activity surveillance. The GPAQ is a 16-question tool

designed to estimate individuals’ physical activity levels in three major domains (work, travel and recreation) and sedentary behaviour. GPAQ has been validated in a large nine country study.25 Questionnaire such as GPAQ are shown to have fair–moderate correlation with objective methods of physical activity estimates like pedometers or accelerometers.26

In the GPAQ, participants report time (in hours and minutes) spent doing work activities, recreational activities, transport-travel preferences and sedentary activities (mainly time spent sitting) in a typical week. The questionnaire defines ‘vigorous-intensity activities’ as those that require hard physical effort and cause large increases in breathing or heart rate for at least 10 minutes continuously (e.g. lifting heavy loads, digging, construction work, running, playing football). ‘Moderate-intensity activities’ are defined as those that require moderate physical effort and cause small increases in breathing or heart rate for at least 10 min continuously (e.g. brisk walk, carrying light loads, cycling, swimming, playing volleyball). Sedentary behaviour is defined as any behaviour during wakeful hours typically associated with time spent in sitting, reclining or lying down, characterized by energy expenditure of - 1.5 metabolic equivalents (METs).

## Physical activity computations

In the GPAQ, physical activity is calculated as MET-min/week as per WHO recommendations.11 The MET values were applied to the time variable according to the intensity of physical activity. For the domains work and recreational physical activity 4 MET value is applied for moderate intensity exercise and 8 MET value is applied for vigorous intensity exercise.11 For the travel domain, that is, walking or cycling, a 4 MET value is applied during the computations.

In accordance with GPAQ recommendations we calculated physical activity for work and recreational domains as MET-min/week ¼ (vigorous activity min per week  8) þ (moderate activity min per week  4). Physical activity related to travel was calculated as MET-min/week ¼ (walk/bicycle ride min per week  4). Discretional physical activity was defined as time spent in non-obligatory physical activities. We calculated discretional physical activity as the sum of ‘recreational physical activity’ and ‘travel related physical activity’.

## Statistical analysis

We performed all analysis using SPSS statistical package (IBM Corporation, Version 22). All statistical tests were two-tailed and a 5% significance level maintained throughout the analyses. Summaries of continuous baseline variables were presented as means and standard deviations or medians and inter-quartile ranges as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

To analyse the effect of TEXT ME on physical activity domains and sedentary times at six months we used an analysis of covariance model which uses the baseline values of the corresponding variables as the covariate. We used a stepwise approach to assess whether participant demographics, risk factors and baseline physical activity levels were predictors of engaging in physical activity at six months. Guideline recommended physical activity level (total physical activity < 600 MET-min/week or  600 MET-min/week) was used as the dependent variable. We used a binary logistic regression model and expressed the associations as odds ratio (OR). The covariates present in the final model were determined on the basis of univariate significance and/or clinical judgment. In order to examine the consistency of effects of TEXT ME across the subgroups, we conducted stratified analysis using a logistic regression model and tested for interaction to assess whether there was significant heterogeneity of effect across sub-groups. Our choice for sub-grouping was guided by clinically relevant participant characteristics. We also performed analysis to study interaction with cardiac rehabilitation which is traditionally considered as a gold standard for enhancing exercise in patients with CHD.

# Results
Between September 2011 and November 2013, a total of 710 (352 intervention and 358 control) participants

were randomized in the TEXT ME study (Figure A1 in the Supplementary Material online). Baseline physical activity data was available for 708 participants and follow-up physical activity data was available for 689

participants. Five patients had died prior to six month follow-up. Sixteen participants had incomplete data due to varied reasons and were categorized as inactive. At baseline (Table 2), 85% of the participants reported energy expenditure < 600 MET-min/week. Participants categorized as active were marginally younger and had higher total years of education. The two groups had comparable risk factor profiles.

At baseline, intervention and control groups reported similar levels of domain specific physical activity and sedentary times (Figure 1:a,b,c). The most commonly reported type of physical activity was recreational activities (29%) followed by travel-related physical activity (17%) and work or occupational related physical activity (5%). Overall 38% reported undertaking discretional physical activity (i.e. recreational physical activity plus travel physical activity). At baseline, the mean discretional physical activity was 218 MET-min/week (SD 481, 95% confidence interval (CI) 183–254): intervention arm 195 METmin/week (SD 427, 95% CI 150–240) and control arm 242 MET-min/week (SD 530, 95% CI 187–297) (p ¼ 0.187).

## Physical activity domains and sedentary times at six months

Compared with baseline, at six months both study arms reported an increase in discretional physical activity. At six months, 50.4 % of participants in the intervention arm achieved discretional physical activity of  600 MET-min/week compared with 25.3% in the control arm. The mean discretional physical activity was higher in the intervention arm compared with the control arm (703 vs. 434 MET-min/week; difference þ 269 MET-min/week, SE 58, 95% CI 155–382, p < 0.001). Both recreational and travel components of discretional physical activity were higher in the intervention group compared with the control group at six months (Figure 1 and Table 3). There was no significant difference

between the two groups at six months for work related physical activity. At six months the intervention group reported significantly lower sedentary times compared with the control (494 vs. 587 min; difference 94 min, SE 13, 95% CI 120 to 168, p < 0.001) (Table 3).

## Treatment effect by subgroups and cardiac rehabilitation

In a stratified analysis, there was no significant heterogeneity (p value for interaction all > 0.05) across all subgroups tested (by age, gender, baseline body mass index, cardiac rehabilitation, baseline physical activity, diabetes and hypertension) (Table 4), indicating that the TEXT ME intervention worked consistently across all subgroups. Patients that received cardiac rehabilitation and the TEXT ME interventions were seven times more likely to be physically active compared with patients that received neither (OR 7.07, 95% CI 4.30–11.63). Compared with cardiac rehabilitation alone, addition of the text messaging intervention resulted in significant incremental effect on physical activity (Table 5).

## Predictors of achieving target physical activity levels at six months

Male gender (OR 1.59, 95% CI 0.99–2.53, p ¼ 0.053), participation in cardiac rehabilitation (OR 1.68, 95% CI 1.20–2.37, p ¼ 0.003), higher baseline physical activity levels (OR 2.95, 95% CI 1.80–4.82, p < 0.01) and randomization to the text message intervention arm (OR 4.14, 95% CI 2.94–5.84, p < 0.01) were significant predictors of achieving target physical activity levels (Table A1 online). Diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, depression, current active smoker or other socio-demographic variables were not significantly associated with achieving target physical activity levels at six months (Table A1 Supplementary material online).
