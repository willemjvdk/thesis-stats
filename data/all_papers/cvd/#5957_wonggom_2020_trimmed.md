# 3 | METHODS
## 3.1 | Trial design

The study was a multi-centred, non-blinded, randomized, two-armed parallel pragmatic, controlled trial study at three HF outpatient clinics in three public hospitals in South Australia.

Pragmatic randomized controlled trials are an important development in research design for the evaluation of interventions and services in clinical research. These entail a compromise between observational studies and conventional randomized controlled trials to bridge the gap in understanding between clinicians and researchers and ultimately lead to improvements in services (Creswell & Creswell, 2017; Hotopf, 2002). The design of pRCTs reflects the heterogeneity of patients in clinical practice, minimizes exclusion criteria, focuses on groups with a wide range of diagnoses (Creswell & Creswell, 2017). A detailed report of the study protocol was published in 2018, a brief summary of which is provided below (Wonggom et al., 2018).

## 3.2 | Participants and setting

Inclusion criteria for the participants were as follows: (a) a confirmed diagnosis of HF; (b) have had a previous hospitalization for HF; (c) in New York Heart Association Functional Class (NYHA-FC) I–IV; (d) normal cognitive function (MoCA score ≥26; Nasreddine et al., 2005); (e) sufficient English language capabilities to communicate and to follow the study procedure; and (f) willing to give informed consent. Exclusion criteria for the participants were: (a) those who were clinically unstable—defined as patients who did not achieve the five normal vital signs and normal mental status and ability to eat, for at least 24 hr prior to enrolling in the study; and (b) cognitive impairment.

## 3.3 | Usual care

Participants in the control group (UC) received usual care. Usual care included care from HF nurses or regular nurses who are responsible for bedside education to reinforce the diagnosis, treatment, self-care, self-monitoring and follow-up provided by HF nurses at the HF clinic in line with site practice. As part of usual care, all patients were provided with the educational booklets from the Heart Foundation of Australia. Patients were followed up at 30 and 90-days.

## 3.4 | Intervention

Participants allocated to the intervention group received a combination of the usual care and an avatar app (UC + I; Figure 1). The app was pre-installed on a tablet computer. The research nurse introduced the app to each participant and provided an instruction on to how to use it at home. The participant reviewed the app once with the research nurse together before they were

discharged from hospital. The participants were asked to take the tablet home and use the app as often, or as little as they would like to. The content of the avatar app is based on the Heart Foundation of Australia's booklet, ‘Living Well with Heart Failure’ (National Heart Foundation of Australia, 2016) and includes four sections: (a) understanding heart failure; (b) looking after yourself; (c) things to do every day and; (d) emergency action plans. Further details of the intervention are published in the study protocol (Wonggom et al., 2018).

# 3.5 | Outcome measures
The primary outcome was HF knowledge measured by the Dutch Heart Failure Knowledge Scale (DHFKS). Secondary outcomes were HF self-care behaviours, HF-related readmission and satisfaction (UC + I group only). In addition, participant's demographic and clinical information were collected. Participants also completed the Montreal cognitive assessment (MoCA), Rapid Estimate of Adult Literacy in Medicine—Short Form (REALM-SF) and Short Test of

Functional Health literacy in Adults (STOFHLA). The details are reported in the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trial flow chart in the study protocol (Wonggom et al., 2018; Figure 2).

## 3.6 | Sample size

The study was designed to be a full pRCT, with a plan to recruit 88 participants based on power analysis (Wonggom et al., 2018). The Dutch HF knowledge scale (DHFKS) was used as the primary outcome measure in this study. Due to the innovative nature of this intervention and therefore minimal previous studies in this area (Wonggom et al., 2019) the sample size calculations were based exclusively on a study by Delaney et al. (2013). Repeated measures procedure from PASS sample size estimation software was used (Hintze, 2015). Autocorrelation of 0.7 and between-subject SD of 2.075 (HF knowledge using the DHFKS taken as average of SDs from the Delaney's paper) was hypothesized. Results from the Delany study provided the means and the standard deviations used in the PASS procedure to calculate the sample size. Data were entered directly into the software resulting in estimated sample size of 40 participants per group to achieve 80% power at an alpha of 0.05 (Power Analysis and Sample Size 14™). Effect sizes (σm/σ) (i.e. an index of the size of the mean differences relative to the SD) were equal 0.38, 0.78 and 0.35 for B(2), W(3) and B × W terms respectively. We anticipated a loss to follow up of 10% and therefore estimated 88 participants (44 per group) were required to be enrolled in this study.

## 3.7 | Recruitment and randomization

Each hospital's medical and nursing staff assisted the research nurse to identify potential participants from the HF outpatient clinics. The medical and nursing staff obtained each patient's permission to be contacted by the research nurse. Potential participants were screened by the research nurse to determine eligibility at this time.

Participants were randomly assigned to either a control or intervention group with a 1:1 ratio. A block randomization for sequence generation was implemented by an independent clinical trial management centre using a computer randomization system. Neither the research nurse nor the participant could decide which group they were in.

As the avatar education app is an education intervention, blinding of the participants and the researchers who conducted telephone follow-up data collection was not possible.

## 3.8 | Data collection

Data collection was performed between November 2018 and May 2019. Data were collected at baseline using medical record review and face-to-face interview and by telephone at the 30-day and 90- day follow-up. The following instruments were used to collect data from both groups.

## 3.8.1 | Demographic and clinical information

The demographic data sheet was used to collect information from patient interviews and medical records. The demographic data included gender, date of birth, country of birth, language spoken at home, contact details, marital status, living status, occupation and level of education. The clinical information included diagnosis, previous medical history, past procedures, family history, smoking status, past admissions, the Charlson Comorbidity Index, left ventricular function, the NYHA-FC, aetiology of HF and cardiac medications.

## 3.8.2 | The Montreal cognitive assessment

The Montreal cognitive assessment (MoCA) is a brief 30-question test. The MoCA assesses different types of cognitive abilities, including orientation, short-term memory, executive function, language abilities, attention and visuospatial ability. The MoCA was used in this study to evaluate potential participants’ cognitive function, as a part of assessing their eligibility (Nasreddine et al., 2005).

## 3.8.3 | Rapid Estimate of Adult Literacy in Medicine—Short Form

The Rapid Estimate of Adult Literacy in Medicine—Short Form (REALM-SF) provides researchers with a brief, validated instrument for assessing patient literacy in diverse research settings in the healthcare system. The REALM-SF was used to assess the health literacy level of study participants at baseline (Arozullah et al., 2007).

## 3.8.4 | Short Test of Functional Health literacy in Adults

The Short Test of Functional Health literacy in Adults (STOFHLA) was used to assess participants’ capability to read and understand health information materials. The STOFHLA consists of four Numeracy items and two-prose passage and is scored out of 36. There are three functional health literacy levels including inadequate (0-16 score), marginal (17-22 score) and adequate (23-26 score) (Baker, Williams, Parjer, Gazmararian & Nurss, 1999).

## 3.8.5 | The Dutch Heart Failure Knowledge Scale

Participant's HF knowledge is patients’ understanding of their heart condition and HF related self-care regimen. It was measured by the Dutch Heart Failure Knowledge Scale (DHFKS), which consists of 15 multiple-choice items. The DHFKS has a minimum score of zero points (no knowledge) and a maximum score of 15 points (optimal knowledge). The Cronbach's α of this knowledge

scale in HF population was 0.62 (van der Wal, Jaarsma, Moser, & van Veldhuisen, 2005).

## 3.8.6 | The Self-care of Heart Failure Index

The HF patient's self-care behaviours were assessed by the Self-care of Heart Failure Index (SCHFI; version 6.2), which is a survey consisting of 22 questions. The possible range of total score for the index is 0–100. A score of ≥ 70 has been used to indicate adequacy of self-care. The Cronbach's α for each of the three sections are 0.553 (maintenance), 0.597 (management) and 0.827 (confidence) (Riegel, Lee, Dickson, & Carlson, 2009).

## 3.8.7 | Healthcare use

Readmission was assessed for HF-caused readmission including both emergency and elective admissions to hospital and unplanned visits to general physician units via self-report and a research nurse review of medical records for verification of the admission or visit.

## 3.8.8 | The avatar application satisfaction questionnaire

The avatar app satisfaction questionnaire was adapted from previous research on surveys measuring satisfaction with educational materials (Clark et al., 2015; Wakefield et al., 2008). The satisfaction questionnaire consists of 15 Likert-scale items that refer to the avatar's design, audio, visual quality, content, usefulness and userfriendliness. Open-ended questions were included to allow participant feedback about the app.

# 3.9 | Data analysis and statistical methods
All data were collected in written form and entered into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet (Microsoft Corporation, 2016). Most statistical analyses were undertaken in IBM Statistic Package for Social Science (spss 2017) for Windows, version 25 (IBM Corp., 2017). Data were analysed according to the intention-to-treat principle to provide unbiased assessment of intervention effectiveness. Descriptive statistics for the approximately normally distributed numerical variables were presented as mean (SD) and N (%) for categorical variables. Independent sample t-tests were performed to compare the differences of the continuous variables between the two groups when normality and homogeneity assumptions were satisfied; otherwise, Mann–Whitney U tests were used. Chisquared or Fisher's exact tests were used to compare the categorical variables.

The primary outcome variable of interest was HF knowledge. Secondary outcomes included self-care behaviours, HF related

readmission and satisfaction. To estimate the differences between the 30-day as well as 90-day outcomes, mean change between usual care, plus intervention and usual care groups, two ANCOVAs were performed. (Vickers & Altman, 2001). Bootstrapping was implemented to accommodate slight non-normality of some outcome measures (Haukoos & Lewis, 2005). Fisher's exact test was used to compare the two groups’ differences in terms of HF-caused readmission. Bivariate exact binary logistic regressions (StataCorp, 2018) were used to identify the predictors associated with baseline levels of knowledge. However, the limitation of the small number of events (Peduzzi, Concato, Kemper, Holford, & Feinstein, 1996) per parameter produced large confidence intervals. A P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant and all tests were two tailed. The satisfaction questionnaire outcomes were summarized as descriptive statistics. Suggestions regarding data analyses were offered by a statistician who was not involved in the screening, recruitment or follow-up of study participants.

## 3.10 | Validity and reliability

The study adhered to the designated protocol strictly (Wonggom et al., 2018). Randomization was performed after giving informed consent and completing the baseline questionnaires. To ensure intervention fidelity, the same research assistant provided training to all UC + I participants on how to use the app. Training in relation to the purpose and design of the trial, data collection procedures and ethical requirements was provided to the research assistant who was an experienced heart failure specialist. Reporting of the study conforms with the CONSORT Statement (Schulz, Altman, & Moher, 2010).

## 3.11 | Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee (OFR#187.17-HREC/17SAC/268). All researchers had Good Clinical Practice (GCP) accreditation and the study was conducted using GCP principles. The ethical principles on which this study was conducted were based on the National Health and Medical Research Council's National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research (2007). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

## 4.2 | Baseline characteristics

Tables 1 and 2 show the demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants. There were no statistical differences between UC and UC + I at baseline (all P > . 0 5 ).

The mean age of the participants was 67.5 years (SD 11.3); 81% (29/36) were male and 72% (26/36) living with their spouse. Approximately half of the participants had attained diploma level or higher education (53%, 19/36); 89% (32/36) had adequate health literacy and 64% (23/36) were retired (Table 1).

Approximately half (17/36, 47.2%) of the participants had been diagnosed with HF more than 5 years ago and 92% (33/36) were NYHA-FC I or II. The most common cause of HF in the group was dilated cardiomyopathy (15/36, 42%) and 55.6% (20/36) had two or less associated co-morbidities based on the Charlson Comorbidity Index (Table 2).

## 4.6 | Factors associated with improvement in HF knowledge

Demographic and clinical characteristics of the UC + I group were analysed to investigate the predictors of high and low knowledge scores at baseline. These included age, sex, marital status, living status, educational level attained, country of birth, occupation, health literacy, type of HF, aetiology of HF, NYHA-FC and comorbidities. ## 4.7 | Patient reported experiences-satisfaction

During the 90-day study period, 15 UC + I participants used the avatar app. There was considerable variability in the frequency and duration of app use during the 90-day follow-up period. Usage ranged from 2.6–4 times per month.

Overall satisfaction with the avatar app was high at 91.3%. Additionally, the participants stated that the app delivered health information that was easy to understand and appropriate in terms of time required to review the app (100.0%). ## 4.8 | Harms or adverse events

There was no harm, adverse events or unintended effects in either group of the study.

# 5.3  | Patient-centre care and outcomes
There is increased recognition of the importance of adopting a patient-centred approach in designing interventions to improve patient self-management. The European Society of Cardiology recommends consumer co-design for the development all digital solutions (Cowie et al., 2016). Our research project incorporated consumer engagement and peer teaching in the co-design and development process. Using this approach is consistent with patient-centred care and may be one of the reasons the avatar app was effective in increasing HF knowledge (Wonggom et al., 2018). Participants reported excellent satisfaction with their experience. We strongly encourage future research in self-management for health care to include the intended end users in the design of interventions to improve the patient experience.

## 5.4 | Health care use

This small clinical trial was not adequately powered to make any inference on the impact of the avatar app on health care use. However, reducing health care use remains an important focus in self-management to reduce avoidable hospitalization and mortality (Lainscak et al., 2011).

## 5.5 | Strength and limitations

## 5.5.1 | Strengths

This study has multiple strengths that should be considered in future research trials. First, we used a pRCT design. The result was the recruitment of a broad sample of HF participants reflecting the complexity of HF care and increasing the applicability of the results to a broad range of HF patients. In addition, four possible biases associated with a pRCT were controlled for including performance bias, selection bias, detection bias and attrition bias. The details of these controls are published in the study protocol (Wonggom et al., 2018).

Second, we employed an expert cardiac nurse as the research nurse. Having an expert cardiac nurse helped to ensure the integrity of inclusion and exclusion criteria was maintained whilst trying to recruit as many participants as possible.

Finally, while we were not able to blind the participants or researchers to the group allocation, patients were randomized via a telephone service independent of the researchers. The process used was simple but effectively maintained allocation concealment. This became important as recruitment rates were low and the block randomization ensured similar size groups in the final analyses.

## 5.5.2 | Limitations

We acknowledge this study has some limitations. First, despite numerous efforts to increase the recruitment rate, we were unable to recruit the target of 88 participants in the allotted timeframe. The result was a smaller sample than originally anticipated and the generalizability of the study's findings are limited. Furthermore, we cannot fully elucidate the impact of the addition of an avatar app in a broader range of outcome measures.

However, these novel and positive results should be considered as pilot data for a larger funded study in the future. Not only should future research with a larger number of participants be considered to confirm or counter the promising findings presented here, there should be increased emphasis on the importance of educational interventions to that of other clinical trials.

Second, while a pRCT design is a strength, it is also a weakness as there was wide variability in HF diagnosis and number of years with disease. This may have led to a loss of resolution in who truly benefited from the avatar app. We recommend that a future study should recruit newly diagnosed patients. However, despite

the heterogeneity of aetiology, types of HF and years since diagnosis the app demonstrated positive effect on HF knowledge and some self-care behaviours across this broad spectrum of HF.

Third, the avatar-based application was only available in the English language excluding participants from culturally and linguistically diverse populations. Future research studies should consider translating the existing application into multiple languages.

Fourth, while we used validated and reliable tools to measure outcomes of self-care behaviours, a possible ceiling effect caused by testing in a group of HF patients who were not de novo, may explain the non-significant outcomes observed (van der Wal, 2005).

Last, there were 43 patients that declined to participate in this study despite being identified as eligible. Whilst we did not question the potential participants further once they indicated an unwillingness to participate, it would be useful to understand the reasons for declining to participate. For example, previous research has indicated the importance of exploring the perception and readiness among elderly patients towards the use of technology (Gallagher et al., 2017; Liu, Stroulia, Nikolaidis, Miguel, & Rincon, 2016; Makkonen, Frank, & Koivisto, 2017).

## 6 | CONCLUSION

The addition of a co-designed avatar app to usual care improved knowledge in our group of HF participants at 30 days and continued to increase up to 90 days. Collectively, the results presented here suggest that our avatar app was perceived as an enjoyable and engaging means of delivering critical knowledge and self-care information.
