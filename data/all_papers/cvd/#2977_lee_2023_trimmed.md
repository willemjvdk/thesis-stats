# Materials and methods
## Study design

This study was a randomized controlled trial with a blind observer. Patients were randomly divided into two experimental groups: the smart-rehab group and the control group (Figure 1). We used a random permuted block design, in which envelopes were sealed by persons not associated with the study. Patients in the smart-rehab group underwent HEP with an AR system, whereas patients in the control group underwent the written and pictorial HEP. Both groups used the HEP five times a week, 30 min a day, for four weeks. No participant received any type of additional rehabilitation therapy during the study period. The assessments were performed three times: at baseline before the HEP (T0), immediately after the HEP (T1), and four-weeks after

cessation of the HEP (T2). The assessments were conducted by investigators blinded to patient status.

## Participants

Outpatients were included in the study within six months of stroke from two different hospitals. All patients were able to walk with or without supervision at levels 3 to 5 in Functional Ambulatory Category (FAC) [25] and were between 19 and 80 years of age. The FAC assesses functional ambulation in patients with stroke. FAC scores range from 0 to 5, and higher scores indicate higher ambulatory function. Exclusion criteria were progressive or unstable stroke, pre-existing and active major neurological disease or major psychiatric disease, and severe cognitive impairment with a score less than 10 on the Korean Mini-Mental State Examination [26].

All study procedures were performed with written informed consent from the individuals involved and with the approval of the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of each hospital (IRB of Samsung Medical Center (IRB no. 2017-07-072) and IRB of Seoul St. Mary�s Hospital (IRB no. KC17FNDI0559)). We recruited using a participant recruitment poster in the outpatient clinic approved by IRB of each hospital. The study was registered on clinicaltrials.- gov (NCT03282734).

## Home-based exercise program with the AR system

For participants of the smart-rehab group, UINCARE HomeVR (Uincare Co.) was provided for four weeks. A licensed physical therapist or occupational therapist trained participants for 10 min to use UINCARE HomeVR at T0 in a hospital. In addition, the UINCARE HomeVR system was setup in participants’ homes by a technician on the research team, and the technician again showed participants how to use the system for 10–30 min at home. The sensor is a Universal Serial Bus plug-and play device that translates the scene geometry into depth information. From the point at which it is located, the sensor has an effective angle of 70�, a distance range of 0.8–3.5 m, and a response time of 10 ms, and it generates images of the participant with a resolution of 640 � 480 at 30 frames per second. The body parts were isolated from the depth image, the joints were distinguished from each body part, and a relative vector value was calculated for each body part. A computer operated by Window 7 with a 2.9-GHz quad-core CPU and 4 GB SDRAM rendered the images onto a 60-inch monitor with a resolution of 1,920 � 1,080. UINCARE HomeVR was operated by the participants or their caregiver at home, providing control of the training modules and level of difficulty. UINCARE HomeVR simulates arm and trunk movements designed to restore specific functional deficits using an AR system. Participants were able to practice various movements by copying specific motions of the UINCARE HomeVR avatar projected in the real world to show the participant’s actual movements. The motions were intended to promote incremental improvement in range of motion and endurance, strength, and deviation from synergistic motion patterns [27]. The participants’ actual movements during each motion were recorded and played back at the end of the motion to provide visual and auditory feedback, such as success or failure of each motion (Figure 2). An interactive rehabilitation program was accomplished using 30 min of rehabilitation training per session at 10 intensity levels. Each rehabilitation training session consisted of three sets of 10 repetitions for seven motions. One repetition of each motion was performed for eight seconds. The rest time between sets was 20 s. At levels 1 and 2, participants performed a program designed to train the control, endurance, speed, accuracy, and range of motion of the upper extremities and trunk while sitting in a chair. At levels 3–6, participants performed a program designed to train the control, endurance, speed, accuracy, and range of motion for the trunk and hip while standing on a level surface. At levels 7–10, participants performed a program designed to train the control, endurance, speed, accuracy, and range of motion of the whole body while standing on a level surface. The initial intensity level was determined by the Timed

Up-and-Go test (TUG) at the initial assessment (T0) by a licensed physical therapist or occupational therapist at a hospital. The performance status during each session was delivered to the researcher by web-based data collection. Once a week, the intensity level was modulated by the same physical therapist or occupational therapist via telephone according to the self-exercise performance status by web-based data collection. When a participant achieved greater than 80% performance in each rehabilitation training session with AR system, the intensity level in the next week was increased.

## Written and pictorial home-based exercise program

In the control group, a licensed physical therapist or occupational therapist explained a conventional exercise program including stretching, aerobic, and strengthening exercises with a written and pictorial HEP for 10 min at T0. In the conventional exercise program, stretching exercises consisted of static stretching of lower extremity muscles for five minutes before aerobic exercise. Participants performed aerobic exercise such as walking or using a stationary bicycle at moderate intensity for 10 min. After aerobic exercise, standing posture balance exercise or lower extremity strength exercises in the supine position were performed alternately for 10 min daily. Participants performed stretching exercise again during the cool-down session. In addition, a brochure was provided to the participants. Once a week, each participant was encouraged to exercise by the same physical therapist or occupational therapist via telephone.

# Behavioral outcome measures
TUG [28] was used to assess participants’ balance function. In each assessment, two TUGs were performed, and the mean time to complete the TUG was obtained by averaging two trials used for the assessment parameter. In addition, the Tinetti Performance Oriented Mobility Assessment (POMA) [29] and Berg Balance scale (BBS) [30] were used. The POMA scores range from 0 to 28, where higher score indicates better balance function. The BBS scores range from 0 to 56, where higher score indicates better balance function. All assessments were performed independently by a blinded rater in a hospital.

All participants completed structured self-administered questionnaires and a face-to-face interview to assess fear of falling with the Falls Efficacy Scale-International (FES-I) [31]. The Geriatric Depression Sale-Short Form (GDS-SF) [32] and Euro Quality of Life (EQ)-5D [33] were administered to assess mood and quality of life, respectively, through interview with a blinded rater in a hospital. The FES-I scores range from 1 to 64, where higher score indicates greater fear of falling. The GDS-SF scores range from 1 to 15, where higher score indicates greater depressive mood.

## Adverse effects assessment

After participants completed home-based exercise for four weeks, we assessed adverse effects based on participants’ report.

## Statistical analyses

A sample size was determined a priori that assumed a two-tailed independent t-test with a of 0.05 and power of 80%. The sample size was determined to be sufficient to detect a difference (d) of 2.9 [34] on the TUG at T1 as a primary outcome measure, with respective standard deviations of 3.7, as calculated from results of

a similar previous study [35]. Using Lehr’s formula (16/(d/r)) [36] and a 20% drop-out rate, we calculated that 68 participants would be needed.

In this study, the primary analysis was based on the intention to treat (ITT) population using the last observation carried forward method (LOCF), which included all randomized patients who completed the HEP for four weeks and underwent baseline measurement immediately after assessments. LOCF is characterized by replacement of individual missing values by the last observed value of that variable. Therefore, values at T2 for 3 participants who were lost to follow up at T2 were replaced by respective values at T1. The safety population included all patients.

SPSS version 24.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA) was used for statistical analyses. The difference at T0 between the two groups was assessed using independent t-test and \chi ^ { 2 } \cdottest. p-Values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. To test the effects of groups across time points we used simple repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with time as the within-patient factor and group (smart-rehab vs. control) as the between-patient factor for the parametric data with a normal distribution. The post hoc t-test was performed with a paired t-test to analyze the changes of parameters in each group. In addition, the improvements of each value at T1 and T2 (�T1 ¼ (value at T1 – value at T0) and �T2 ¼ (value at T2 – value at T0)) were analyzed using independent t-test between the two groups. To correct for multiple comparisons made, p-values less than 0.017 were considered statistically significant with a Bonferroni correction.

# Results
## Participant characteristics

A total of 68 participants (mean age 64.2 ± 12.8 years, 20 females, mean stroke duration 43.3 ± 53.6 days) from two study centers was enrolled in this study. All participants were randomly allocated to the smart-rehab group (n ¼ 35) or control group (n ¼ 33). In the smart-rehab group, six participants discontinued study participation prematurely due to an adverse event (n ¼ 1), difficulty in using the AR system (n ¼ 1), error of the AR system (n ¼ 2), or withdrawal of consent (n ¼ 2). In the control group, six participants discontinued study participation prematurely due to withdrawal of consent (n ¼ 6). The reason to withdraw their consent in both groups was noted to be, ‘Don’t want to exercise.’ Therefore, a total of 12 participants (the smart-rehab group [n ¼ 6] and the control group [n ¼ 6]) had no post-baseline data, and thus were excluded from the ITT analysis set (the smart-rehab group [n ¼ 29] and the control group [n ¼ 27]) (Figure 1).

There were no significant differences in ITT analysis set at baseline in general, stroke, and functional characteristics (Table 1) or in measures of balance function, fear of falling, depressive mood, and quality of life between the smart-rehab group and the control group (Table 2).

## Adverse effects

In the smart-rehab group, a 61 year-old male participant with infratentorial ischemic stroke due to vertebral artery dissection showed a transient ischemic attack (TIA) after the HEP for three weeks. He was enrolled six days after stroke onset and had participated in the HEP with the AR system. After he performed excessive head rotation during a rotation exercise of the trunk, he felt dizzy with impaired balance. He visited the emergency room and was diagnosed with TIA with no interval change of vertebral artery and stroke. After hospitalization for three days, his symptoms subsided and he was discharged home with no significant neurologic sequelae.

The other participants in the smart-rehab group and control group showed no significant adverse effect, such as falls during exercise.
