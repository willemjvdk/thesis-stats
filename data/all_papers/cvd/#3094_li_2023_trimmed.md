## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# Methods and results
This was a randomized controlled trial to test the effects of a digital learning platform on the health knowledge, beliefs, and behaviours of stroke patients from baseline to 6 months after discharge. The control group received routine health education while the intervention group received health belief education during hospitalization and used a digital learning platform for 6 months after discharge. The health knowledge was assessed by The Stroke Health Knowledge Questionnaire, health beliefs by The Short Form Health Belief Model Scale for Stroke Patients, and health behaviours by the Stroke Health Behavior Scale. A total of 90 patients were included: 45 each in the intervention group and the control group, of whom 38 and 37 completed the study, respectively. At 6 months after discharge, (1) the health knowledge score of the intervention group was insignificantly higher than that of the control group, (2) the health belief score of the intervention group was significantly higher than that of the control group, and (3) the intervention group had higher health behaviour scores especially in physical activity than that of the control group. Other health behaviour dimensions have time effect, but not significant.

## Conclusions

The digital learning platform can improve health behaviours of stroke patients 6 months after discharge, especially in physical activity.

# Methods
## Study design

This study was a randomized controlled trial. A total of 90 participants were randomly assigned to either the intervention group or the control group. The participants were recruited from two teaching hospitals in Guangzhou, China. The two hospitals involved in the study are affiliated hospitals of the same university and their treatment modes, specialty levels, and hospital support are similar.

## Participants

The participants were inpatients in the neurology departments of two teaching hospitals in Guangzhou, China, from July 2019 to January 2020. Repeated measurements were collected at 0/3/6 months after discharge. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) cerebral CT- or MRI-confirmed ischemic stroke that conformed to the definition of ischemic stroke in the Chinese Clinical Management Guidelines for Cerebrovascular Diseases 2019,20(2) aged ≥18 years, (3) able to understand the content of the questionnaire and able to write or communicate verbally, (4) had a smartphone, iPad or computer, and (5) provided informed consent and agreed to cooperate with follow-ups at 3 months and 6 months after discharge.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a history of serious liver disease, kidney disease, or malignant tumour; (2) a history of mental illness or psychiatric symptoms; (3) Wernicke’s aphasia; and (4) participation in other health education research projects after discharge.

## Intervention

Participants were randomly divided into two groups: the control group was provided with routine health education and the intervention group used the digital learning platform.

## The routine health education

The control group received routine care during hospitalization, including health education, stroke prevention handouts, and telephone follow-up by nurses at 1 week and 1 month after discharge.18

## The digital learning platform

The intervention group received the following contents: (1) health belief education during the hospitalization and (2) the digital learning platform based on the HBM after discharge.

## Construction of digital learning platform

The intervention program is guided by the health belief theory. The intervention content and measurement tools of this study were determined based on a literature review of the health knowledge, beliefs, and behaviours of stroke patients. The researchers solicited the opinions and suggestions of experts, including neurologists, nurses, professors of internal nursing, and technicians, and repeatedly revised the study protocol to ensure the accuracy and operability of the intervention content. The website is https://prstroke-wxt.kuaizhan.com/. The site is available on both mobile and computer devices.

## Recruitment of staff for intervention

Four neurology nurses were selected as intervention nurses. They were required to have at least a bachelor’s degree and have worked in neurology for more than 10 years. The intervention nurses received training three times, including a lecture (lecture, discussion, and roleplaying), hospital onsite training, and an examination. Intervention nurses needed to pass the examination before they started the intervention.

## Health belief education before discharge

Face-to-face health belief education intervention was carried out by the intervention nurse for 20–30 min 2 days before discharge. The contents included assessing the risk of recurrent stroke, helping patients understand the susceptibility and severity of stroke, recognizing the benefits of healthy behaviours for stroke prevention, assessing barriers to improving health behaviour, identifying the gap between the patient’s existing lifestyle and secondary stroke prevention guidelines, and stimulating the motivation to change behaviour to improve self-efficacy.

## Digital learning platform after discharge

Before discharge, the researchers introduced the functions and use methods of the digital learning platform to patients, ensuring that patients were skilled at logging on to the digital learning platform and watching online learning videos.

After discharge, patients were required to use digital learning platforms, including 4 modules: (1) online health education videos, (2) blood pressure recording, (3) task reminders, and (4) online consultation.

## Telephone follow-ups after discharge

Patients were followed up by telephone twice, at 3 and 6 months after discharge. Telephone follow-ups lasted approximately half an hour.

# Data collection methods
## Demographic and disease-related data

A self-designed questionnaire was used to survey demographic and disease-related data. The demographic information included gender, age, educational level, working conditions, marital status, payment method for medical expenses, household income, and living situation. The disease-related information included first/recurrent stroke, family history of stroke, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidaemia, and muscle strength.

## Stroke Knowledge Questionnaire

The Stroke Knowledge Questionnaire (SKQ) was designed by Wan et al.21 to measure the knowledge of recurrence prevention in stroke patients. The scale includes 10 dimensions and 25 items, including exercise (one item), nutrition (two items), low-salt diet (one item), blood pressure monitoring (two items), smoking cessation (one item), alcohol restriction (one item), medication (three items), stroke risk factors (10 items), stroke aura (three items), and stroke treatment (one entry). Each item is selected as ‘incorrect’, ‘uncertain’, or ‘correct’. Those who answer ‘correct’ receive

1 point and those who answer ‘incorrect’ or ‘uncertain’ receive 0 points. The score of a specific dimension is calculated as the sum of the items in the dimension divided by the possible total score of the dimension multiplied by 100. The total score consists of the score of each of the 10 dimensions. The higher the score is, the higher the level of health knowledge of the stroke patients. The previous study showed that Cronbach’s α of the scale is 0.87 and the CVI was 0.89.21 In this study of 90 participants, Cronbach’s α was 0.8.

## Short Form Health Belief Model Scale for Stroke Patients

The Short Form Health Belief Model Scale for Stroke Patients (SF-HBMS-SP) was developed by Wan et al.22 on the basis of the champion HBM scale23 and the Chinese version of the stroke health belief scale revised by Peng et al.24 The scale has 20 items and 6 dimensions, including disease susceptibility, disease severity, health motivation, self-efficacy, perceived health behaviour benefits, and perceived health behaviour disorders. The scale adopts a Likert grade 5 scoring method and gives 1–5 points,

from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’. The dimension of ‘perceived health behaviour barriers’ is reverse scored. The higher the score is, the higher the patient’s health belief level. The previous study showed that Cronbach’s α of the scale is 0.84 and the CVI was 0.85.22 In this study of 90 participants, Cronbach’s α was 0.87.

## Health Behavior Scale for Stroke Patients

Wan et al. 25 developed the Health Behavior Scale for Stroke Patients (HBS-SP). The scale has 25 items and 7 dimensions, including exercise, medication compliance, nutritional diet, low-salt and low-oil diet, blood pressure monitoring, smoking cessation, and alcohol restriction. This scale investigates the health behaviours of stroke patients in the last month. The scale adopts a Likert grade 4 scoring method and assigns 1–4 points to ‘never’, ‘sometimes’, ‘often’, and ‘always’, respectively. The higher the score is, the better the patient’s healthy behaviour. The previous study showed that Cronbach’s α of the scale is 0.88 and the CVI was 0.85.25 In this study of 90 participants, Cronbach’s α was 0.82.

## Procedures

After each patient was admitted to the hospital, potential enrolment patients were identified by the study coordinators. Random sequencing of group identifiers was performed using SPSS 20.0 software. Group identifiers (1 = intervention group, 2 = control group) were printed on a folded paper and sealed in the same opaque envelope by people not involved in the study. The study coordinators assigned envelopes with corresponding serial numbers. Then, participants were randomly assigned to either an intervention group (n = 45) or a control group (n = 45).

All patients have given informed consent and completed the baseline data. The contents of the questionnaire were explained to each patient before completion. The SKQ and SF-HBMS-SP were completed within 24 h after admission before stroke education. The HBS-SP were completed before intervention during hospitalization. This was a single-blind study. The investigators who measured the results were blinded to the patient allocation. Data entry was completed by individuals with no patient contact.

## Sample size

In this study, health behaviour was used as the primary outcome indicator. According to a previous study,26 the total score of the control group’s Health Behavior Scale for Stroke Patient was 2.75 ± 0.4 and the total score of the intervention group was 3.23 ± 0.35. A two-sided test was performed, α＝0.05, β＝0.2. PASS software was used to calculate the sample size. The sample size of each group was calculated to be 36 participants and a total of 72 participants were needed. Considering the 20% sample loss rate, the minimum sample size was 90. Therefore, a total of 90 samples were planned in this study, with a minimum of 45 samples in each group.

## Ethical

The study was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (protocol ID: ChiCTR1800019936) and conformed to the moral principles of the Helsinki Declaration of 1975. This study was approved by the ethics committees of the two hospitals where the study was conducted [(2018) 02-421-01 and BF2020-030-01]. All patients signed informed consent forms before the study.

## Data analysis

SPSS 20.0 software was used for data processing. Significance level α = 0.05.

The parameters of independent sample tests (χ 2 test) or non-parametric tests (Mann–Whitney U test) were used to compare the differences in sociodemographic data, disease-related data, baseline health knowledge, health beliefs, and health behaviour scores between the two groups.

The primary outcome of the trial was health behaviours. The secondary outcomes were health knowledge and health belief. Multivariate repeated measure ANOVA was used to compare the trend of health behaviour scores of patients at the baseline and 3 and 6 months after discharge and to analyse the main effect and time effect of intervention, as well as the interaction effect between intervention and time. When there was a significant difference in the interaction effect between the two groups, simple effect analysis was used to compare the difference in the scores of health behaviours between the two groups at each time point.

Wilcoxon tests were used to compare the differences in health knowledge and beliefs of patients in the intervention/control groups at the baseline and 6 months after discharge.

# Results
## Participant recruitment and retention

A total of 90 patients were recruited for the study. At 3 months after discharge, 7 participants dropped out, including 1 case that had a recurrence of stroke, 3 participants that declined to be followed up due to a lack of interest or being busy or unwell, and 3 participants that lost contact. At 6 months after discharge, 8 patients declined to be followed up due to a lack of interest or being busy or unwell.

A total of 90 patients were included in the study and divided equally between the intervention and control groups, with 45 patients in each group. The final 38 participants in the intervention group (84.44%) and 37 participants in the control group (82.22%) completed the 6-month follow-up survey after discharge. The main causes of attrition during the study were rejection of follow-up due to a lack of interest or being busy or tired (11 participants) and loss of contact (3 participants). The flow diagram of participants is shown in Figure 1. No significant differences were found between the patients who dropped out of the two groups.

## Demographic and disease-related data

There was no significant difference between the two groups in terms of sociodemographic data or disease-related data. There was no significant difference in the two groups of patients in terms of sociodemographic data, including age, gender, educational level, employment status, marital status, household income, living situation, and payment method of medical expenses ( P > 0 . 0 5 ), as shown in Table 1. There was no significant difference in the disease-related data between the two groups, including stroke type, first/recurrent stroke, family history of stroke, hyperlipidaemia, hypertension, diabetes, and muscle strength. P > 0 . 0 5, as shown in Table 1.

## Implementation of intervention

The mean time of face-to-face health belief education for patients in the intervention group before discharge was 27.33 ± 3.77 min. Participants received 1 study score on the platform after completing watching a health education video or a blood pressure recording. ## Health knowledge and health beliefs

The secondary outcomes were health knowledge and health beliefs, which were measured at baseline (before discharge) and at 6 months after discharge. Before the intervention, there was no significant difference between the intervention group and the control group in the total score of health knowledge and beliefs at the baseline (P > 0.05). The baselines of the two groups are equally comparable.

The total score of health knowledge in the intervention group was higher than that in the control group, with no significant difference at 6 months after discharge ( P > \bar { 0 . 0 5 } )). The total score of health beliefs in the intervention group was significantly higher than that in the control group ( P < 0 . 0 5 )) at 6 months after discharge. The results are shown in Table 2.

## Health behaviour

Health behaviours, as the primary outcome of this study, were measured at baseline (before discharge) and 3 and 6 months after discharge. Before the intervention, there was no significant difference between the intervention group and the control group in the baseline health behaviour total score and all dimensions ( \bar { P } > 0 . \dot { 0 } 5 ). The two groups were equally comparable.

There were statistically significant differences in the main effect of time and the interaction effect between intervention and time ( F =47.858, P < 0 . 0 0 1 ; F = 3 . 8 3 6 , P = 0 . 0 3 2 ). Repeated measure ANOVA showed no significant difference in the main effects of the intervention between the two groups ( F = 1 . 1 6 3 , P = 0 . 2 8 3 ), as shown in Table 3.

ANOVA was used to compare the differences at each time point. The total scores of the health behaviours and physical activity dimensions in the intervention group and the control group at 3 and 6 months after discharge were significantly higher than the baseline level (P < 0.05), as shown in Table 4.
