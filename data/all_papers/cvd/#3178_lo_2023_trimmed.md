# METHODS
## Data Availability

Anonymized data and relevant supporting clinical trial documents are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

## Study Design and Participants

This assessor-blind RCT was approved by the Joint Chinese University of Hong Kong-New Territories East Cluster Clinical Research Ethics Committee (CREC Ref. No.: 2017.660) and the Research Ethics Committee of the respective hospitals (Ref. No.: NTWC/REC/21035; KW/FR-21-079(159- 07)) and registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR1800016101). A study protocol was published previously.21 The trial was reported according to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials statement.

Participants were recruited from 10 public hospitals in Hong Kong (HK); individuals eligible for participation were adults ≥18 years old, diagnosed with ischemic/hemorrhagic first-ever/recurrent stroke, could be enrolled within 12 months of stroke onset, had a montreal cognitive assessment score ≥10, communicable in Cantonese, provided their consent to participate, and able to operate the study device kit. Survivors were excluded if they had severe dysphasia or hearing impairment, which impeded their participation in video/phone calls.

The sample size was calculated using the power analysis software PASS V.13.0 (NCSS, Kaysville, UT), and empirical effect sizes on the primary outcome of SE from previously published systematic reviews on self-management-based interventions for stroke survivors were considered.10–12 As there is no consensus-based minimal clinically important effect size on SE, the lowest bound of the empirical effect sizes of 0.32 was adopted to guide the present sample size estimation. An estimated sample size of 154 eligible participants per arm was preferred as it would give the 2-arm RCT 80% power at a 2-sided 5% level of significance to detect an effect size of at least 0.32 for the primary outcome of SE after the intervention. Allowing for an attrition of 20%,22 a target of 384 stroke survivors (192 per arm) was aimed for recruitment.

## Randomization

After conducting a baseline assessment of consenting eligible participants, they were randomly allocated at a 1:1 ratio to the

intervention group (I), which received the VMSCC service and usual care, or the control group (C), which received usual care only. Block randomization was conducted using a computergenerated random schedule (block size of 12), except the last one. The group allocation sequence was concealed from the outcome assessor using identical, opaque, and sequentially numbered envelopes. A project member opened the envelopes according to the participants’ enrollment time and recorded the participants’ grouping. Blinding of participants, persons delivering the intervention, and conducting interviews with participants was not possible due to the nature of the intervention.

## VMSCC Intervention

The VMSCC intervention was grounded in Bandura’s principles of SE.23 It consisted of the VMSCC service and an online stroke care platform. The VMSCC service was established for the intervention group participants to virtually meet with health care professionals. Monthly video-based consultations were conducted by a registered nurse with each participant and his/ her caregiver for 6 months; each consultation lasted for 30 to 45 minutes in which the nurse discussed with the participant about their recovery and self-management progress and addressed their concerns. Training sessions were provided by the project team to the nurse managing the VMSCC and delivering the intervention.

Monthly telephone calls were scheduled approximately a week before the video consultation session to tailor the upcoming session by addressing additional needs of the participant, and to remind the participant to record their blood pressure (BP) readings and attend the scheduled consultation.

An online platform containing 85 informative videos (duration: 10 hours) in 9 chapters on stroke care and rehabilitation was created in collaboration with a multidisciplinary team of health care professionals (nurses, physicians, Chinese medicine practitioners, pharmacists, physiotherapists, occupational therapists, dieticians, psychologists, social workers, and survivors) to present reliable information aiding in the development of survivors’ self-management skills (https://vmscc.nur. cuhk.edu.hk). The videos cover essential information related to stroke care: nutrition, exercise, medications, community services, peer-sharing, experts’ advice; and videos for enhancing caring skills and confidence, offering support to caregivers. Unlimited access to the online platform was provided to the intervention group participants (Supplemental Material).

Additionally, intervention group participants were provided with, and given training for using, a device kit consisting of a tablet and BP monitoring device to readily access the website and regularly monitor their BP, respectively. Their weekly BP records were shared with the nurse via an application preinstalled on the tablet.

A hotline was created for the intervention group participants to discuss their nonurgent health and psychological concerns with the nurse outside the session times. The nurse would consult the multidisciplinary team of experts, when the participants’ recovery needed additional attention.

## Control

The control group participants received the usual stroke care services offered in the community.

# Outcome Measurements
Data were collected at baseline (T0), and at 3 months (T1) and 6 months (T2) after commencing the intervention (T2: right after the intervention).

# Primary Outcome
## Self-Efficacy

Participants’ SE in performing daily functional activities and self-management was measured by the 13-item Stroke SE Questionnaire (Chinese version).24 Each item was rated on an 11-point scale (0=not at all confident; 10=very confident). High internal consistency was demonstrated (Cronbach’s α=0.92). Convergent validity was supported by its significant correlation with the General SE Scale.24

# Secondary Outcomes
## Self-Management Behaviors

Participants’ satisfaction with their performance of self-management behaviors was measured by the 11-item Stroke Self-Management Behaviors Performance Scale (Chinese version).25 The scale was developed by the investigators based on an extensive literature review and validation in Chinese community-residing survivors. Each item was rated on an 11-point Likert scale (0=very dissatisfied; 10=very satisfied). Acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α=0.65–0.88) and convergent validity were demonstrated.25

## Social Participation

Participants’ level of participation after stroke was measured using the 11-item reintegration to normal living index (Chinese version).26 Each item was rated on a 4-point scale (1=does not describe my situation; 4=fully describes my situation). It has high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α=0.92).26

## Depression

Depressive symptoms were assessed by the Geriatric Depression Scale (Chinese version). On a 30-item scale with yes/no answers, a score of 11 indicates mild depression and 17 or higher indicates severe depression. Correlational analysis revealed the Chinese version of the Geriatric Depression Scale has good convergent validity when assessed against the London Handicap Scale.27

## Statistical Analysis

Normality of variables with continuous data was assessed using skewness statistics and normal probability plots. Participants’ baseline demographic and clinical characteristics and outcome measures across study time points were presented by their means (SDs), medians (interquartile ranges), and frequencies (percentages), as appropriate. A generalized estimating equation (GEE) model was used to compare the differential changes in each outcome at T1 and T2 (3 and 6 months after commencing the intervention, respectively) with respect to T0 (baseline) between the control and intervention groups in which the time effect and baseline group difference on the outcome were adjusted.28 Two group-by-time point interaction terms (Group×T1 and Group×T2) were included in the model to assess the differential changes between groups. Since all the randomized participants had complete baseline outcome data

and had at least 1 episode of the repeated measures outcome data, all were included in the outcome analysis as per their randomly assigned groups, and the intention-to-treat principle was therefore followed in the outcome comparisons between groups. The SPSS built-in quasi-likelihood method, which does not need to impute missing data, was used to handle missing data in the GEE analysis. This method can produce unbiased results for GEE analysis even in the presence of missing data, provided the data are missing completely at random. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 26 (IBM Crop. Armonk, NY) with a level of significance set at 0.05 (2-sided).

## Protocol Deviation

The study was conducted amid the coronavirus pandemic period, posing several challenges to the study and changes were made to the protocol to adjust to these unusual circumstances. This included increasing the number of recruitment sites to 10 for reaching the target, however, due to accessibility and funding limitations, ending the study early upon reaching participants well above the estimated sample size (n=308) but not the target (n=384). Besides, the intermediate data collection time point at 3 months after commencing the intervention (T1) was collected to maintain contact and monitor the progress of the participants.

# RESULTS
## Participant Characteristics

A total of 850 survivors were screened for eligibility from July 2019 to June 2022. Among them, 335 eligible and consenting participants were enrolled in the study at baseline, and 274 (82%) completed the study while 61 dropped out (Figure). The reasons for drop out included refusal to answer questions or unreachability of participants (95%), admission to residential care homes (3%), and death (2%). The baseline characteristics of participants retained and those who dropped out from the study were statistically comparable (all P>0.05; Supplemental Material). The reasons for drop out cases, as shown above, further support the plausibility of data missing completely at random.

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1. Participants were aged between 24 and 88 years (mean=62.3 years, SD=9.8). Most of them were male (61%), married (72%), educated till secondary school or higher (68%), living with family (89%), and not working (65%). Most of the participants had a stroke of ischemic (90%) and first onset (85%), and a history of hypertension (63%). The mean score of the montreal cognitive assessment was 23.0 (SD=4.2), and Modified Barthel Index was 87.0 (SD=12.9). The demographic and clinical characteristics of the 2 groups were generally comparable.

# Outcomes
The descriptive statistics of the outcomes at T0, T1, and T2 and their estimated Hedges’ g effect sizes based

on the change scores from T0 to T2 are presented in Table 2. Participants in the control group, on average, scored higher in the outcome variables at T0 than those in the intervention group.

As the intervention was completed at T2, the interpretations of the GEE analysis results were essentially based on the group×T2 interaction-term, which estimated the additional mean change in the underlying outcome at T2 with respect to T0 in the intervention group compared with the control group. The results of the GEE analyses are shown in Table 3. For SE, participants in the intervention group had a significantly greater increment in the stroke SE questionnaire total score at T2 with respect to T0 than those in the control group (group×T2 regression coefficient, B=4.60 [95% CI, 0.16–9.05]; P=0.042). Regarding self-management behaviors, no significant change in the Stroke Self-Management Behaviors Performance Scale total score was found in the intervention group as compared with the control group (B=3.45 [95% CI, −0.87 to 7.77]; P=0.117). For social participation, participants in the intervention group had a significantly greater increment in the reintegration to normal living index total score at T2 than those in the control group (B=5.07 [95% CI, 0.61–9.53]; P=0.026). Regarding depression level, participants in the intervention group had a significantly greater reduction in the Geriatric Depression Scale total score at T2 than those in the control group (B=−2.33 [95% CI, −4.06 to −0.61]; P=0.008). The effect sizes of the intervention on the outcomes ranged from 0.19 to 0.36 (Table 2), conventionally interpreted as within small to medium.29
