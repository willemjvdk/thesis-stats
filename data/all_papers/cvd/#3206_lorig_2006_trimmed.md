## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# METHODS
Subjects were continually recruited over the course of 18 months by placing links to the study website on established websites and discussion groups, such as Yahoo!Health, WebMD, and SeniorNet.org. In addition there were calendar announcements and articles in newspapers that directed subjects to the study website. After reaching the study site, potential participants read about the study and left their e-mail address so that they could be contacted when we were ready to have them enter the study. Approximately 3 weeks before each workshop, the next 70 to 100 potential subjects were sent e-mails with a link to the study’s informed consent and questionnaire. They first completed the informed consent online and then progressed to the questionnaire. The study, consent form, and consent procedure were reviewed and approved by the Stanford University Administrative Panel for the Protection of Human Subjects. The first page of the questionnaire was screened for eligibility. Those found to be ineligible were thanked and could not progress to the rest of the questionnaire. Approximately 50% of those leaving their name on the waiting list actually completed the informed consent and baseline questionnaire. Figure 1 gives additional details of the recruitment process.

After completion of the online questionnaire, participants were randomized to either the intervention group or to a control group. Control subjects received usual care with no intervention, ie, whatever care they had been receiving or chose to receive after completing the online questionnaire. They were sent a $10 Amazon.com certificate for each questionnaire completed. At the end of the 1-year study, control subjects received the book used in the intervention. Treatment subjects received usual care plus the intervention. Each participant was asked to fill out on-line questionnaires at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months.

## Subjects

Subjects met all of the following criteria: 1) at least 18 years of age; 2) a physician’s diagnosis of heart disease, chronic lung disease or type 2 diabetes; 3) in addition to one of these diagnoses, subject could have other chronic conditions but could not have been in active treatment of cancer for 1 year; 4) not participated in the small-group Chronic Disease Self-Management Program; 5) access to a computer with Internet and email capabilities; 6) agreed to 1–2 hours per week of log on time spread over at least 3 sessions/wk for 6 weeks; 7) are able to complete the online questionnaire. By design, this sample was very heterogeneous, as had been the case with the original small-group Chronic Disease program. Like that program, the online program was intended to be capable of benefiting a broad spectrum of the population with chronic diseases and to be useful where populations of patients may not be large enough to justify more diseasespecific, targeted programs.

# Outcome Measures
There were 7 health-related quality of life measures or health indicators. Visual numeric scales (VNS) were used to measure pain/physical discomfort, shortness of breath, and fatigue.18 These are adaptations of visual analog scales.19 –21 Visual numeric scales have been found to correlate 0.72 with same-worded visual analog scales (VAS), and have a 94% completion rate compared with a 76% rate for the VAS.18 Ranges and desirable directions for all of the variables are included in Tables 1 to 3.

The Illness Intrusiveness Scale measures the impact of disease on role activities such as work, recreation, and social activities.22,23 The Health Distress Scale was adapted from the Medical Outcome Study.24 It focuses on the distress specifically associated with health problems. Self-Rated Global Health comes from the National Health Survey and has been found to be predictive of future health status.25 The 20-item Health Assessment Instrument measures disability and is also used in the National Health Survey.26

Four health-related behaviors were measured: stretching and strengthening exercise, aerobic exercise, use of cognitive symptom management techniques, and use of techniques to improve communication with health care providers. These instruments were developed and validated by the Stanford Patient Education Research Center for use in previous studies.27

Three utilization measures were used: self-reported outpatient visits to physicians, emergency room visits, and

nights in hospital. In a previous study, we found that selfreport of outpatient visits correlated r  0.70 with chart audit data, while days in the hospital correlated r  0.83. Discrepancies were consistent over time and across treatment groups.28

We also measured perceived self-efficacy, which reflects participants’ confidence to manage their disease in general. Readers may access all instruments and descriptions of their psychometric properties at http://patienteducation.stanford.edu/ research/. Table 1 shows ranges and positive directions for each outcome measure where appropriate.

## Additional Variables

Demographic variables included age, gender, years of education, marital status, and ethnicity. We also enumerated the total number of times each treatment participant logged into the class website and how many weeks that they logged on at least once. Finally, all participants were asked how often they visited health-related web sites in the previous 6 months.

## Data Analyses

The randomization was checked using t-tests comparing baseline demographic and outcome variables for the intervention participants versus the usual care controls. The 1-year dropouts (those who failed to complete the 1-year questionnaire) were then compared with those who completed 1-year on all the outcome, disease and demographic variables at baseline, again using t-tests.

Outcome measures with highly skewed distributions and high proportions of zero values (greater than 30%) were dichotomized into none and any. Logistic regression was then

used to determine if there were differences by randomization at 1 year after controlling for the dichotomized baseline value of the outcome and demographic variables (age, sex, education, whether married, and whether non-Hispanic White).

Analyses of covariance (ANCOVA) was used to compare the outcomes at 1 year for variables with more continuous distributions. Demographic variables and the outcome variable at baseline were used as controlling covariates in ANCOVA models where the dependent variables were 1-year outcomes and the factor or class variable was randomization. Leastsquared means (adjusted for covariates) were computed and tested to determine if there were significant differences at 6 months or 1 year. All logistic and ANCOVA analyses were also run with baseline general health as a covariate, as a proxy control for the severity of the chronic illnesses.

Repeated measures analyses of variance were conducted on each outcome to include 6 month data. The direction and significance of the time by randomization interaction was examined. ANCOVA or logistic models also were used for an exploratory comparison of intervention participants to intervention participants in the original small-group Chronic Disease Self-Management Program. Comparisons of the common variables used in the 2 evaluations were made at 1 year. Unlike the current internet-based program, which included people with heart disease, lung disease and type 2 diabetes, the original small-group program included partici-

pants with heart disease, lung disease, and arthritis. For this reason, participants from the internet study with diabetes and from the original small-group study with arthritis were excluded for the purposes of these comparisons. The same analyses also were conducted for the all participants in both studies, irregardless of primary diseases.

In a separate set of analyses, changes in self-efficacy from baseline to 6 months as well baseline self-efficacy were added to the 1-year ANCOVA models for health status outcomes. This allowed us to examine whether baseline self-efficacy or 6-month improvements in self-efficacy were significantly associated with improvements in health status at 1 year. All ANCOVA analyses used the Statistical Analyses System (SAS) General Linear Models procedure.

# RESULTS
## Subjects

Nine hundred fifty-eight subjects with the targeted diseases were recruited. All subjects completed an online informed consent and baseline questionnaire and were immediately randomized, with 501 in the usual care control group and 457 participating in the intervention. Of these, 430 usual care (85.8%) and 353 intervention subjects (77.2%) completed 6-month questionnaires. Nearly the same numbers (426 or 85.0% and 354 or 77.5%) completed 1-year questionnaires. Of those who completed the 1-year questionnaire, 9.1% (71 of 780) had skipped the 6-month questionnaire. See Figure 1 for disposition of study subjects.

## Baseline

Table 1 compares the means at baseline for usual care controls and intervention participants. There were no significant differences for any outcome measures, incidents of diseases or demographics, although there was a trend toward a significant difference for years of education, with the usual care group having slightly more years (15.8 vs. 15.4, P  0.084). As expected, the population of participants was quite diverse (Table 1).

## Dropouts

There were no significant differences in outcome measures at baseline between those who completed 12 months and those who did not complete 12-month questionnaires. Those who dropped were significantly more likely to be men (37% vs. 29% women, P  0.026) and less likely to be non-Hispanic white (81% vs. 88%, P  0.036). As expected, for the treatment participants, there was a significant difference between 12-month completers and dropouts in the mean number of intervention logins (13.9 vs. 26.5, P  0.001).

When the treatment dropouts (n  103) were compared with control dropouts (n  75), there were no significant differences in their outcome variables at baseline. The treatment dropouts had a higher mean education level than the control dropouts (15.8 vs. 14.7 years, P  0.02).

## Participation and Web Usage

Intervention participants logged in an average of 26.5 times (SD  22.8, range of 0 to 177). Eight intervention participants never logged in but completed their 12-month

questionnaire. At each time point– baseline, 6 and 12 months– there were no significant differences between intervention and usual care control participants in the frequency of visits to health-related websites (see Table 1 for baseline means).

# Twelve-Month Outcomes
Five variables had high proportions of zero values (practice stress management, aerobic exercise, stretching and strengthening exercise, emergency room visits, and nights in the hospital) and were dichotomized and evaluated using logistic regression. Other variables with zero values were also tested used logistic regression, and each of the 5 variables was also tested using ANCOVAs. Table 2 indicates which test is reported for each variable.

Of the 7 health indicators, 4 (health distress, fatigue, pain, and shortness of breath) had statistically significant differences with the intervention group showing greater improvement at 1 year ( P < 0 . 0 5, Table 2). All health indicators were in the expected direction, with intervention participants showing better results. Stretching and strengthening exercise, 1 of 4 health behaviors, was statistically significant. The other 3 behaviors were in the hypothesized direction.

Self-efficacy showed a strong trend toward significance ( P \ : = \ : 0 . 0 6 1 )in the hypothesized direction. None of the 3 utilization variables were significant in the hypothesized direction, and Emergency Department visits were significantly less for the control group.

When alternate tests were used (ANCOVAs for the 5 variables where logistic regression was used and logistic regression for other variables with zero values), the results were similar, with the exception that Emergency Department visits was no longer statistically significant. When baseline general health was added as a covariate (to approximate a control for level of disease severity), the results remained basically unchanged.

## Comparison of the Online Program and the Small-Group Program at 1 Year

There were major differences between the recruitment for the original small-group self-management program study5,6 and the current online program. Participants in the original study all came from a number of sites in the San Francisco Bay Area, and recruitment used pubic service announcements, flyers in physicians’ offices and community clinics, posters at senior citizen centers, announcements in HMO newsletters and referrals from county government employers. Programs were held in churches, senior and community centers, public libraries and health care facilities.

There were 153 participants in the original small-group program 136 in the online programs, after eliminating those whose primary chronic diseases were arthritis and diabetes, respectively (Table 3). When we compared the participants in the 2 programs, the original small-group participants were less likely to be men (26% vs. 39%), older (63.3 vs. 59.8), and more likely to be married (70% vs. 59%). They had a higher level of baseline disability and a higher level of aerobic exercise (Table 3, all P \le 0 . 0 5 ).

Participants in the original small-group program had been measured on 4 of the same health indicators, 3 health

behaviors, self efficacy, and 3 utilization variables as were measured for the online evaluation. The participants in the online program were compared at 1 year to the participants in the original small group program using ANCOVA or logistic regression with baseline outcomes, demographic variables and baseline disability as covariates. Using least-squared means, the on-line participants had significantly greater reductions in disability ( P \bar { < } 0 . 0 5 ). There were no differences in health distress, pain or self-reported global health between the 2 programs. Online program participants had significant improvements in stretching and range or motion exercise, while small group participants did significantly better in improving aerobic exercise. There were no significant differences in self-efficacy or medical utilization.

When comparisons were made with all participants in the 2 programs (n  479 and 354 for small-group and on-line, respectively), the results were basically the same except that the change in stretching and strengthening exercise was no longer significantly different between the 2 programs. In addition, changes in pain approached significance ( P = 0 . 0 6 2 )with the online participants doing better.

# Six-Month Changes in Self-Efficacy and One-Year Outcomes
Six months changes in self-efficacy were related to improved health status outcomes at 1 year. In ANCOVA models including baseline health status outcomes, and demographic variables as covariates, baseline self-efficacy and increases in self-efficacy at 6 months were significantly associated with improvements for all 7 health indicators at 1 year: pain, shortness of breath, fatigue, disability, illness intrusiveness, health distress, and global general health at 1 year (all P < 0 . 0 0 1; except 6 month change in shortness of breath, P = 0 . 0 0 7 ). In regression models with the independent variable the one-year health indicator, most of the variance was explained by the baseline health indicator, with overall model r-squares of 0.42 to 0.59. The addition of baseline self-efficacy and 6-month changes in self-efficacy to the regression models resulted in incremental r-square improvements ranging from 0.031 to 0.121.

# Changes in Outcomes
At 1 year, online participants had improvements in health distress, fatigue, pain, and shortness of breath, and a trend toward improvement in illness intrusiveness in comparison to usual care controls. Contrary to our original hypotheses, there were few significant differences in health behaviors at 1 year (only stretching and strengthening exercise) and no significant differences in utilization in the hypothesized directions. Although we had hypothesized significant decreases in utilization based on the findings of the small-group self-management program, similar changes were not found in this study. It is unclear whether this should be considered a failure of the intervention or if there were differences in the appropriate amount of utilization desirable for participants in the 2 different studies.

The exploratory comparison of the 1-year results of the online program with the previous small group program found only minor differences. The online program did at least as well if not better at improving health statuses, suggesting that both programs are effective. This is encouraging as there is a need for effective programs that can be delivered in different ways. The addition of an online program gives people with chronic conditions a choice of delivery modes and thus increases the possibility of increasing the number of people reached by these programs.

As with the original small-group Chronic Disease Self Management Program, improvements in self-efficacy measured within a few months of the completion of the program were associated with improved outcomes at 1 year. Thus improvements in self-efficacy appear to be associated with favorable outcomes in the internet-based Chronic Disease Self Management Program. In the future, a study of the mediating effects of self-efficacy between the self-management programs and health statuses should prove informative.

These are long-term nonreinforced changes and, although significant, are modest. The question arises, do they have clinical meaning? It should be noted that the population is very heterogeneous for disease, age, education and symptom distribution. Thus group changes and mean effect sizes tend to be modest. When we looked at how many intervention participants had improvements of at least 0.3 effect sizes among the 7 health status indicators, 81% had 2 or more, and 59% had 3 or more improvements. Sixty-nine percent had 2 or more health indicators improve by at least 0.4 effect sizes. The differences between intervention and control groups were statistically significant using \chi ^ { 2 } ~ ( P ~ = ~ 0 . 0 2 8for 0.4 effect size improvements).

Based on e-mails, letters to physicians, and messages on the discussion board, we believe that most of participants were enthusiastic about the intervention, and many reported important personal changes. This was an early attempt at a new self-management delivery mode. It is our hope that the learnings from this trial, when applied to future interventions, will bring stronger results.

## Limitations

The online intervention is limited to those who are computer literate and who have access to the Internet. At the same time the use of computers allows for participation by people who cannot or will not attend a small-group program. In addition, as computers and the Internet become more accessible, this form of education will be available to a larger population.

We do not know whether potential participants who were randomized to controls continued their usual care in the same manner as before they were randomized. We do know that they continued to access health-related websites at the same rate as the intervention participants. It is quite likely that many of the internet-literate usual care controls, who had self-selected themselves by volunteering for the program, actively continued to seek out information and/or programs to help themselves deal with their chronic conditions.

The comparison of the earlier small group program with the internet-based program cannot be considered con-

clusive but only suggestive. For this reason we referred to the comparison as exploratory. There were differences in recruitment methods, disease frequencies, computer literacy and other factors. However the question is not which program is better, but rather are the 2 programs similar in their efficacy, which they appear to be. This is important as they provide patients with additional means to access self-management support.

## SUMMARY

The Internet-based Chronic Disease Self-Management Program is based on a similar small-group program. Both programs appeared to be beneficial to participants with some differences. At 1 year, online participants, when compared with randomized controls, had significant improvements in health status. The Internet-based Chronic Disease Self-Management Program, like its predecessor small-group program, appears to be effective in slowing or reducing the negative effects of chronic disease over a 1-year period of time. Both programs should be considered for assisting patients living with chronic conditions.
