## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# 2. Methods
The RENEWING HEALTH project, part of the European Competitiveness Innovation Framework Programme ICT Policy Support Programme (CIP ICT PSP), was carried out between October 2011 and May 2014 in 9 European regions. The project aimed to validate - in real life settings and with rigorous assessment methodology - the use of telemedicine systems for the remote monitoring of patients with chronic disease, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or diabetes mellitus, older people with multiple morbidities, patients with implantable defibrillators and those with chronic heart failure.

In this manuscript we report the results of a trial on the telemonitoring service in patients with chronic heart failure.

The ethics committees of participating centers approved the study and all participants gave formal written consent to enrolment after being informed about the nature and the objectives of the study.

## 2.1. Study design

The study should have been performed in the Veneto Region (Italy) and the Region of Central Greece with identical inclusion/exclusion criteria and the same dataset.

The inclusion criteria were: discharge from hospital after acute heart failure in the previous three months and age ≥65 plus left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) b40% or EF N40% plus BNP N400 (or NT-proBNP N1500) during hospitalisation.

The exclusion criteria were: the presence of severe co-morbidities with life expectation b12 months, inability to use the telehealth equipment, myocardial infarction or percutaneous coronary intervention in the previous three months, cardiac surgery performed in the previous 6 months or being scheduled for these interventions, being on waiting list for heart transplantation or enrolment in another trial.

The primary end-point of the study was the combined occurrence of 12-month all-cause mortality or at least one hospitalisation for HF.

The secondary end-points were: 12-month all cause mortality, number of hospitalisations for all causes and for heart failure, duration of hospitalisations, number of scheduled and urgent outpatient controls and health related quality of life as measured by SF-36 v2 [21].

The analysis was performed on the intention-to-treat model for the primary and secondary end-points. Quality of life assessment was performed only for patients that provided these data.

As the cross-over from the remote monitoring to the usual care group was higher than expected, an additional analysis on the ontreatment model was performed comparing the patients that ultimately received the allocated RM to all other patients, regardless of initial randomization.

Due to the economic crisis in Greece, enrolment was interrupted there and we report the results from the Veneto Region for patients discharged from seven Cardiology and one Internal Medicine Departments.

## 2.2. Statistical analysis

The sample size needed to demonstrate a clinically relevant benefit of telemonitoring was powered on the assumption that patients would be enrolled in both Italy and Greece.

The estimate of the potential impact of telemonitoring on clinical outcome was based on the data published in a Cochrane metaanalysis [10]: 6-month mortality (15–17%) was reduced by 33% by RM and the proportion of patients with rehospitalisations for heart failure (29%) reduced by 24%. Based on these data, but considering the large variability of patients characteristics in different studies considered in this metanalysis, we assumed a 12-month incidence of 35% for the primary end-point (all-causes mortality or at least one rehospitalisation for heart failure) in the usual care group (UC) and a 35% relative reduction of the primary end-point in RM group.

Based on these assumption, in a 2:1 randomisation scheme, a statistical power of 0.80 and a type I error rate of 0.05, the total sample needed was at least of 284 patients for the RM group and 142 for the UC group. This was increased to 310 (210 in Italy and 100 in Greece) and 155 patients (105 in Italy and 50 in Greece), in RM and UC group respectively, assuming a 10% dropout rate.

The algorithm used for the sample size calculation is described by Fleiss et al. [22] and the formula for equal sample sizes was derived by Casagrande et al. [23], which can be generalised to the case of unequal sample sizes (in this case 2:1 proportion).

The primary and some secondary outcomes (mortality, hospitalisations, patients with at least one hospitalisation for all causes or HF, patients with at least one urgent evaluation) were analysed using the chi-squared test. For all other outcomes, including SF36, the sample size was large enough to use the Central Limit Theorem's normal approximation and the t-test to analyse difference between groups was used.

SF36's values at 12 months were calculated controlling for basal values by ANCOVA, because of a significant difference between groups in baseline data.

Confidence intervals are at 95% level and p-values minor than 0.05 are considered significant. All analyses were performed by R software (version 3.2.2).

## 2.3. Quality of life assessment

The Italian version of the SF36v2 Health Survey questionnaire was used to assess the health related Quality of Life (QOL) of the enrolled patients [21].

The SF36v2 covers both mental and physical health aspects on eight domain scales and two summary measures, the Physical Component Summary (PCS) and the Mental Component Summary (MCS). Scores above 55 of the two components summary indicate little or no measurable physical limitations, disabilities or decrements in well-being and/or good general health. Scores between 45 and 55 are considered to be in average range for individual respondents. Scores under 45 for the PCS indicate limitations due to physical problems, a high degree of bodily pain and/or poor general health. Scores under 45 for the MCS indicate psychological and social disability due to emotional problems.

In our study the questionnaire was submitted through phone interview twice, at enrolment and after 12 months.

## 2.4. Randomization process

After signing the informed consent form, patients were assigned a unique ID to ensure confidentiality and protect their privacy.

Randomization was managed through an online system that allocated each ID to either the intervention or control group, according to the randomization table in PASS 2008.

## 2.5. Usual care and remote monitoring service characteristics

## 2.5.1. Usual care

Patients in the UC group received the treatments and outpatient controls as prescribed at the participating hospitals. In general, routine outpatient visits were scheduled after the first month from discharge. If the patients' condition remained stable, following visits were usually scheduled at three or six months, and after one year. Patients could also approach the outpatient or emergency departments of the hospital whenever they became symptomatic. In addition, the telecare service, routinely offered to these patients in the Veneto Region for the detection of home emergencies, was also provided. All patients received the personal alarm device that could be activated in case of sudden worsening of health condition or home accident; the device sends an alarm to the eHealth Centre where operators manage the emergency by putting the patient in touch with the Emergency Department (ED) or Social Services.

## 2.6. Telemonitoring service

In the RM group, patients were equipped with a Personal Health System, composed of a wearable Wrist Clinic device and a digital weight

scale for clinical data collection. The delivery of the devices and monitoring of the data were performed by a single Regional Center for all patients enrolled at the eight participating hospitals.

When consigning the device at the patient's home, the Regional Center's operator gave the instructions for the appropriate use of the equipment. The patients, either on their own or with the help of a caregiver, measured their heart rate, blood pressure, 1-lead ECG and pulse-oximetry with the Wrist-Clinic device, and their weight with the digital weight scale. These data were transmitted on a daily basis, 5 days/week, to the eHealth regional center, and were processed automatically in order to identify potential alarm values based on a personalised telemonitoring plan, set up by the clinician at enrolment. The operators verified the alarms and, if necessary, informed the clinicians on an elective basis.

After notification by the Center's operator, clinicians accessed the Home Care portal to check the patient's data, and took the appropriate actions as guided by their clinical judgment.

This service was not designed for emergencies and in the case of acute clinical worsening, patients and their caregivers were instructed to contact their physicians or emergency department or to activate the alarm device.

# 3. Results
Out of 419 assessed patients, 339 were randomized, 229 to Remote Monitoring (RM) and 110 to Usual Care (UC). In the intervention

group, the RM devices were delivered to 190/229 (83%) patients after a median of 33 (IQR 19.3–64) days. The reasons for protocol deviation were: death ( n = 1 4 ) ,patient's or relatives preference (n = 17), technical difficulties (n = 6) and transfer to nursing home (n = 2). The baseline clinical and demographic characteristics are detailed in Table 1: the two groups were well balanced except for the presence of diabetes mellitus (more frequent in the RM group) and a trend for higher heart rate and presence of atrial fibrillation in UC group. In the RM and UC groups respectively, 19.2 and 14.5% ( p = 0 . 4 6 )of participants had had an ICD previously implanted. Biochemistry data are summarized in Table 1 and were similar in the two groups. BNP or NTproBNP were available for 249/339 patients (73.4%) with similar values in the two groups. At least one echocardiogram was performed during hospitalisation in 84.7% and 85.5% (p = 0.99) and the median left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) was 39.1 ± 12.9 and 3 9 . 1 \pm 1 4 . 6 ( p =0.78) in the RM and UC groups respectively, with severe EF reduction (b35%) present in 36.2% and 43.4% of patients in the RM and control group respectively. The pharmacotherapies at enrolment, described in Table 1, were similar in both groups, except for diuretics which were more frequently prescribed in the RM group. After 12 months, pharmacotherapy use was similar to enrolment in both groups: diuretics in 97.7% and 95.2% ( p = 0 . 4 4 ), aldosterone antagonists in 44.3% and 52.4% ( p = 0 . 6 0 ), ACE-inhibitors in 62.6% and 54.0% (p = 0.19), angiotensin receptor blockers in 16.0% and 14.3% (p = 0.78), beta-blockers in 77.1% and 68.3% ( p = 0 . 3 3 ), digitalis in 20.6% and 30.2% (p = 0.22) and anticoagulants in 53.4% and 63.5% (p = 0.27), in the RM and UC group respectively.

In the RM group, during the 365 days after enrolment, 58,403 alarms were managed by the eHealth regional center: 8052 were considered to have minor and 1973 major clinical relevance and were medically managed.

All interventions were performed by the e-health center personnel and/or local physicians by telephone contacts or scheduling an outpatient visit.

## 3.1. Primary end-point

In the intention-to-treat analysis the primary end-point occurred in 44.1% and 46.4% (p = 0.78) of patients in the RM and UC groups, respectively (Table 2), with no difference in all-cause mortality (24.0% vs 21.8%, p = 0 . 0 9 7 )or the rate of patients with at least one hospital readmission for HF (34.5% vs 39.1%, p = 0.48). In 174 and 86 patients

alive after 12 months, in the RM and UC groups respectively, at least one hospital readmission for HF occurred in 26.4% and 31.4% (p = 0.49).

As 39 patients (17.0%) randomized to RM did not receive the allocated treatment, an additional per protocol on-treatment analysis was carried out (Table 2) which found a trend for reduction in the primary-end point in the RM group (40.0% vs 51.0%, p = 0.055). In 154 and 106 patients alive after 12-months, at least one hospital readmission for HF occurred in 26% and 31,1% (p = 0.44), in RM and UC groups respectively.

## 3.2. Secondary end-points

As summarized in Table 3, the intention-to-treat analysis found no difference in all-cause mortality, in the number and durations of hospital admissions, and in the number of urgent evaluations in emergency departments in the two groups.

Overall, 340 and 166 all-cause hospital admissions occurred in the RM and UC group respectively, with a mean length of stay of 13.1 ± 16.3 and 16.5 ± 32.0 (p = 0.21) days. Hospital admissions for HF occurred in 161 and 93, with a mean length of stay of 1 3 . 5 \pm 1 4 . 2and 1 9 . 0 \pm 3 9 . 3 ( p = 0 . 2 0 )days, in the RM and UC group respectively.

Quality of life assessment (Table 4) was available for 61.6% and 57.3% of patients in the RM and UC groups respectively (i.e. those that completed the questionnaires at both enrolment and after 12 months). In the RM group a significantly better outcome in physical and mental status was reported as compared with the UC group: after 12 months the mean basal PCS score decreased by 1.4 points in the RM group compared to 4.1 points (p b 0.0001) in the UC group and the mean basal MCS score in the RM group increased by 1.3 points compared to a fall of 0.4 points (p = 0.04) in the UC group.
