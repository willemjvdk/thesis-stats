## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# Methods
Software was developed to collect patient data on a PDA, and subsequently transmit the information wirelessly to a server for storage and analysis. The system was designed to communicate with each patient, asking simple questions about their symptoms and giving information and counselling through visual and audible reminders.

## Telemonitoring

The telemonitoring technology comprised a patient frontend, a medical front-end and a web-based system for assisting with clinical decisions. The patient front-end operated through a PDA given to each patient leaving hospital. The cardiologist decided what variables should be followed up (e.g. heart rate, body weight, blood pressure, ECG) and the frequency of monitoring (e.g. daily for blood pressure and body weight, weekly for the ECG) according to the patient’s clinical characteristics.

After system initialization, patients were asked to transmit the requested information at a scheduled time. A pre-set acoustic alarm reminded them of the timing. The PDA was also structured for remote transmission of ECG and blood pressure data collected with a commercial device.15 Finally, the software included questionnaires or visual scales for the monthly testing of anxiety and depression.16,17 After each transmission, the information was stored on a server for subsequent analysis.

At a pre-scheduled time, the cardiologist analysed the information received from each patient, evaluated their clinical priority and could call the patient or send a text message (SMS) to modify the therapy or recommend a clinical consultation.

## Study

A randomized, parallel open study was conducted on patients with chronic heart failure leaving hospital after being treated for clinical instability. No sample size calculation was performed. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are shown in Table 1. The study was approved by the

appropriate ethics committee. Therapy at enrolment followed European guidelines.18 Patients were randomized 1:1 either to an Integrated Management group or to a Usual Care group, using a computerised random number generator.

Before discharge, all patients completed an educational session with a specialised nurse regarding the everyday management of their disease (drugs, relevant signs and symptoms, lifestyle). The patients’ General Practitioners (GPs) were sent a detailed clinical report.

## Data collection

At baseline (time T0), all patients underwent a 12-lead electrocardiogram, a 2-D echocardiograph with colour Doppler scanning, a cardiopulmonary test if feasible and measurement of plasma BNP. In both groups, clinical evaluation was repeated 3, 6, 9 and 12 months afterwards (T1, T2, T3 and T4 respectively, Table 2). Echocardiography was performed on visits T2 and T4. At all evaluations from T0 to T4, adherence to the drugs prescribed was assessed using a validated scale.19

## Home-monitoring and home-care protocol

In the Usual Care group, patients were discharged with appointments for follow-up every three months at our Heart Failure clinic, in accordance with guidelines for fragile patients.20,21 In the Integrated Management group, patients and their caregivers had specific training in the use of the dedicated PDA described above. Each day, the PDA acted as a reminder of the correct timing for the pills. At a predefined time patients were asked to send their body weight, blood pressure and heart rate data via the PDA. In some cases patients were asked to monitor their diuresis. Each month, a psychological assessment was performed through the PDA software about anxiety (STAI-6; Spielberger’s State Trait Anxiety Inventory,17 depression (PHQ-9; Patient Health Questionnaire)18 and perceived well being (PGWBI; Perception of General Well-Being Inventory).22 In addition, the psychologist was available for counselling during each follow-up visit. The patients’ clinical conditions and their compliance to prescribed medications was checked daily through automatic questionnaires (using the Morisky scale for compliance to treatment) and direct questions by the staff if needed. The cardiologist could modify the treatment and suggest further evaluations or tests as required.

Telemonitoring was active from 09:00 to 17:00, from Monday to Friday. Messages were received by a staff operator and checked by a nurse. We made clear to the patients and to their caregivers that the system was not meant to provide an emergency service. In the project, GPs were not involved directly; rather, they were informed of their patients’ acceptance of the protocol, of clinical information gathered at clinical evaluations from T0 to T4, and of any change in medical treatment.

## Adverse events

Cardiovascular death or hospitalization for heart failure lasting more than 3 days were considered major adverse effects. We considered the following as minor adverse effects: (1) any hospital stay of less than three days, including Emergency Department visits; (2) any unplanned investigation and/or blood testing; (3) any unplanned clinical visit.

## Economic evaluation

We calculated the following:

1. fixed costs, such as for starting Integrated Management (electronic equipment such as PDAs, software, telephone line, monitoring equipment for patients such as scales, blood pressure and ECG monitors) and for the medical and non-medical personnel

2. variable costs, such as fees for visits, hospital admissions, blood tests, investigations.

These costs were attributed to the two study groups in Euros.

## Statistics

Analysis was performed using a standard package (SPSS 11.5). In the Integrated Management group, we analysed data from patients who sent at least one set of data by PDA. We compared values at baseline (T0) and at one year follow-up (T4) by t-test (within the same group) and by one-way analysis of variance (between groups). Categorical variables were compared using the chi-squared test with Yates’ correction.

# Results
Overall, 105 patients were screened, 80 agreed to participate and were randomized to the two groups (40 in each). The two groups did not differ significantly in demographic, clinical, echocardiographic or neurohumoral characteristics (Table 3).

The PDA was well accepted by the patients and their caregivers: the system was easy to use, its instruction were straightforward and the telephone line was always available. A toll-free number was available on weekdays, connecting the patients with their reference centre. Overall, patients learned to use the system correctly (more than 80% of the planned contacts were performed). Only one patient dropped out after a few days.

# Clinical results
The clinical and echographic variables at baseline and at the end of follow-up (T4) in the two groups of patients are summarised in Table 3. There was a significant improvement in NHYA class in both groups. There was a significantly greater improvement in the Integrated Management group. In these patients only, there was a significant increase in left ventricular ejection fraction and a reduction in estimated pulmonary pressure at echocardiography. BNP levels were lower at T4 compared to T0 in the Integrated Management group (-36%, P < 0.05), while they did not change significantly in the Usual Care group. Patients’ compliance was higher at T4 than at T0 in both groups, but highest in the Integrated Management group. The effect of the two treatment strategies on major and minor adverse events is summarised in Table 4. There was a significant reduction in major adverse events in the Integrated Management group.

## Psychological symptoms

At baseline, patients showed psychological distress, such as anxiety, mild depression and a low perceived well being (see Table 1), but there were no differences between groups. At follow-up, in the Integrated Management group, there was a significant reduction in depressive symptoms (-36%, P < 0.0001), anxiety score (-38%, P < 0.0001) and an increase in the perceived well-being score (PGWBI þ 23%, P < 0.0001), see Table 3 In contrast, in the Usual Care group, there was a slight worsening in depressive symptoms (-16%, P < 0.01) and in the perceived well-being score (PGWBI -11%, P < 0.01).

## Pharmacological treatment

The process of titration was performed according to current guidelines.18 In the Integrated Management group, the daily analysis of variables by the cardiologist allowed a better and faster titration, while patients in the Usual Care group were seen only during 3-monthly visits, and the enforcing effect on compliance was less relevant. At each 3-month visit, we expressed for each drug the dose administered to the patient as a percentage of its target dose. A gradual, significant drug titration for ATIIA/ ACEI and beta blockers was obtained only in patients of the Integrated Management group, while in the Usual Care group there was a gradual reduction in the dosages of these drugs (significant at T4), see Table 5. In contrast, the average dose of diuretics was reduced in the two groups at follow-up: the average dose was slightly higher

in the Integrated Management group, probably because the daily monitoring resulted in a more aggressive approach.

## Adverse events

The mortality at one year was 18% (14 deaths: 9 in the Usual Care and 5 in the Integrated Management group). There was no significant difference between the groups, Table 4. However, in the Integrated Management group there was a significantly lower incidence of hospitalizations (12 vs 23; P < 0.03), and a significant reduction in the composite end-point of mortality and hospitalizations (17 vs. 32, P < 0.04) (Table 4). Both kinds of adverse events were more frequent in the first 6 months of the study (P < 0.05).

In the Usual Care group, there was a lower incidence of minor adverse effects compared to the Integrated Management group (32 vs. 41, P < 0.05). In the Integrated Management group, the most frequent minor adverse event was an unscheduled visit (35 vs 15, P < 0.01). In the Usual Care group, unscheduled visits were requested by the GP suspecting a worsening in clinical condition, while in the Integrated Management group they were mostly requested by the cardiologist, when the analysis of the data transmitted by the PDA suggested an impending deterioration. On the other hand, Emergency Department admissions for worsening heart failure were more frequent in the Usual Care group: there were 17 admissions, of which 7 were coded as severe events, 4 as minor events, 2 as mild events and 4 were uncoded (P < 0.02). No differences were found between groups regarding other end-points such as ICD implantation/ revision, syncope, need for cardiac rehabilitation, need for cardiac surgery or hypertensive emergency (see Table 6).

## Cost analysis

Integrated Management was more expensive than Usual Care, by 65% if the service provided was taken into account (Table 7). There was a 42% cost reduction for adverse events cost (minor plus major events). Integrated Management allowed a 45% reduction in the costs for major adverse events compared to Usual Care

(2756 Euro/patient vs. 4976 Euro/patient, P < 0.05), while the cost for minor adverse events was 50% less in the Usual Care group than in the Integrated Management group (105 Euro/patient vs. 209 Euro/patient, P < 0.05).

# Clinical and psychological results
Clinical management of heart failure patients in everyday practice differs from what is recommended by international guidelines. For patients at high risk of relapse and those in NYHA class III and IV, most papers suggest a referral to a heart failure outpatient clinic managed by dedicated cardiologists,10 where a stricter adherence to recommended treatments improves prognosis.23,24 This is why for the Usual Care group we chose the best care recommended by guidelines, i.e. referral to our heart failure clinic. To confirm the correctness of our choice, the clinical status of these patients was stable at follow-up and their mortality was lower than that reported in the literature.24,25 At follow-up, patients in the Integrated Management group showed higher adherence, better clinical and psychological status, recovery in indices of prognostic relevance such as ventricular function and BNP levels, and reduction in hospitalizations for heart failure. We attribute these favourable results to the daily

monitoring of vital signs and compliance, the weekly check of psychological well-being and the availability of unscheduled evaluation without a previous appointment.

## Cost-effectiveness

According to some previous studies, telemonitoring reduces the cost of patient management by 20–30%.26,27 However, these figures often do not include the extra expenses due to technical supplies, personnel, unscheduled visits and clinical tests. Although crude, the present analysis showed that the cost of the patients in the Integrated Management group was lower (reduction of serious events), but the cost of the service was increased compared to that of standard care, leading to an overall greater cost for this model of care. This was not unexpected, due to the need to develop and implement the whole system and train the personnel. Had the study included a greater number of patients, the increased saving due to the reduction in hospitalization would probably have outweighed the cost of the service. In patients with more severe disease like those we studied, telemonitoring is cost-effective in the long run.28 However, the data presented here do not allow any definite conclusions on the cost/effectiveness of our model of care.

## Telemonitoring/telecare models

The goal of disease management is to reduce the operating cost without lowering effectiveness, by creating diseasespecific pathways of diagnosis and care. In heart failure, a key matter is the definition of the personnel involved, of their level of expertise and of which patients should and could be treated.8,9 Thus, cooperation and information exchange among heart failure specialists, dedicated nurses and GPs is mandatory: a patient should be followed, according to his/her clinical status, by any of these caregivers at different times.27 In general, a network of telenursing for heart failure could help the GP, allowing

regular exchange of clinical information and consulting with a heart failure centre.13,14 In the present study, we did not involve the patients’ GPs, on the assumption that hospital-centred telemonitoring was most useful soon after hospital discharge in fragile patients.15 Indeed, most events took place within six months of discharge.3 Later in time or in less severe patients, home-centred telemonitoring involving both specialized nurses and GPs would be more appropriate, using the cardiologist only as a consultant: this is currently being trialled in some Italian areas with favourable results.29

There are two further considerations. First, telemonitoring does not imply telecare: this may explain the failure of ambitious, multicentre studies10,11 and the better results of local experiences, where patients are likely to benefit from individual interaction with dedicated, well-trained and specialized care providers.7,8,13 Indeed, some data show that telemonitoring following predetermined and rigid flowcharts is costly and poorly effective.11 Second, a range of different variables has been used in telemonitoring: remote checking of bodyweight, blood pressure, heart rate, even natriuretic peptides, have all been tested to prevent hospital admissions.9,11 In the future, researchers should therefore explore which variables are cost-effective to analyse, to help nurses and physicians in providing telecare for their patients.
