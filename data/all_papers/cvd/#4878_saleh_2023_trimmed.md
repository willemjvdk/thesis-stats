## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# Methods
The study procedures and findings were reported according to Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials guidelines.22

## Design

An experimental 2-group design was used to determine whether an 8-week home-based mobile health app intervention affected physical activity levels, HF symptoms, and health-related quality of life in patients with HF. Participants in the intervention group completed questionnaires on HF symptoms and health-related quality of life at 3 time points: 2 baseline measurements that were 2 weeks apart (a run-in baseline and a preintervention baseline) and immediately post the 8- week intervention. A mobile health app was used to record physical activity levels between the 2 baseline time points and between the second baseline time point and postintervention. Participants in the usual care group underwent the same assessments as the intervention group. The assessment was conducted 2 weeks before the intervention to determine whether using the mobile health app among all participants would have an impact on their physical activity levels and other study outcomes. The effects of the intervention were determined during the intervention for physical activity levels and immediately after the 8-week intervention for HF symptoms and health-related quality of life. Sociodemographic, clinical, and other variables that can affect study outcomes were collected at baseline.

## Sample and Setting

G*Power software was used to determine the number of participants needed to conduct repeated-measures F test within factors, expecting a medium effect size of 0.40, with α set a priori at .05, and with power set at 0.80.23 The optimal sample size was determined to be 127 participants. With an expectation of 20% attrition based on previous studies, the total number of subjects who were sought for recruitment was 152.14,15

For this study, a nonprobability convenience sampling design was used. All patients with HF in New York Heart Association functional classes I to III attending outpatient cardiologist clinics located in the 3 biggest cities in central and north Jordan were eligible to participate in this study. Despite patients in New York Heart Association functional class (I) having no or limited impact of symptoms on their activities of daily living, most of them still experience a reduced functional capacity and quality of life.8,24 The diagnosis and etiology of HF were confirmed by a cardiologist using established criteria: (a) signs and/or symptoms, (b) risk factors, and (c) abnormal electrocardiogram, (d) B-type natriuretic peptide greater than 35 pg/mL, and/or (e) abnormal echocardiography.25

Other inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) having a smartphone, (b) being on a stable medication regimen for at least the previous 3 months of the recruitment, (c) having not been referred for heart transplantation or coronary artery bypass graft surgery, and (d) having the ability to read and speak Arabic. Patients were excluded if they (a) had a medical recommendation of bed rest related to health conditions such as terminal lung, liver, or kidney diseases; (b) were enrolled in a gym program; (c) were rehospitalized within the previous 3 months in relation to cardiovascular disease; and (d) had a coexisting illness known to be associated with a physical disability or weight loss.

## Independent Variable

## Participant characteristics

To characterize participants and obtain data on potential confounding variables, information concerning the following variables was collected by face-to-face interview and medical chart review: age, sex (male or female), marital status (single, married, widowed, or divorced) and whether the participant lived alone (yes or no), education level (high school or less, or greater than high school), employment status (employed or unemployed), health insurance status (insured or uninsured), body mass index, comorbidity, and prescribed medication regimen. Heart failure history included most recent ejection fraction, time since diagnosis, and the etiology of HF (ischemic or nonischemic).

Body mass index is known to be directly associated with the severity of HF symptoms and paradoxically with longer survival.26,27 Body mass index was defined as the ratio between body weight measured to the nearest 0.1 kg and height measured to the nearest 0.01 meter.2 All measurements were conducted on participants wearing light clothing without shoes using a digital weight scale and a portable stadiometer height by a trained research assistant.28

Comorbidity was assessed using the Charlson Comorbidity Index.29 This instrument uses a standardized checklist of 17 underlying health conditions. Each condition is weighed on a scale ranging from 1 to 6 based on the risk for mortality. As our study sample had HF, the sum of all weights could range from 1 to 34; higher scores indicate greater comorbidity burdens. Reliability was established, and predictive validity was demonstrated by the ability of the comorbidity scores to predict mortality, complications, healthcare resource use, and length of hospital stay.29–31

# Outcome Variables
## Physical activity

We considered the outcome variable as the average daily step count per week during the 8-week intervention in comparison with baseline and participants in the usual care group. The daily steps were determined using the Samsung mobile health app that supports the Arabic language and is preinstalled in Galaxy smartphones and freely available for all smartphones with Apple iOS or Android operating systems. This app was designed to provide long-term records about some aspects of the performance of certain health behaviors.32,33 This includes detecting the motion of body parts during physical activities and counting these as steps automatically, without user action to start or stop the app.34,35 These features have benefits in terms of providing data on participants' physical activity behavioral change, reducing the time and effort in participant follow-up, and decreasing dropout rate.36 Moreover, the Samsung mobile health app has interactive features, such as establishing goal setting for daily physical activity level, giving feedback on goal achievement, and sharing achievements in social media, which when combined could substantially motivate change in physical activity behavior.20,36 All participants were instructed to hold the mobile phone in the pant pocket during the activities of daily living and purposeful physical activities to avoid postural measurement errors.34,35,37 Daily step counts for the participants in both groups were obtained from screenshots sent by participants at the end of the week. Mobile health apps are considered an accurate tool for measuring daily physical activity level.34,35,37

## Symptom burden

Heart failure symptom burden was measured using the Memorial Symptom Assessment Scale–Heart Failure.4

The instrument was translated into Arabic by the principal investigator. The accuracy of translation was checked through back-translation by a healthcare provider and professional translator. The instrument consists of 32 items reflecting 3 aspects of HF symptoms: psychological (6 items), physical (21 items), and HFspecific (5 items) symptoms. A statement precedes the items asking about the patient's experience of the symptom during the previous 7 days. If present, each symptom is assessed on 3 scales representing the frequency of symptoms ranging from 1 to 4 (rarely to almost constantly), the severity of symptoms ranging from 1 to 4 (mild to acute), and the degree of symptom distress ranging from 0 to 4 (not at all to very severe). The 3 scores are summed to obtain a burden score for each symptom. A higher total score indicates greater symptom frequency, severity, and distress.

The ultimate goals of HF management are to prevent and control the symptoms of dyspnea, edema, and fatigue.1,3,4 Therefore, the burden score for each of these symptoms was compared between time points to test the effectiveness of the intervention. Moreover, we used the total psychological, physical, and HF-specific symptom subscale scores. Internal consistency reliability values for psychological, physical, and HF-specific symptom subscales were estimated to be 0.92, 0.83, and 0.87, respectively, in patients with HF.4 In the current study, Cronbach α coefficients were 0.91, 0.87, and 0.90, respectively.

## Health-related quality of life

Health-related quality of life was assessed using a health-specific instrument, the Arabic version of the 36-Item Short-Form Health Survey (SF-36). The SF-36 is one of the most commonly used instruments in patients with HF because it is available free of charge, short, easily understood, and simple to score.38–40 The instrument uses 36 questions to assess 8 dimensions of the patients' perception of how the current health problem impacts their functioning in everyday life. The 8 dimensions focus on general health perceptions; current physical, social, and psychological functioning; and occupational and social role limitations due to physical or psychological problems. Each of the 36 questions is scored on a range of 0 (lowest level of functioning) to 100 (no impairment). The 36-item scores are summed and averaged, with higher scores indicating a better health-related quality of life. The validity and reliability of the SF-36 have been established in a wide range of medical conditions including HF.38–40 The SF-36 has shown an ability to discriminate among New York Heart Association functional class groups and predict hospitalization or mortality.41–43 In the current study, Cronbach α for internal consistency reliability ranged between 0.82 and 0.91 across the 8 subscales.

## Procedure

Permission for this study was obtained from the University of Jordan Institutional Review Board. Participants were referred to this project by the cardiologist and cardiac nurse working in the outpatient cardiologist clinics from April 1, 2021, through June 30, 2021. Participants' eligibility was confirmed by the principal investigator from the medical record. The principal investigator conducted face-to-face interviews with participants to explain the purposes of the study as well as the requirements and to answer all questions. Signed informed consent was obtained from participants who agreed to participate.

All participants were listed in a sequence based on their date and time of clinical visits. Participants' allocation into the usual care or intervention group was determined by a randomization list that was generated in a 1:1 ratio using Microsoft Office Excel for Windows 2010 before the randomized controlled trial began. The randomization list was done by a co-investigator who was not involved in recruitment.

A research assistant collected data at baseline, 2 weeks from baseline assessment, and immediately post intervention by face-to-face interview and medical record review. At baseline, the research assistant set up the Samsung mobile health app to start recording participants' daily step counts during the 2-week preintervention and 8-week intervention periods. Once the second preintervention baseline assessment was conducted, only the participants in the intervention group were informed about their allocation assignment, and the intervention was started. The cardiologists ensured the safety of the intervention for participants who took part in the intervention group. All data were collected in the clinic, participant's home, or other place preferred by the participant.

## Intervention

The intervention was conducted using the Samsung mobile health app and was tailored to decrease the time spent in sedentary behaviors and to increase the time spent in physical activities performed at light or greater intensity levels. Our intervention was based on 3 observations from previous studies44–46: (1) prolonged time spent in sedentary behaviors was associated with the development of cardiovascular disease even with being physically active, (2) light-intensity physical activity level could produce cardiovascular health benefits at moderate or greater intensity levels, and (3) interventions that only focused on promoting physical activity levels would not have an effect on sedentary behaviors.

The intervention was guided by the Theory of Planned Behavior. The Theory of Planned Behavior has been extensively applied to many health behaviors, and research to date supports implementation of the Theory of Planned Behavior in lifestyle interventions

that require behavior change.47–56 The theory describes attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control as key constructs of behavior change. Attitude is determined by the individual's values about the consequences of performing the health behavior.57,58 The intervention encouraged positive attitudes toward being physically active by explaining simplistically the pathophysiology of HF and the significance of a sedentary lifestyle in the development and progression of HF. The benefits of physical activity in decreasing symptoms of HF, increasing functional capacity, and improving quality of life were also emphasized. This was done by daily texting and multimedia messages using free mobile social media apps. All messages were prepared in the Arabic language by the principal investigator and were validated by 2 cardiologists.

Subjective norm refers to beliefs by an individual about the presence of certain significant others such as family, friends, healthcare providers, or other persons with the same condition supporting their performance of health behavior.57 Significant others motivate an individual to follow a behavior to meet their expectations of behavior performance. Thus, individuals who perceive approval from significant others to engage in behavior are more likely to hold a positive subjective norm. A mobile social media app was used to establish a group for all participants of this study. Many cardiologists were invited to join this group, and the participants could invite and add significant others who have a strong positive influence on their behaviors. The intervention encouraged participants to share their behavioral progression, daily goal achievement, and experiences of being physically active. All other users were encouraged to interact with one's achievement. The principal investigator also established an ongoing discussion forum to ask an expert for points of clarification about HF, sedentary lifestyle, and physical activity.

Perceived behavioral control is determined by an individual's relative weighing of perceived factors in favor or presence of resources with the barriers to behavioral performance.57 To affect perceived control, participants were provided with tangible aids and skills required to empower them with the ability to enact physical activity behavior change. Participants were taught how to monitor and track their physical activity behavior change using the Samsung mobile health app. A research assistant helped participants to connect with social media through the mobile health app and how to use key features in social media, such as sharing a story or an event and sending messages or comments. The key features of the app were displayed in Arabic or other languages preferred by the participants. The principal investigator assisted participants in setting up a weekly goal for physical activity through the mobile health app and in determining the amount of weekly physical activity to achieve based on baseline physical

activity level. The intervention focused on both replacing and interrupting prolonged sedentary behaviors with 3 minutes of light-intensity walking after 30 minutes of inactivity, and promoting incidental (eg, climbing stairs) and long-bout planned physical activities at any intensity level. Each week of the intervention was designed to promote planned physical activities by sending participants hyperlinks of videos about a variety of methods of physical activity. This was conducted to allow participants to choose preferred forms of physical activity. All videos were established by the free Runtastic app. The daily step counts were reviewed at the end of the week for every participant, and those who did not attain a goal were contacted to solve any issues. The final goal at the end of the intervention was set up as 10000 steps per day at week 6 of the intervention for all participants. The American College of Sports Medicine recommends this amount of steps per day to meet the current physical activity guidelines. 59

## Usual Care

Participants in the usual care group received setup for the Samsung mobile health app without providing excessive details on its features. A cardiac rehabilitation intervention is not available, and referral to a community physical activity–promoting programs (eg, gym) is extremely rare in clinical practice in Jordan.13,60 Medical management by a cardiologist for patients with HF was restricted by providing detailed information on prescribed medications, fluid management, symptoms of HF, and sodium intake.60 During regular clinical appointments, the cardiologist reviewed patients' compliance with previous self-management recommendations and encouraged patients who were noncompliant.

## Data Analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 21.0. Analysis started with a descriptive examination of baseline demographics and clinical characteristics, as well as the average daily physical activity step counts per week, HF symptom burden, and quality of life before and after the intervention for both groups. Descriptive statistics including means, standard deviations, ranges, and frequencies were used as appropriate to the level of measurement of the variables. Differences between the home-based mobile health app intervention group and the usual care group were determined using t tests or χ2 tests.

Repeated-measures analysis of variance was conducted to examine within-group and between-group over-time differences in the average daily step counts per week between baseline to preintervention baseline time points and preintervention to postintervention time points. For the HF symptom burden and healthrelated quality of life, the comparisons over time were accomplished using repeated-measures analysis of variance among all 3 time points, including baseline, preintervention baseline, and postintervention. Over-time differences

in the outcomes were determined using Fisher least significant difference procedure used for post hoc tests. Statistical significance was determined at an α level of .05 or less.

There were significant group-by-time interactions for the average daily step counts from week 2 to week 8 of the intervention ( F = 8 7 . 4 , P < . 0 0 1 ), indicating that the intervention group had a greater improvement in the daily step counts during the intervention.

Table 3 shows HF symptom burden and healthrelated quality of life at baseline, preintervention baseline, and postintervention time points. The participants in the intervention group showed improvements in the fatigue (F = 35.8) and shortness of breath ( F = 6 . 7 )) burden scores, HF symptom burden total score (F = 5.5), and health-related quality of life (F = 3.4) post intervention in comparison with scores at baseline and preintervention time points ( P < . 0 1 ). There were significant group-by-time interactions ( P < . 0 1 ), suggesting that the intervention group had a greater reduction in fatigue (F = 5.1) and shortness of breath (F = 4.1) burden scores and HF symptom burden total score ( F = 7 . 7 ), and better health-related quality of life ( F = 4 . 6 )) post intervention. For leg or ankle swelling burden scores, there was no significant main effect within or between groups, indicating that this HF symptom did not change in either group post intervention.
