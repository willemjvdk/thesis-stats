## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# METHODS
This trial was approved by the Research Ethics Boards of participating hospitals. Data are available from the corresponding author pending approval of Research Ethics Boards of participating institutions and on reasonable request received from qualified researchers trained in human subject confidentiality protocols. Details of the CHF-CePPORT methodology have been described for the trial protocol13 and in a usability study.14

## Participants

Recruitment began in January 2014. Written informed consent from patients was obtained during enrollment. Final 12-month assessments were completed in February 2018. Data collection

for hospitalizations and blood work was completed in June 2018. Patient enrollment was based upon criteria that controlled for comorbidities, invasive medical procedures, and CHF features that would limit the potential benefit of digital self-care counseling: (1) male and female outpatients ≥18 years of age, (2) CHF diagnosis with left ventricular ejection fraction ≤45% for at least 3 months, with confirmation of left ventricular ejection fraction impairment by contrast ventriculography, radionuclide ventriculography, or quantitative echocardiography, (3) no worsening of CHF 1 month before recruitment, as well as confirmation of optimal and stable pharmacotherapy as determined by the referring cardiologist, (4) comprehension of English or French, (5) familiarity with and access to a personal computer with internet and email services, and (6) informed written consent. Exclusion was based on (1) documentation at enrollment of renal failure (serum-creatinine ≥300 μmol/L, ≥3.0 /dL), liver disease (alanine aminotransferase >3-fold upper limit of normal), or poorly controlled diabetes (fasting blood glucose >10 mmol/L or hemoglobin A1c >8%), (2) current symptomatic hypotension and systolic blood pressure ≤85 mmHg or persistence of systolic blood pressure >170 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure >100 mmHg despite antihypertensive therapy, (3) CHF secondary to clinically significant conditions such as uncorrected valvular cardiomyopathy, predominant right-sided heart failure, or acute myocardial infarction, (4) planned or cardiac surgery within 4 weeks before enrollment, or either history or current wait list for heart transplant, and (5) diagnosis of a major psychiatric disorder (eg, psychosis) or drug/alcohol abuse in the past year.

## Study Design

CHF-CePPORT was a Canadian multi-site, 2-arm, parallelgroup, double-blind, randomized controlled trial of usual care plus either online conventional CHF self-care education (e-UC) or e-counseling that used internet-based multimedia with motivational11 and cognitive-behavioral12 tools for CHF self-care. Assessments were administered online at baseline, 4, and 12 months. Recruitment was conducted through the heart function clinics of 3 tertiary care hospitals in Toronto, Vancouver, and Ottawa, Canada. Randomization was blocked in each site. The research design adhered to the CONSORT-eHealth checklist.15 Randomized allocation to trial arms was conducted using an independent and secure website: www.randomize.net. All research personnel were blinded to the treatment allocation code during assessments, data review, and statistical analyses, with the exception of a research associate who was not part of the CHF-CePPORT team.

In keeping with the design of our previous trial for hypertension,16 patients were blinded as to whether they were in the experimental group. They were informed that in addition to usual medical care, they would be contacted by our program via email on a weekly basis for months 1 to 4, then bi-weekly for months 5 to 8, and monthly for months 9 to 12. Each email provided a hyperlink to the respective e-counseling or e-UC program. Sessions were automated and delivered exclusively via the internet. Patient blinding was maintained as both arms of this trial provided regular contact and CHF self-care resources that exceeded usual care. Patients were informed that their automated program was designed to complement (not replace) the medical treatment provided by their cardiologist and health care

team and that all decisions about medical care would remain with their cardiologist. Finally, research staff and patients were directed to not discuss details of the digital intervention during assessments to maintain the double-blind design of this trial.

## Study Interventions

## Enhanced Usual Care With Online CHF Self-Care Education

The e-UC program provided comprehensive educational material on CHF and self-care behavior that was representative of websites of professional CHF/cardiovascular organizations in North America and Europe. All material was available from the initial logon. CHF topics included an overview of CHF symptoms, medical treatments, and recommended self-care behaviors (active living/exercise, diet, fluid and sodium intake, medication adherence, weight monitoring, smoke-free living, immunizations/vaccinations, stress management, and psychosocial support). CHF education also included trackers for selfcare behaviors.

## E-Counseling

Figure 1 provides a schematic summary of the patient-centered e-counseling protocol. The initial phase of the intervention incorporated key features from motivational interviewing11 to enhance intrinsic motivation for initiating and sustaining lifestyle habits that promoted CHF self-care behavior. E-sessions on planning and initiating behavior change used key features from cognitive-behavioral therapy.12 The clinical method for this digital intervention relied on multimedia that was integrated with interactive self-assessment tools and trackers for daily values of the following: activities that promote quality of life, CHF symptoms, readiness for changing CHF self-care behaviors, weight, step count, fruit and vegetable servings, fluid intake, CHF medications, smoke-free living, and negative self-talk associated with stress, anxiety, or depression. The multimedia included expert summaries of self-help tips for adhering to each CHF self-care behavior. In addition, dramatic vignettes were used to reflect and validate patient experience while providing positive role modeling about (1) accepting the CHF diagnosis, (2) connecting therapeutic lifestyle change to HRQL priorities, and (3) initiating and sustaining self-care behaviors to improve HRQL. Finally, a video-taped discussion among CHF patients provided positive role modeling for self-care adherence and reinforcement for the personal connection between CHF self-care and improved HRQL.

## Study End Points

A positive association has been reported between improvement in HRQL, measured by the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire Overall Summary scale (KCCQ-OS), and decreased CHF hospitalization or mortality.17–19 Therefore, the 12-month primary end point for this trial was the proportion of patients with clinically meaningful improvement as defined by a KCCQ-OS increase ≥5 versus ΔKCCQ-OS <5. The primary outcome was also defined in 2 ways: (1) KCCQ-OS increase ≥5 or KCCQ-OS ≥95 at baseline and 12 months versus ΔKCCQ-OS <5 and (2) KCCQ-OS improvement (≥5, or ≥95 at baseline and 12 months), versus deterioration (≥5 points), or no change (<5 points).17–19 Planned secondary outcomes included patient

engagement with CHF self-care resources as defined by duration (total weeks before a logon lapse ≥2 months) and intensity of engagement (total logons and total logon hours). Additional planned end points included behavioral health indices: 6-minute walk test, 4-day step count assessed by the LifeSource/ A&D XL-18CN triaxial activity monitor as detailed previously,13 and the Diet History Questionnaire subscale for daily servings of fruit and vegetables.20 Patient-reported readiness to initiate or maintain CHF self-care behavior was also assessed using the transtheoretical algorithm: precontemplation=1, contemplation=2, preparation=3, action=4, or maintenance=5.21 This was applied to (1) planned exercise up to 150 min/wk, (2) active living habits 5 to 6 d/wk, (3) 3 to 5 vegetable servings/d, (4) 2 to 4 fruit servings/d, (5) <30% calories from fat at each meal, (6) not adding salt to food at each meal, (7) avoiding salty snacks or fast foods, (8) checking labels when shopping for food that is low in sodium and fat, and high in fiber, (9) taking medications as prescribed, (10) recording weight each morning, (11) limiting fluids to <2 L (or 8 cups)/d, (12) smoke-free lifestyle, and (13) ≤1 drink of alcohol/d. The composite score for CHF self-care behavior was the total of the above items coded as 0=preaction (precontemplation, contemplation, or

preparation), and 1=action (action or maintenance).21 Patientreported readiness to use the e-UC or e-counseling program on a weekly basis at baseline and 12 months was also assessed with the Transtheoretical algorithm.21 Outcomes at 4 months are not reported here.

## Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were powered for a sample of 278 CHF patients with type 1 error of 5%, power at 80%, and estimated attrition of 21%, which is common for digital trials with minimal contact with research personnel.10,16 This estimate was based on HF-ACTION (Heart Failure: a Controlled Trial Investigating Outcomes of Exercise Training) which reported 12-month KCCQ-OS improvement (≥5 points) of 53% for exercise training versus 33% in usual care.22 Background characteristics of patients were examined with Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables. Primary and secondary end points were analyzed using an intention-totreat approach. These variables did not deviate from a pattern of missing at random. Multiple imputation of missing data was not conducted since this would invalidate planned analyses of the association between patient engagement with e-based

programs and the 12-month KCCQ-OS. The primary end point was analyzed using Pearson χ2 for e-counseling versus e-UC patients with an improved 12-month KCCQ-OS, defined as improvement ≥5 points versus change <5 points. Additional χ2 analyses assessed improvement as KCCQ-OS change ≥5 points or KCCQ-OS ≥95 at baseline and 12 months, versus change <5 points; and as KCCQ-OS improvement (≥5 points or ≥95 at baseline and 12 months) versus deterioration (≥5 points) or no change (<5 points).17–19 For secondary outcomes, Mann-Whitney U tests evaluated group differences for program engagement: total weeks before logon lapse ≥2 months, total logon hours, and total logons. A Cox proportional hazards model evaluated group differences over 12 months for total weeks before a logon lapse ≥2 months. Ordinal logistic regression subsequently assessed whether the 12-month KCCQ-OS was associated with a main effect for group (e-UC versus e-counseling) or an interaction between group and program engagement (total weeks before logon lapse ≥2 months, total logon hours, or total logons) while adjusting for covariates at baseline: age, left ventricular ejection fraction (<35, 35–40, and 41–45), and KCCQ-OS. KCCQ-OS scores at baseline and 12 months were transformed into tertiles for this analysis due to negative skewness. Significant group-by-program

engagement interactions were followed by separate ordinal logistic regression models for e-counseling and e-UC groups: dependent variable=12-month KCCQ-OS tertile, with adjustment for baseline age, left ventricular ejection fraction (<35, 35–40, and 41–45), and KCCQ-OS tertile. Finally, logistic regression analyses evaluated whether e-counseling versus e-UC was associated with 12-month improvement for behavioral health (median split for 6-minute walk test, 4-day step count, and daily servings of fruit and vegetables20) and ordinal logistic regression for total CHF self-care behaviors. Covariates included the baseline score for the behavioral health variable or total CHF self-care, as well as family income tertile. Covariates for all analyses were selected using χ2 tests for categorical variables and ordinal logistic regression for continuous variables. Analyses were conducted using SPSS v.26, with significance, P<0.05, 2-sided.

# RESULTS
## Patient Characteristics

CHF-CePPORT enrolled 231 patients with CHF (Figure 2). Background characteristics did not differ

significantly between e-UC and e-counseling groups (Table 1).

Over 12 months, medical therapies and supplemental interventions were prevalent among our patient sample, which was consistent with the tertiary level of care at our participating hospitals. This included patients with an implantable cardiac device (n=144, 62.3%) and cardiac medication adjustment (n=128, 55.4%). The e-UC and e-counseling groups did not differ significantly in the proportion of patients who participated in a supplemental program for diet or exercise (n=26 [23%] versus n=24 [21%], P=0.67, respectively) or who had an emergency department visit or hospital admission at our participating hospitals (n=8 [8%] versus n=12 [12%], P=0.41, respectively). Mortality was observed only in e-UC versus e-counseling (n=4 [3.3%] versus n=0 [0%], respectively, P=0.045), however, this analysis was exploratory in nature.

# Primary Outcome
## Clinically Meaningful Change on 12-Month KCCQ-OS

Baseline median KCCQ-OS indicated good-to-excellent HRQL, with low perceived burden due to CHF symptoms, or problems with psychosocial functioning and emotional distress24–26: e-UC=83.9 (interquartile range, 68–93), and e-counseling=82.3 (interquartile range, 67–93), respectively. Further, baseline KCCQ-OS tertiles corresponded approximately to good, good-to-excellent, and excellent HRQL categories (Table 1). E-Counseling versus e-UC did not demonstrate a significantly greater

proportion of patients with improved 12-month KCCQ-OS, when defined as an increase ≥5 points, or when defined as a combination of KCCQ-OS improvement ≥5 or ≥95 at baseline and 12 months. Groups also did not differ in a comparison of 12-month KCCQ-OS improvement ≥5 points or ≥95 at baseline and 12 months, versus deterioration ≥5 points, or change <5 points (Figure 3).

# Secondary Outcomes
Patient Engagement With CHF Self-Care Resources Patients in e-counseling versus e-UC were more actively engaged with their respective self-care program over 12 months, in terms of the number of logons, total logon time, and total number of weeks before a logon lapse ≥2 months (Table 2). This latter index of patient engagement represented an increase of 35% in active engagement with CHF self-care resources (Figure 4). Interestingly, patients in e-counseling versus e-UC also reported greater readiness at 12 months to continue using their CHF self-care program on a weekly basis: odds ratio=1.89 (95% CI, 1.04–3.4) P=0.04.

## Association Between Patient Engagement and 12-Month KCCQ-OS

Ordinal logistic regression analyses on 12-month KCCQ-OS tertiles indicated a significant interaction between group (e-UC versus e-counseling) and program engagement as measured by total weeks before a logon lapse ≥2 months (P=0.04) and total logon hours (P=0.02). Subgroup ordinal logistic regression analyses following

both interactions indicated that total weeks before a logon lapse and total logon hours were positively associated with 12-month KCCQ-OS tertile only for the e-counseling group (Tables 3 and 4). When total number of logons was used as the independent variable in the ordinal logistic regression analysis, both the group × program engagement interaction (P=0.77) and the main group effect (P=0.08) were not significant.

## Behavioral Health and Patient-Reported CHF Self-Care

Logistic regression analyses on 12-month assessments of behavioral health and CHF self-care variables indicated that e-counseling versus e-UC was associated with increased daily servings of fruit and vegetables and total CHF self-care behaviors (Table 5). Specific selfcare behaviors that improved with e-counseling included active living habits 5 to 6 d/wk, avoiding the addition of extra salt at meals, avoiding salty snacks and fast foods, and limiting fluid intake to <2 L (or 8 cups)/d (Table I in

the Data Supplement). Groups did not differ significantly on the 6-minute walk test or 4-day step count.
