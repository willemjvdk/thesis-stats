# Methods
## Study design and participants

This was a two-arm cluster RCT involving 60 stroke survivors with the physician as unit of randomization and patients as unit of analysis. The protocol for the study has been previously published.9 Institutional approval for this study was obtained in Ghana and at the Medical University of South Carolina, USA. Eligible subjects included were aged -18 years with a recent CT-scan confirmed stroke of <1 month and uncontrolled hypertension SBP - 140 mmHg at screening and/or enrollment visit. Exclusion criteria included:

(Note: MMSE 24 and global disability (mRS) -3 exclude patients who have severe cognitive impairments

and medical limitations that would interfere with adequate participation in the PINGS project.)

## Randomization and masking

Participants meeting eligibility criteria at screening were invited to the research office for enrollment (typically 7–14 days after discharge from the hospital) after obtaining informed consent from patients or proxy. Eligible participants were allocated to either the intervention or control arms (1:1) at baseline visit using a computergenerated randomization sequence. Baseline clinical assessments and questionnaires were administered by study physicians and trained research assistants.

Neither participants nor investigators were masked to group assignment in this open trial. Outcome measurements were however performed by a blinded assessor using an automatic sphygmomanometer to measure BP without the need for intervention by the investigator except placement of the cuff and switching on the device. All study participants were followed up at the out-patient Neurology clinic at the Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital, in Kumasi, Ghana.

## Procedures

Using a computer-generated sequence, patients were randomly allocated into four clusters of 15 patients each per physician – two clusters in the intervention group (henceforth referred to as IG) and two in the control arm (SC). Each sequence generated was kept concealed in an opaque envelope and opened by the Research Coordinator in the presence of the consenting eligible participant at enrollment. Patients in the IG received a Blue-toothed UA-767Plus BT BP device and a smartphone with embedded application for monitoring and reporting BP measurements and medication intake under nurse guidance for three months. Participants were instructed to monitor their own BP in their non-dominant arm, twice each morning and evening. A study nurse took each participant through a standardized operating procedure on how to measure blood pressure using the BP device and observed the participant/care giver perform the procedure satisfactorily. Participants who had difficulties with the protocol were re-trained until proficiency was attained. Tailored motivational text messages were delivered based upon levels of adherence to the medication intake regimen. A secure web page hosted at the Medical University of South Carolina automatically calculated the mean BP for each monitoring session, as well as daily medication intake adherence scores. This information was used in auto-generated summary reports and forwarded to nurse managers and PINGS project manager.

To control for attention exposure, the SC arm of the study received SMS messages dealing with healthy lifestyle behaviors but not with medication adherence. Attending clinicians were asked to review BP readings in the intervention and standard of care groups on a monthly basis for the first three months or as often as they wished. All study participants were followed monthly for the initial three months for interim analysis and afterwards at month 6 and month 9 for post-trial follow-up analyses by study physicians and nurses.

# Outcomes
Primary clinical outcome measure was clinic BP of <140/90 mmHg at month 9. Secondary outcome measures included: (i) medication adherence measured using medication possession ratio score at months 3, 6, and 9 and self-reported adherence using a modified version of the eight-item Morisky Medication adherence scale (MMAS). Other outcomes included (ii) hypertension management competence using the 18-item Perceived confidence scale,10 (iii) autonomous self-regulation using the 15-item Treatment Self-regulation questionnaire,11 (iv) patient satisfaction using the telemedicine satisfaction and usefulness questionnaire, (v) reported side effects of antihypertensive medications, and (vi) hypertension and stroke knowledge using a 14-item questionnaire.

## Statistical analysis

For this pilot trial, our sample size focused on precision of estimates for a future study design rather than power of statistical tests. Hence we recruited 30 participants (15 per physician ¼ cluster) per treatment group based on the number of degrees of freedom within each cluster to obtain reasonable precision for variance estimates. We compared medians and means for continuous variables using either the Mann–Whitney’s U-test or Student’s t-test while categorical variables were compared using the Chi-squared tests accounting for clustering by physicians. Comparisons of means of systolic BP (SBP) and diastolic BP (DBP) at month 9 between the two groups were made using linear mixed model with a random intercept to account for the clustering by physician via Proc MIXED in SAS 9.4. Similarly, the proportions for SBP < 140, DBP < 90 and SBP/DBP < 140/ 90 were made using Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) to account for the clustering by physician via Proc GENMOD in SAS 9.4. An empirical standard error was used to estimate 95% CI for the estimated parameters. Both intent to treat and protocol analysis were computed. Moreover, a subset analysis of those with enrollment SBP - 140 mmHg was made on each of the primary outcomes. Model diagnostics were

made via residual analysis. A p < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant difference.

# Results
## Baseline characteristics

Of 109 individuals assessed for eligibility, 49 (45%) were excluded: 43 did not meet inclusion criteria and 6 withheld their consent (Figure 1). The main reasons for exclusion were stroke with severe disability (n ¼ 34) and severe cognitive impairment (n ¼ 9). Two participants in the IG and three in SC groups were lost to follow-up. Reasons for the loss to follow-up are shown in Figure 1, and CONSORT statement form. The two groups enrolled into the study were well matched, with mean age of 55  13 years and 65% males. Comparisons of baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1.

# Primary outcome
Primary outcome data were available from 55 (91.7%) participants, 28 (93.3%) in the IG versus 27 (90.0%) in the SC group. At nine months, proportion of patients with BP < 140/90 mmHg was 14/30 (46.7%) in the IG versus 12/30 (40.0%) in the SC, p ¼ 0.79 by intentionto-treat and 14/28 (50.0%) versus 12/27 (44.4%), p ¼ 0.68 respectively by per protocol analysis. Proportion of participants with month 9 systolic BP < 140 mmHg in the IG was 22/30 (73.3%) versus 13/30 (43.3%), p ¼ 0.035 in the SC; conversely, diastolic BP < 90 mmHg at month 9 was 14/30 (46.7%) in the IG versus 23/30 (76.7%) in the SC arm, p ¼ 0.03 (Table 2).

The adjusted OR (95% CI) of achieving BP < 140/ 90 mmHg on the intervention was 1.24 (0.83–1.84), p ¼ 0.29 shown in online Supplementary Table S1. # Feasibility outcomes
Participants generally expressed excellent satisfaction ratings with the intervention on the telemedicine satisfaction and usefulness questionnaire (online Supplementary Table S4). Commonly cited challenges with the intervention included inconsistency with network connectivity, inability to login onto the application for taking BP and sending medication intake information, inability to read text content on health educational materials due to low literacy level, as well

as inability to personally operate the devices except via the aid of relatives.
