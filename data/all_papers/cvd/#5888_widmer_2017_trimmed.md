# Methods
Patient enrollment and experimental design

Patients were recruited, consented, enrolled, and randomized in a prospective fashion after PCI for ACS according to an approved Mayo Clinic IRB protocol registered on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT01883050) between

August 2013 and February 2015. As described previously,11 inclusion criteria included willingness to participate in CR as well as access to the Internet. All participants gave written, informed consent to participate both in CR and the trial. The 2 parallel study groups consisted of patients entering 3 months of Mayo Clinic CR randomized in a 1:1 fashion via computer generated sequence to standard CR versus standard CR + DHI (Figure 1). The group randomized to CR + DHI was educated on the use of the online and smartphone-based CR program within 1 week following enrollment, and entered their metrics (weight, BMI, blood pressure, glucose, lipids, diet habits, physical activity, quality of life, medication adherence, and smoking

status) with the help of a study coordinator. No changes pertinent to patient enrollment or data collection/analysis were made to the protocol after the initiation of the study (August 2013), however, the last administrative changes were made in October 2014. There were no cross-over participants in either group.

The DHI has been previously described.11,12 Briefly, the DHI encapsulated reporting of dietary and exercise habits throughout CR as well as educational information toward patients' healthy lifestyles. Those with compatible smart phones (iPhone and Android compatible) were assisted in downloading the appropriate application, and those without smartphone utilized the Web-based portal (not optimized for mobile use). Training consisted of study coordinators instructing the patients on the program use in a 30 minute session during the first week of CR. Patients were prescribed a standard phase II CR program as described previously4,11 for 36 sessions (approximately 12 weeks). Patients could contact the study team or obtain technical support through the online program, and inquiries were usually answered within 24 hours. Patients who entered values for metrics (such as blood pressure, lipids, glucose, or weight) that were more than 2-fold higher their prior value, or 2 standard deviations above the mean of the normal limits for lab values, were asked to verify the intended entry.If patients confirmed these values, messages to consult their physician appeared; as such changes in weight, blood pressure, or lab values could represent a potential danger to their health. Aside from the initial 30-minute orientation session for the DHI group, there was no additional patient contact from the healthcare provider team through the DHI. This was done to ensure that any differences in results seen between the 2 groups were solely secondary to the DHI.

Baseline and 3 month assessments included standard laboratory blood tests for fasting lipid panels and serum glucose values. These data were obtained by a blinded abstractor from the Mayo Clinic Cardiovascular Health Clinic Database and underwent statistical review by a blinded statistician. Furthermore, CR staff collected CR data blinded to the group allocation. Most patients in the study group underwent exercise stress testing at baseline and after 3 months per clinical protocol. The patients' CR providers assessed secondary endpoints such as blood pressure, height, weight, and the health behavior questionnaires (including diet, physical activity, Dartmouth quality of life (QOL), stress, and smoking status) at baseline and after 3 months in standard fashion.Weight and blood pressures were taken at every CR visit in standard fashion, with weight being assessed with clothes on and shoes off, and blood pressure assessed by BpTRU (Canada). Stress scores were answered on a 1–10 scale,13 with QOL surveys utilizing the Dartmouth format.14 Diet scores were calculated by the summation of daily servings of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and lean proteins with

points taken away for daily servings of saturated fats and sweets.11Follow up assessment at 3 months consisted of a replication of the baseline parameters in a similar fashion. CVD-related rehospitalizations and ED visits were considered the primary outcome and harvested by chart review and subsequently adjudicated by an independent, blinded cardiologist per pre-specified criteria at 6 months. ED visits consisted of any trip to the ED where a staff physician charted on the patients. Rehospitalization was considered to have occurred if there was an admission/dismissal process completed on the patient. These visits were counted individually as they occurred, and were considered cardiovascular-related visits if the patient's ED admission chief complaint was listed as chest pain, dyspnea, angina, or fatigue. Alternatively, if a cardiologist evaluated the patient in the ED or once admitted to the hospital, then the visit was considered cardiovascular-related. These data were confirmed by patient reported data in the cardiovascular health clinic database.

De-identified data were transmitted through Healarium, Inc (Dallas, TX), to the investigators for a comprehensive de-identified data analysis at the completion of the program. Patient-provided data in the DHI group was collected but not used in the analysis comparing the 2 groups. Patients who did not initially report for their intake into CR were removed from the analysis as primary and secondary outcomes data could not be assessed and verified. The investigators had full and direct access to all primary clinical data for the clinical trial, and take responsibility for its integrity and data analysis.

Using CVD events as the primary outcome, and given this information we set the minimum detectable difference for this parameter at 15%, with a standard deviation on 25% based on our prior Mayo Clinic data.11 These data provided for 80% power for the minimum detectable difference provided 32 patients are randomized to each group for a total of 64 patients. We assumed a 5% attrition rate, and initially aimed to randomize at least 68 patients over 2 years.

## Statistics

Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± SD; categorical variables as frequency (percentage). Group comparisons were made using Studentttest or the Pearson chi-squared test, respectively. Time-to-event data are summarized using Kaplan–Meier methods with comparisons made by the log-rank test. Data reported on 90-day measures and changes between baseline and 90-days reflect summaries of those with data available. As an additional analysis, we also fit mixed models to estimate the effect of DHI on the health measures. Subjects may have had measures scheduled at baseline, 30days, 60days, and 90days. For these models, repeated measures were modeled with terms for time from baseline and an interaction between time and treatment.

The slope and intercept were modeled both as fixed effects, and for each individual were modeled as random effects with an unstructured variance matrix. Additionally, error terms within individuals were modeled with an autoregressive covariance matrix. The interaction term from these models were scaled to estimate the DHI effect on change from baseline to 90 days. All tests are 2-sided with a 0.05 Type I error rate. Analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

## Baseline demographics

Baseline demographics revealed similar baseline statistics between both groups (Table I) with 6 patients in the control arm never reporting to CR (15%) versus 3 patients in the CR + DHI arm (8%). Initial medication profiles and self-reported medication adherence during CR were similar between the groups, with prescribed medication adherence being largely over 95% (Tables I and II).There were similarities between those randomized to DHI versus control at baseline regarding socioeconomic and demographic factors (Table I). There were 6 complaints about the program, 7 logistical questions about the program, 5 suggestions, and 2 compliments.

## ED visits and rehospitalizations

Additionally, we observed reductions in re-hospitalization and emergency department visits in those randomized to DHI + CR (Table III). All-cause (Figure 2A) as well as CVD-related ED visits and rehospitalizations (Figure 2B) were reduced in those randomized to the DHI group, though these did not meet statistical significance. Specifically, results suggest that those in the DHI + CR group had fewer CV-related rehospitalizations plus ED visits compared to the control group at 180 days, but the difference did not meet the threshold for statistical significance (8.1% vs. 26.6%; RR 0.30, 95% CI 0.08 to 1.10, P = .054). This resulted in a number needed to treat of 5.4 (95% CI 5.3, 5.6). The study participants experienced no harms or unintended consequences.
