# Materials and methods
## Design

A randomized controlled trial with a pretest– posttest design and 1:1 allocation ratio was used. We followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines [15]. Blinding was not feasible since the principal investigator (PI) collected the data on the outcome variables, and the participants were aware of their group allocation.

## Sample and setting

The target population for this study was patients with hypertension with the following inclusion criteria: (i) confirmed medical diagnosis of hypertension [systolic BP (SBP) > 140 mmHg or diastolic

BP (DBP) > 90 mmHg]; (ii) aged between 18 and 60 years; (iii) being diagnosed with hypertension for no >8 years and (iv) having smartphone that works on Android or iOS system and the users able to use the mobile application without assistance. The exclusion criteria were (i) history of myocardial infarction, heart failure, stroke and renal disease/dialysis; (ii) aged >60 years and (iii) unable to use application due to cognitive disorder. The elderly were not included in the study since they were found to face many usability problems when using computer-based self-management systems and find it difficult to deal with information technology [16].

The sample size was estimated using the G*power version 3.0.10, based on a two-tailed independent samples t-test at a significance level of 0.05, medium effect size (0.50) and a power of 0.80 [17]. A minimum of 102 participants was needed (51 in each group). To avoid possible attrition, the sample size was increased to 150 participants. However, due to dropout and loss of follow-up, the final sample was composed of 116 participants.

The participants were selected from cardiology clinics affiliated with one of the teaching hospitals <to be identified after acceptance>. This hospital locates in the center of <to be identified after acceptance> and has a total capacity of 599 beds. These clinics ensured the availability of patients having hypertension.

## Ethical consideration

The study adhered to Helsinki guidelines. Permission for conducting this study was obtained from the scientific research committee at the School of Nursing at the University of Jordan (Number 2019- Dwairej-SON), and from the Jordan University Hospital IRB committee. Written informed consent was obtained from the participants. The voluntary participation was assured and that there would be no consequences if they decided to withdraw from the study at any time. Participants’ medical information was extracted from the records that included

diagnosis, presence of comorbidities and the type of antihypertensive medication.

## Procedure

After obtaining the permissions to conduct the study, the PI was available at the data collection sites from 8.00 a.m. to 1.00 p.m. on specific days for 4 months to approach patients with hypertension who came to the clinics at different times. The PI identified potential participants by using an electronic search through the hospital electronic medical record system. After eligibility was determined, the PI invited the patients to participate in the study. The PI briefly introduced the study’s objectives and obtained informed consent. If the participants signed the informed consent, the data were collected. These included demographics, baseline self-care, self-efficacy and knowledge score. BP was measured by the PI at the baseline using a credible electronic sphygmomanometer in a sitting position after at least 5 min of rest. After obtaining the baseline measures, participants were randomly assigned either to the intervention group or to the control group using randomly selected sealed envelopes.

Block randomization was used to allocate the participants into the study groups. In this study, the block sizes randomly varied between 4, 6, 8 and 10. The random block size was generated by a person having no contact with the enrollment process using specialized software to generate a randomization list [18]. To determine the participants’ assignment to the control or intervention group, the PI randomly chose an envelope from a block.

The intervention group received the educational application. The educational program for the application was developed by the researchers. The educational application was designed to provide the participants with hypertension-related knowledge. The application was provided with 41 articles in the Arabic language addressing issues regarding hypertension disease, hypertension management, diet, physical activity, weight, smoking,

compliance with antihypertensive medication and BP self-monitoring.

The educational application was also designed to provide the participants with two daily notifications; these notifications appeared on the participant’s phone screen at two different times. The first reminder notification was sent to remind the participants to read from the educational materials and record their daily BP reading. For the second notification, the researchers developed a brief 46 non-tailored different tips regarding healthy diet, regular exercise, stress management, smoking cessation, weight reduction and medication adherence from the articles’ content available in the educational application.

The control group received the usual care. Usual care includes providing patients with appropriate antihypertensive medication, measuring BP infrequently and providing disorganized and fragmented education.

The participants were recruited from 1 October 2019 to 1 February 2020. Seventy-five participants were assigned to the intervention group, and 75 participants were assigned to the control group. The date for collecting the baseline data were recorded for all participants in both groups. The expected date for the follow-up visit, 8 weeks after receiving the intervention, was scheduled. The participants received two telephone calls to arrange for the follow-up visit. Participants who did not respond to the telephone calls were sent a reminder SMS. In the follow-up visit, selfcare, self-efficacy and knowledge scores were obtained.

Additionally, BP was measured by the PI at a follow-up visit using a credible electronic sphygmomanometer in a sitting position after at least 5 min of rest. For those participants who did not attend the final visit, the PI completed their posttest using telephone calls, and they were asked to send a BP reading after completing the telephone call. Sixty-three participants (37 from intervention group and 26 from control group) completed their posttest via telephone calls, and fifty-three participants (29 from intervention group and 24 from

control group) came to the clinic and completed their posttest.

## Study intervention

Material for the educational program was extracted from evidence-based websites. The application was provided with 41 articles in the Arabic language addressing issues regarding hypertension disease, hypertension management, diet, physical activity, weight, smoking, compliance with antihypertensive medication and BP self-monitoring. The developed educational program passed through several rounds of reviews and revisions by experts in the field to check the content accuracy. The steps of translation that include forward translation, expert back translation, pretesting and developing the final version were used to translate the educational program [2]. That is, the educational intervention was finalized and translated into Arabic and subsequently back-translated and was assessed for the last time by the experts before being used in the study.

After that, the Hypertension management application (HMA) was developed by sending the educational materials to specialized programmers. The application was designed to contain an initial project screen with a logo design and a list of several options with a side menu. The HMA side menu contained six options, including home page, my account, BP measurement registry, my stars, articles, information about the researcher, and exit button. On the home page, the participants reached the educational materials by clicking show all. Moreover, on the home page, the HMA allowed the participants to record and save their daily SBP and DBP readings; the date, time and the type of the device that participants used to measure their BP were recorded and saved. Participants reached their BP readings by selecting the measurement registry option. Furthermore, the PI accessed participants’ BP reading through the admin tool website.

The hypertension management application was provided with my stars option. When the participants open an article, the number of stars increases. The HMA was provided with 41 articles; thus, each

participant should have at least 41 stars to prove that he/she open all HMA articles. The number of stars was saved on the participant’s HMA under the ‘my stars’ option. Additionally, the researcher accessed the number of stars for each participant through the admin tool website. This feature helped the researcher to specify the frequency of using the application by each participant.

The educational application was also designed to provide the participants with two daily notifications; these notifications appeared on the participant’s phone screen at two different times. The first reminder notification was sent to remind the participants to read from the educational materials and record their daily BP reading. For the second notification, the researchers developed a brief 46 non-tailored different tips regarding a healthy diet, regular exercise, stress management, smoking cessation, weight reduction and medication adherence from the articles’ content available in the educational application.

The application was designed to work on most smartphones’ prevalent systems, Android and iOS, and to be browsed in Arabic. The programmer published the HMA on the Google Play and the app store. The developed HMA was tested for its accuracy by the programmers to check for any flaws before the beginning of data collection. Additionally, the usability of the developed HMA was evaluated by nursing and medical informatics faculties. The faculties were asked to try the application and to provide their comments on its usability.

## Control group

Participants in the control group received the usual care provided by clinics. Care for patients with hypertension in the clinics includes providing patients with the appropriate antihypertensive medication, measuring BP infrequently and providing disorganized and fragmented education by physicians. Only those patients with heart failure received an educational pamphlet and verbal instructions from a specialized nurse during their visit to the clinic.

## Measures

The study’s primary outcomes were self-care and BP control at baseline and 8 weeks after receiving the study intervention. Secondary outcomes included self-efficacy and knowledge scores measured at baseline and 8 weeks after receiving the study intervention.

## Hypertension Self-Care Profile

The participants’ self-care and self-efficacy scores were measured using the Hypertension Self-Care Profile (HBP-SCP) [19]. The tool has a high internal consistency with Cronbach’s α ranging between 0.83 and 0.93. The scale also demonstrated a satisfactory concurrent validity through a significant correlation with popular self-care hypertension measures such as the Hill–Bone Adherence Scale [19]. The Arabic version of HBP-SCP was used in the current study [4]. The translated version of HBP-SCP revealed a high internal consistency with a Cronbach’s α for the self-care behavior scale of 0.84 and for the self-efficacy scale of 0.9 [4]. In the current study, the HBP-SCP revealed a high internal consistency with a Cronbach’s α for the self-care scale of 0.77 and for the self-efficacy scale of 0.83.

The high BP self-care profile tool is selfadministered and consists of three separate scales. Those subscales are measures for self-care, motivation and self-efficacy. For this study, only the self-care and self-efficacy scales were used. Each scale has 20 items; each item is scored in a 4-point Likert scale, where the lowest score equal 20 and the highest score equal 80 in each subscale. The higher the score, the higher the levels of self-care behavior and self-efficacy [19].

## Hypertension Knowledge Level Scale

Participants’ knowledge was measured using Hypertension Knowledge Level Scale (HK-LS). This tool is a self-administered questionnaire with a relatively high internal consistency of Cronbach’s α of 0.82 [20]. This tool consists of six subdimensions with 22 items. Each item was prepared as part of a standard answer (Correct, Incorrect

or Do Not Know). Each correct answer was given one score, and the scores range between 0 and 22. The higher the total scores, the higher the level of knowledge [20]. In the current study, the HK-LS items were translated into Arabic. Pilot testing of the translated tool was conducted with a sample of 20 patients with hypertension before conducting the study. The participants in the pilot study were not included in the study sample. The translated version of HK-LS revealed a moderate internal consistency with Cronbach’s α of 0.67.

## BP control

The PI measured participants’ BP at the clinic using the same electronic sphygmomanometer after at least 5 min of rest at the pretest. At the posttest, the PI measured the BP at the clinic using the same sphygmomanometer. BP control was calculated as the proportion of participants who had a SBP <140 mmHg and a DBP <90 mmHg at baseline and 8 weeks after using the study intervention.

## Data analysis techniques

Data entry and analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0 [21]. Descriptive Statistics for Clinical and Demographic Characteristics was conducted. To assess the homogeneity of the sample characteristics at the baseline, a t-test was used for variables measured on the continuous scale and a chi-square was used for variables measured on the nominal level. A comparison of participants’ BP control, self-care and self-efficacy scores between the participants in the study groups was conducted using t-tests.

# Results
Out of the 116 participants, 57 (49.1%) were assigned to the intervention group and 59 (50.9%) were assigned to the control group. Eighteen participants in the intervention group were not included in the data analysis for the following reasons: 10 participants lost to follow-up. Their posttest data were not completed, and eight participants discontinued the intervention. Sixteen participants

in the control group were not included in the data analysis because they were lost to follow-up and their posttest data were not completed. Thus, 116 participants (77.5% of the initial 150 participants) had been included in the data analysis

(attrition rate = 22.5%). Figure 1 represents the CONSORT diagram for the progress of the study.

Sixty-three participants (54.3%) were males. The participants’ mean age was 50 years (SD = 6.2), ranging from 34 to 60 years. The

majority of the participants ( n = 9 4 ; ~ 8 1 \% )were married. The mean duration for the hypertension diagnosis was 36 months (SD = 6.2). Participants demonstrated normal mean SBP level at baseline, which was 131 mmHg (18.4). Table I demonstrates the frequencies and percentages of participants’ demographics and clinical characteristics.

To assess the homogeneity of the sample characteristics at the baseline, a comparison between the participants in the intervention and control groups was conducted. The mean baseline self-care, selfefficacy, knowledge, SBP and DBP were not statistically different. Table II demonstrates the results of the comparisons between the participants in the intervention and control groups.

## Self-care, self-efficacy and knowledge scores

To assess whether the means of self-care, selfefficacy and knowledge differed significantly for the intervention group compared with the control group after 8 weeks of receiving the intervention, an independent samples t-test was performed post-intervention. The mean self-care score differed significantly between the intervention and control groups; t (114) = 2.0, P = 0.04. The mean of knowledge score differed significantly between the intervention and control groups; t (114) = 2.30, P = 0.005 (Table III).

## The proportion of BP control

A chi-square test was performed to assess if there was a group difference in BP control. In the posttest, the proportion of the participants who controlled their BP in the intervention group was significantly higher than the proportion of the participants who controlled their BP in the control group; χ2 (1) = 8.1, P = 0.005 (Table IV).
