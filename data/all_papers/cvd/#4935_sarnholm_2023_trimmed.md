# METHODS
STUDY DESIGN. This was a single-center randomized controlled trial performed at the Karolinska University Hospital in Stockholm, Sweden. Recruitment started on January 1, 2018, and ended on March 28, 2019, and the last data were collected on September 14, 2020. The trial protocol was preregistered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03378349) and approved by the Regional Ethics Review Board in Stockholm (no. 2017/1882-31/2). PATIENTS. To be eligible for the trial, patients had to have: 1) paroxysmal AF with an average of $1 episode per month; 2) medical care as deemed optimal by the discretion of the study cardiologist and in accordance with current clinical guidelines8 ; 3) AF symptoms experienced as troubling or causing limitations in daily activities, that is, European Heart Rhythm Association (EHRA) class $ IIb3 ; and 4) an age of 18 to 75 years. Exclusion criteria were: 1) heart failure with a left ventricular ejection fraction of # 35%; 2) significant valvular disease; 3) planned catheter ablation for AF or catheter ablation performed within 3 months before inclusion; 4) other severe medical illness; 5) any medical restriction to physical exercise; 6) severe psychiatric disorder, severe depression, or risk of suicide; 7) concurrent psychological treatment; or 8) alcohol dependency. Trial participants were asked not to participate in any concurrent psychological treatment until AF-CBT had been concluded and to maintain their current pharmacologic therapy unless adjustments were clinically necessary.

## RECRUITMENT AND DETERMINATION OF ELIGIBILITY.

Patients were recruited from cardiology practices in greater Stockholm or by self-referral in response to advertisements in the daily press and on social media. All applicants registered on a secure webpage and completed online screening containing informed consent, medical history, the Atrial Fibrillation Effect on Quality of Life (AFEQT),18 the Cardiac Anxiety Questionnaire,19 the Patient Health Questionnaire-9,20 and the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test.21 Inclusion criteria 1–10 were then assessed based on the screening data, medical records, and a telephone interview by a study

cardiology nurse (E.Ó., J.K.). Documentation of at least 1 AF episode on a 12-lead ECG was required. If not available, a portable handheld ECG device (Zenicor EKG thumb) was provided for a maximum of 1 month to obtain AF documentation. All ECG diagnoses were verified by a cardiologist (F.B., H.S.). A licensed psychologist (E.A., M.B., M.B., J.S.) further assessed criteria 3, 11, and 12 via a structured telephone-based clinical interview.

Patients were then further assessed by medical history, current medication, blood pressure, a 12-lead ECG, weight, and height. In case of ongoing AF, patients were rescheduled within 7 days to ensure that they had spontaneously converted to sinus rhythm and thus fulfilled the criteria of paroxysmal AF.8 An echocardiogram was performed onsite if not taken within the previous 12 months. There were no other study visits conducted onsite.

TRIAL PROCEDURES. The included patients were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to either AF-CBT or to standardized AF education (AF-EDU) using a true random service. (See the Supplemental Appendix for a more detailed description of the randomization procedure.) Treatment began within 3 days after randomization. Patients and therapists were not blinded to allocation because of the nature of the treatment. All self-assessments were conducted online at baseline, posttreatment, and 3 months posttreatment (ie, approximately 6 months from baseline). After the 3-month assessment, the AF-EDU group was crossed over to AF-CBT and also assessed posttreatment. Further assessments were collected from the AF-CBT group 6 and 12 months after treatment completion.

INTERVENTIONS. The AF-CBT protocol has been previously evaluated in 2 pilot studies.16,17 The treatment was delivered online via a secure platform that included online therapist guidance and lasted for 10 weeks. AF-CBT targets symptom preoccupation; ie, fear and hypervigilance toward cardiac-related symptoms, fear of recurrent AF episodes, and AF-related avoidance of physical and social activities to reduce disability. The treatment emphasizes systematic and repeated exposure to cardiac-related symptoms and avoided situations as well as reducing excessive symptom-controlling behaviors, such as repeated pulse checking. AF-CBT was delivered in 6 interactive online treatment modules, and participants completed weekly homework assignments that they reported back to their therapist online. The following treatment interventions were covered in the modules.

1. Standardized education on AF and its pathophysiology, prevalence, common symptoms, and

treatment; information on preventive measures; the importance of a healthy lifestyle22; and symptom preoccupation

Interventions 1–5 aimed to reduce symptomrelated fear and hypervigilance and were used in conjunction to maximize their effectiveness.

The treatment was guided by 4 licensed psychologists (E.A., M.B., M.B., J.S.) with thorough experience in exposure-based CBT. The therapists received basic training on AF by the study cardiologist (H.S.) and on AF-CBT by lead author (J.S.). Their role was to give feedback on homework assignments and answer questions from participants. Cardiologists could be consulted throughout the treatment if needed. No deviation from the treatment protocol was detected.

Participants randomized to AF-EDU received 1 module of standardized AF education based on routine care practice and clinical guidelines22 to control for the provision of basic patient information and lifestyle advice but without any CBT interventions or guidance of a psychologist. The module contained: 1) education on AF, its pathophysiology, prevalence, common symptoms, and treatment; and 2) information on preventive measures and a healthy lifestyle, including engaging in physical activity (eg, to walk briskly 3 times a week for 30 minutes), alcohol consumption, weight control, and leading a social life.

OUTCOMES. The primary outcome was the estimated difference in mean change from baseline to 3 months on AF-specific QoL, as measured by the AFEQT. The full 20-item AFEQT questionnaire evaluates AFspecific QoL in the following domains: AF symptoms, impairment in social and physical activities,

medical treatment concerns, and AF-specific treatment satisfaction.18 Responses are given on a 7-point rating scale from “no limitations/symptoms” to “severe limitations/symptoms.” The AFEQT sum of all items is linearly transformed to range from 0 (severe symptoms and disability) to 100 (no symptoms and disability). The scale has good psychometric properties and is sensitive to change.18 The subscale

“AF-Specific Treatment Satisfaction” (items 19 and 20) measures satisfaction with current medical treatment and is not included in the total score and, thus, not analyzed in the present study. Secondary outcomes included AF-specific health care consumption and AF symptoms (University of Toronto Atrial Fibrillation Severity Scale),23 AF symptoms frequency and severity (Symptoms Checklist, Frequency, and

Severity Scale),24 symptom preoccupation (Cardiac Anxiety Questionnaire),19 general QoL (World Health Organization Disability Assessment Schedule),25 depression (Patient Health Questionnaire–9), 20

treatment satisfaction (Client Satisfaction Questionnaire)26 and adverse events.13 (See the Supplemental Appendix for a more detailed description of the secondary outcomes.)

AF BURDEN. AF burden (the proportion of recorded time during which patients exhibited AF) was measured at baseline, posttreatment, and at the 3-month follow-up using a continuous 3-channel ambulatory ECG patch monitoring for 5 continuous days (ePatch, BioTelemetry, Inc).27 The baseline ECG patch was handed out at the assessment visit along with verbal and written instructions. The follow-up assessment ECG patches were sent via mail.

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS. The original power calculation for the present study was based on an interim analysis of our previous pilot study of internetdelivered AF-CBT17 showing a within-group improvement on the AFEQT from baseline to posttreatment of 16.47 points (95% CI: 9.26-23.68). We assumed a similar improvement from AF-CBT at the 3-month follow-up in the present study and 40% or less improvement in the AF-EDU group, yielding a between-group difference in change of 9.89 points or more and an estimated raw score SD of 16.94. Each group would then need to include 45 participants to achieve 80% power (a  0.05). Allowing for 10% missing data, we needed to include at least 50 patients per group, but if feasible within the study’s timeframe, we aimed to include up to 60 per group to increase statistical power. Applicants who were in the recruitment phase of the study when the sample size of 120 had been reached were allowed to complete that phase, thus giving a total sample size of n  127. For the purpose of the present paper, we revised the power calculation based on the actual use of linear mixed models instead of between-groups Student’s t-test. In this calculation, we assumed a difference in mean change between the groups of 9.89 points and included the SD of mean change, SD 12.48, from the interim analyses of the previous pilot study. This calculation yielded power of 96% with n 45 per group.

The main outcome analyses were conducted according to the intent-to-treat principle, using hierarchical linear mixed-models analyses with random intercept. Analyses were performed in R (R Foundation) and Stata (StataCorp) by authors J.S. and B.L. and a statistical consultant. The models were fitted with group, time (as a 3-level factor including baseline [reference value], posttreatment, and 3-month follow-up), and group*time interaction effect as fixed effects. The group*3-month follow-up (primary endpoint) and group*posttreatment interaction effect

estimates were used to test the hypothesis that the AF-CBT group would show larger improvements from baseline to the 3-month follow-up and from baseline to posttreatment compared with the AF-EDU group, respectively. Within-group changes in the treatment group from baseline to posttreatment, 3-month follow-up, 6-month follow-up, and 12-month followup, respectively, were also analyzed using mixed models, where time was entered as a 5-level factor with baseline as the reference value. AF-specific health care consumption was analyzed with generalized linear mixed models using the Poisson distribution and a log link function. To analyze change in ECG-measured AF burden from baseline to posttreatment and 3-month follow-up, a Poisson regression model in the generalized estimating equations framework was used. See the Supplemental Appendix for further details.

# RESULTS
SAMPLE. Figure 1 displays the participant flow through the trial. The sample consisted of 127 patients who were randomly assigned to AF-CBT (n 65) or AF-EDU (n 62). Baseline characteristics were well balanced between the 2 groups (Table 1, Supplemental Table 1). The average baseline AFEQT score for the whole sample was 62.5  16.7, corresponding to moderate AF severity.18 Eight of 127 (6%) participants were lost at the 3-month follow-up (8 in the AF-CBT group and 0 in the AF-EDU group). At the 12-month follow-up in the AF-CBT group, 12 of 65 (18%) were lost. In light of the relatively small proportion of missing data, we did not impute or otherwise handle missing data.

PRIMARY OUTCOME. At the 3-month follow-up, as compared with baseline, QoL in the AF-CBT group was

significantly improved (Table 2, Figure 2). The mean AFEQT summary score improvement was 21.0 points for AF-CBT relative to 6.0 points for AF-EDU, resulting in a between-group difference of 15.0 (95% CI: 10.1- 19.8; P < 0.001). The improvement of AF-specific QoL from baseline was still significant at the 12-month follow-up (17.7; 95% CI: 14.4-21.0; P < 0.001) (Supplemental Table 2). Figure 2 summarizes changes in AF-specific QoL over the study period.

SECONDARY OUTCOMES. We also observed a statistically significant reduction of cardiac-specific health care visits at 3 months (Table 3). Participants who had received AF-CBT consumed 56% less AF-specific health care (95% CI: 22-90; P 0.025) than those in the AF-EDU group. Other secondary outcomes (Table 2) showed the same outcome pattern as the primary outcome with statistically significant differences in estimated mean changes at the 3-month follow-up favoring the AF-CBT group. Significant and maintained within-group mean changes were observed 12 months after the treatment (Supplemental Tables 2 and 3). For effect size calculations, see Supplemental Table 4.

AF BURDEN. The baseline average AF burden was 5.3% in the AF-CBT group and 5.0% in the AF-EDU group. At the 3-month follow-up, AF burden had increased to 7.3% and 9.2%, respectively, with no difference between groups (P 0.59) (Supplemental Table 5). A post hoc analysis showed a nearsignificant increase in AF burden in the total sample, from 5.1% at baseline to 8.3% at the 3-month follow-up (incidence risk ratio: 1.62; 95% CI: 0.96- 2.73; P 0.07).

TREATMENT ACTIVITY AND SATISFACTION WITH THE TREATMENT. The AF-CBT participants completed, on average, 5 modules ( 1.34; range: 1-6).

A large majority (n ¼ 54; 83%) completed at least 4 modules and, thus, received all the core treatment components. The psychologists spent a weekly mean of 5.7 2.2 minutes per patient. At posttreatment, participants scored, on average, 26.0 - 4.7 of 32 points on treatment satisfaction as measured by the Client Satisfaction Questionnaire,26 corresponding to overall “very satisfied” with the treatment.

ADVERSE EVENTS. No serious adverse cardiac or other medical events were reported. In the AF-CBT group, 27 (44%) patients reported 46 adverse events during treatment concerning increased focus on symptoms and stress. A majority of the adverse events were rated as mild, and 6 events were rated as “affecting me very negatively.” At the posttreatment assessment, 4 (6.5%) patients in the AF-CBT group and 3 (4.8%) in the AF-EDU group reported mild and transient adverse events (eg, increased stress caused by the study procedure, discomfort during exercises, or increased cardiac attention). At the 3-month follow-up, 1 participant in the AF-CBT group

reported stress caused by participating in the study, and at the 6- and 12-month follow-up, 1 participant reported a mild adverse effect.

CHANGES IN CARDIAC HEALTH AND MEDICATION. At the 3-month follow-up, 7 (12%) participants in the AF-CBT group and 15 (24%) participants in the AF-EDU group reported a perceived deterioration in cardiac health (most commonly increased AF symptoms). Participants reported very few changes in medication and cardiac-related procedures (Supplemental Table 6).

This randomized controlled trial evaluated 10 weeks of online atrial fibrillation cognitive behavioral therapy (AF-CBT) targeting symptom preoccupation in patients with symptomatic paroxysmal atrial fibrillation (AF). AF-CBT was compared to AF education (AF-EDU), and the primary endpoint was AF-specific QoL 3 months after treatment conclusion. AF-CBT led to large improvements in AF-specific QoL and reduced health care use compared to AF-EDU, but AF burden was unchanged. Online AF-CBT may offer a valuable addition to current AF management. With its exposure-based approach, AF-CBT aims to increase the tolerance of cardiacrelated symptoms through physical exercise, for example, and an increased focus on symptoms may be expected as an effect of the treatment. Other studies of exposure-based CBT for somatic conditions have also shown that some patients experience a transient increase in symptoms during treatment.13,14 Importantly, the average patient satisfaction score corresponded to “very satisfied,” and only 4 patients in the AF-CBT group reported mild and transient adverse events posttreatment.

Taken together, the positive results obtained in multiple domains of AF disability, participants’ high adherence to and satisfaction with the treatment, and the absence of serious adverse events indicate that exposure-based AF-CBT is an effective and safe additive treatment option for AF patients.

STUDY STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS. Strengths of the study were the thorough cardiac and psychological assessment procedures, the use of a wellvalidated criterion for AF disability (ie, EHRA class $IIb),3 and the combined use of subjective and objective measurements of AF burden. Limitations of the study include the use of AF-EDU as a control condition. AF-EDU controlled for the provision of information and self-management of AF but did not control for attention from the caregiver. Because AF-EDU participants knew that they would be crossed over to AF-CBT, AF-EDU probably did not control for expectation of improvement. Nevertheless, patient education is commonly offered in routine care for AF and is associated with improved outcomes,36 which suggests ecological validity of the condition. In addition, 5-day ECG patch monitoring27 may offer too short a time period to reliably measure AF burden.

Using an implantable loop recorder could have increased reliability of the AF burden estimates but would have subjected participants to an invasive procedure for evaluation of a secondary endpoint. The AF burden was relatively low in the sample, and the effectiveness of AF-CBT also needs to be confirmed in other populations of symptomatic AF patients and other regional and clinical contexts. In general, the sample also consisted of more women and those younger than the average AF population, which may limit the generalizability of the results on one hand. On the other hand, female sex and younger age are associated with more impaired QoL in AF,37 which could explain why there were proportionally more women in the study sample.

## CONCLUSIONS

To our knowledge, this is the first randomized controlled trial that has evaluated exposure-based, internet-delivered CBT for patients with symptomatic paroxysmal AF already receiving routine AF care. The effects of AF-CBT on AF-specific QoL in our study compare well with other interventions. These findings indicate that using systematic and repeated exposure to reduce cardiac-related fear, AF-related avoidance, and symptom-controlling behaviors led to large improvements on AF-specific QoL, AFspecific health care consumption, self-reported AF symptoms, and symptom preoccupation. We conclude that symptom preoccupation is an important target for treatment and that internet-delivered AF-CBT is both feasible and clinically effective adjunctive treatment for the target population. If further research replicates these results, AF-CBT can be made available for a large group of patients where current treatment methods have limited effectiveness.
