## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# Method
The study is a rater-blinded RCT with participants assigned to either the GRASP HEP treatment group or the UCT control group (Thompson & Schoenfeld, 2007).

## Participants

All human subject activities were conducted under the University of Virginia Institutional Review Board for Human Subject Research (IRB-HSR). The study was registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT03872466). Participants were identified from a population of stroke patients receiving in-patient rehabilitation care, outpatient rehabilitation, or who had been previously discharged from rehabilitative care at a regional health system. All study participants provided informed consent, and all study records were maintained in a secure and confidential manner. Inclusion/exclusion criteria were verified through a three-step process including an initial medical record review, verbal confirmation prior to administration of consent, and a screening conducted by a study OT following consent. All participants had a history of right or left hemispheric stroke affecting normal hand function. Inclusion criteria required UE active range of motion (assessed in a seated, against-gravity position) of at least 45 degrees elbow flexion, 30 degrees shoulder flexion and abduction/ adduction, and 15 degrees shoulder rotation. Participants had sufficient active finger flexion at the metacarpophalangeal joint in at least one finger to be visually detected by a study OT. Participants had visual acuity with corrective lenses of 20/50 or better and were able to understand and follow verbal directions. The study excluded participants with visual field deficit in either eye or hemispatial neglect that impaired the ability to view, process, or perceive visual stimuli. Candidates were excluded if they had significant contractures restricting normal UE movement or more than mild tone/spasticity (measured on a modified Ashworth, 5-point scale). Other exclusions included open wound or infection, severe edema, rheumatoid impairments, or excessive swelling which might interfere with wearing the glove orthosis.

Rolling enrollment began on June 11, 2020, and continued until February 6, 2022. A study coordinator assigned each participant to either the GRASP HEP or the UCT control group following consent and pre-assessment using a permuted block randomization scheme with a block size of 4 (pre-generated prior to start of enrollment by the study biostatistician using the Statistical Analysis Suite procedure). The study coordinator was blinded to participant assignment until after the completion of pre-assessment. Figure 1 presents a CONSORT flow diagram. Final data were collected on April 26, 2022. A total of 56 eligible candidates were screened for eligibility. Of the 21 participants enrolled in the study and randomized, 18 completed the protocol and all assessments. No adverse incidents or harm were reported. Table 1 presents the characteristics of the participants who completed the clinical trial.

## Study Protocol

Prior to the randomized group assignment, all participants underwent pre-assessment by a blinded evaluator OT. All participants also underwent post-assessment following an 8-week period, again by a blinded evaluator OT. If a participant was randomized to the GRASP HEP group, a nonblinded study OT conducted a home visit to set up the system and provide orientation and training. GRASP HEP participants were asked to employ the system in asynchronous telehealth sessions for 45 min, 4 times per week during the 8-week intervention. A non-blinded study OT provided telehealth services including monitoring real-time participant data from the asynchronous telehealth sessions via a webbased provider dashboard. A screenshot of the provider dashboard is shown in Supplemental Figure 1. Every other week during this period, participants also participated in a synchronous telehealth visit with a non-blinded study OT. Participants in both the UCT control group and GRASP HEP group continued to receive any therapy services that may have been prescribed outside of the study. The UCT participants did not receive additional treatment services through the study prior to the 8-week post-assessment. The GRASP HEP participants did not receive additional treatment services through the study beyond the GRASP telehealth protocol. To promote equity, following post-assessment, UCT participants were offered the opportunity to use the GRASP system at home for an equivalent 8-week period.

## Assessments

Pre- and post-assessments of UE motor function were administered by blinded OTs in an outpatient therapy clinic. The primary outcome measure was the Fugl-Meyer Upper Extremity (FMUE) assessment. Secondary outcome measures included the Wolf Motor Function Test (WMFT), the Box and Blocks Test (BBT), and the Motor Activity Log (MAL). Study therapists were trained in the use of the assessments and demonstrated proficiency in consistent administration.

Fugl-Meyer. FMUE is one of the most widely used and accepted quantitative measures of motor function in stroke clients, used in clinical and research settings. Individual movements associated with specific motor functions are scored using a 3-point ordinal scale (0, 1, or 2). Higher scores correspond to higher levels of motor function. The FMUE subscale incorporates 33 items relevant to shoulder, elbow, forearm, wrist, hand, and grasp function, thus creating a possible range of 0 to 66 points (Fugl-Meyer et al., 1975). The FMUE subscale was found to have excellent interrater reliability (Duncan et al., 1983).

Wolf Motor Function Test. The WMFT test is a functionally oriented clinical and research instrument that has been shown to have high interrater reliability, validity, and internal consistency when used with the stroke population (Wolf et al.,

2001). The test consists of 15 motor tasks scored both for time and quality of movement. Nine are functional movements while the remaining elements are joint-segment movements. The WMFT was selected as a secondary outcome measure as tasks include interactions with real-life objects (i.e., pencil, paperclip, soda can, face towel) that are clinically relevant to OT practice. WMFT-TIME is a quantitative measure calculated using the average time to complete the tasks. A reduction in average completion time implies improved motor function. The WMFT Functional Assessment (WMFT-FA) is an observational assessment of movement quality and scored using an ordinal rating scale that ranges from 0 (no use of the affected side attempted) to 5 (normal function). A total WMFT-FA score is calculated by taking the average across all 15 UE tasks. An increase in WMFT-FA score implies improved motor function.

Box and Blocks. The BBT provides a measure of unilateral gross manual dexterity with excellent inter-rater reliability (Platz et al., 2005). Participants are asked to move 2.5 cm wooden cubes from one 8.5 cm tall box to another, as quickly as possible (Desrosiers et al., 1994). The BBT score is simply the number of blocks moved in 60 seconds.

Motor Activity Log. The MAL is a valid and responsive structured interview assessing use of the affected arm in 30 daily activities in the client’s real-life experience (Uswatte et al., 2005; Van der Lee et al., 2004). These range from turning on a light to eating finger foods. For each activity, the client provides a self-assessment of the frequency with which the affected arm is involved in the activity (MAL-Amount) and the quality with which the affected arm performs (MAL-How Well). Grading is on a Likert-type scale with 0 corresponding to never using the weaker arm for the activity and 5 corresponding to using the weaker arm equivalently to the stronger arm.

# Materials
GRASP System. The GRASP system incorporates the SaeboVR therapy software which employs Kinect-based

motion capture to allow the virtual practice of IADLs and is described in a previous publication (Adams et al., 2018). Each virtual IADL comprises a sequence of tasks. These include pick-and-place interactions and sustained, stability movements involving an avatar that tracks the patient’s UE and virtual objects such as utensils, tools, food items, selfcare instruments, clothing, appliances, household items, drawers, and cabinets. Tasks are designed to require functional UE movements with variable levels of active range of motion in the shoulder and elbow joints.

The GRASP system extends the capabilities of SaeboVR by introducing an instrumented glove orthosis, shown in Figure 2, that tracks the wrist, index finger, and thumb of the affected UE to enable participants to engage in manual interactions with virtual objects. The glove orthosis, a commercial SaeboGlove product, is transformed into a virtual reality glove by adding motion-sensing modules on the wrist and fingers. A first-generation GRASP prototype system was previously described (Adams et al., 2019). The present study employed a second-generation prototype, shown in Figure 2, that employs Bluetooth wireless communications, a rechargeable battery, and a custom electronics enclosure. The motion capture algorithms used for tracking of the UE have previously been described (Adams et al., 2018 & 2015).

For the GRASP study, the original software was extended to include a more complete collection of IADL activities. These are grocery shopping, putting away groceries, vegetable garden harvesting, orchard fruit picking, preparing breakfast, preparing dinner, BBQ grilling, table setting, dishwashing, doing laundry, organizing closet, pet shopping, puppy care, horse care and feeding, and restaurant dining. In the GRASP activities, manual interactions with virtual objects require both hand placement and orientation with respect to the virtual objects and the performance of necessary digit flexion (for grasp) or extension (for release). The necessary flexion and extension required to complete the grasp-release interactions can be graded and calibrated to match participant capabilities at the start of each session, ensuring that the motor challenge level is appropriate for each participant. Even those with only a minimal digit active range of motion can successfully complete the activities, and the challenge level increases with functional improvement over the course of the HEP. Figure 3 shows a participant interacting with a GRASP virtual environment using the glove. The GRASP system is designed for telehealth use and thus includes preprogrammed cues if needed. These include a gentle visual cue (pulsating arrow) after 10 s and a verbal cue (i.e., “Get the plate”) after another 10 s. The system employed in this study did not include haptic (vibratory or force) feedback.

Telehealth Sessions. Telehealth OT included both asynchronous and synchronous sessions. In asynchronous sessions, the participant used the GRASP system independently at home. The telehealth OT would monitor these asynchronous sessions, using the provider dashboard web application to gauge

compliance and interpret metrics including the number of reaches, number of grasp interactions, number of activities completed, average task completion time, and total time using the system. In synchronous telehealth sessions, which occurred every 2 weeks, the telehealth OT interacted with the participant via Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)-compliant video conferencing or telephone, per the participant’s preference. During the synchronous telehealth sessions, the telehealth OT provided feedback about system use and progress, supported use of the technology, and determined if system settings for reach difficulty and repetitions should be adjusted to provide a “just right” challenge.

## Data Analysis

A summary of analytical methods is presented here. Please refer to the included Supplemental File 1 for detailed coverage. A conventional repeated measures linear mixed model (LMM) approach was used to analyze the FMUE, WMFT, and MAL outcome results (McLean et al., 1991). For the WMFT completion time outcome (WMFT-TIME), the preintervention and post-intervention average task completion times were analyzed on the natural logarithmic scale. The ratio of geometric means (GM ratio; Manikandan, 2011) was used for comparing the post-intervention WMFT-TIME GM to the pre-intervention GM. As the BBT produces count data (number of blocks placed in 60 s), the outcome data were analyzed using a generalized estimating equation (GEE) negative binomial modeling approach.

# Results
Results are presented below for each of the outcome measures considered. A more expansive presentation is provided for the FMUE assessment (primary outcome), followed by summary results for secondary outcome measures.

# Primary Outcome Measure Results
FMUE Assessment Results: The by-subject scores are graphically summarized in Figure 4A. Empirical distributions for

the pre- to post-assessment change in FMUE score for the UCT control group and GRASP HEP group are illustrated in Figure 4B. Summary statistics and results of statistical analysis for the FMUE assessment are provided in Table 2.

For the UCT control group, there was no significant change in FMUE score over the 8-week period between assessments. The mean of the distribution of the pre- to postintervention FMUE composite score changes for the UCT group was 1.6 units (95% CI: [−1.9, 5.0 units], p = .351). There was a significant and positive change in the FMUE score for the GRASP HEP group. The mean of the distribution of the pre- to post-intervention FMUE composite score changes for participants assigned to GRASP HEP telehealth protocol was 10.1 units (95% CI: [6.7, 13.5 units], p < .001). The difference between FMUE outcomes between the GRASP HEP group and the UCT control group was significant. The mean pre- to post-intervention change in the FMUE composite score was 8.6 units greater (95% CI: [3.7, 13.4 units]) for the participants who underwent 8 weeks of GRASP HEP therapy than for the UCT control group (p = .002). Differences in pre-assessment FMUE score and in the time since stroke between the two groups did not impact statistical conclusions. After adjusting the LMM to account for between-group disparities in pre-intervention FMUE composite score and in the time since stroke, the mean pre- to post-intervention change in the FMUE composite score remained 8.6 units greater (95% CI: [4.9, 12.2 units]) for the participants who underwent 8 weeks of GRASP HEP therapy

than for the UCT control group ( p < . 0 0 1 ). The primary investigational hypothesis is thus confirmed—the GRASP HEP provided superior UE motor function outcomes (as measured by FMUE score, the primary outcome measure) in stroke survivors after 8 weeks, compared with the UCT control group.

Furthermore, in looking at results within the FMUE scale, we found particularly large differences between GRASP HEP and UCT outcomes for the test components corresponding to flexor synergy (moving the hand between the knee to the face), grasp (various fist and opposition patterns), and volitional movement (shoulder abduction, shoulder flexion, and pronation/supination). These items are represented as task groups 3, 4, and 7, respectively, in Supplemental Figure 2. These movement patterns were practiced repeatedly within the virtual IADL sessions using the GRASP system, perhaps influencing the especially strong gains. Other FMUE components that showed less pronounced gains, such as reflex activity in the biceps, triceps, and finger flexors, were not directly practiced using the GRASP system.

# Exploration of Assessment Results
Improvements in FMUE scores for participants in the 8-week GRASP HEP were statistically superior to outcomes for subjects in the UCT control group. Furthermore, the estimated 8.6-point mean difference in outcomes between the groups can also be considered to be a clinically important difference (CID), defined as an improvement of at least 10% of the 66-point range of the FMUE scale (Page et al., 2012).

The WMFT-TIME analysis did not reveal a statistically significant difference between the GRASP HEP and UCT control groups. This finding is consistent with (Adams et al., 2018). It appears that high variability in the time-based measure may make it difficult to extract sufficient statistical power without larger sample sizes. The WMFT-FA data provided more evidence of a differential improvement for the GRASP HEP group (estimated mean difference of 0.53 on a 5-point scale), but it also fell below the threshold for statistical significance (p = .126). Likewise, within the Box and Blocks data, no statistically significant change was noted. High variability in this count-based assessment masked any potential evidence of a differential positive effect of the GRASP HEP intervention. It is also possible that the virtual IADL practice using the GRASP system did not generalize well to the specific BBT task of moving cubes.

Analysis of MAL outcome measures revealed a statistically significant and large CID in both the Amount and HowWell self-assessments (0–5 Likert-type scale). For both

the MAL-Amount and MAL-HowWell measures, the estimated improvement from pre- to post-test was 1.31 greater for the GRASP HEP group than for the UCT control group. For the GRASP HEP participants, the pre-intervention selfassessment mean response for MAL-Amount items was between “very rarely” and “rarely.” After the GRASP HEP intervention, the mean response approached “half” (used the weaker arm about half as much as before the stroke). The mean pre-intervention response for MAL-HowWell rating was between “very poor” and “poor” (only minimal use of weaker arm in activities). Post-intervention, the mean response increased to “fair” (now used weaker arm in the activities, but still not as well as before the stroke).

The positive differential outcomes for the participants in the GRASP HEP group may be explained, in part, by considering the high number of UE movements elicited in the virtual practice of IADLs. In addition, most participants demonstrated strong engagement with both asynchronous and synchronous telehealth sessions (see Table 1). This participation is consistent with other published studies that found that technology-enhanced therapy sessions may result in more UE repetitions than traditional therapy (Adams et al., 2018; Rand et al., 2014).

## Study Limitations

A significant limitation of this study was the enrollment challenge associated with conducting the study during the COVID-19 pandemic. Many candidates declined to enroll due to concerns about person-to-person contact associated with study visits (assessments in the clinic and initial system setup at home). Ultimately, only 21 participants were enrolled, of which 18 completed the protocol. The study was ended before enrollment goals were met due to the end of the funding grant. Fortunately, the observed effect size in the FMUE primary outcome measure was greater than had been assumed in the original power analysis. Another limitation is that additional covariates (beyond pre-assessment scores and time since stroke) that could have the potential of biasing results were not included in the analysis model. Because the control arm of the study is UCT, the benefit of the GRASP HEP telehealth protocol should be interpreted as being incremental with respect to normal care. The results do not imply that a GRASP HEP intervention is superior to a dosagematched alternative. In addition, this study has the potential for selection bias, as participants who chose to join the study may have been more inclined to adhere to a HEP than the average stroke survivor.

## Future Research

Analysis of the data collected by the GRASP motion capture system is ongoing and is expected to reveal interesting details regarding actual participant use of the system beyond the summary dosage information presented in this article. Additional

analysis is also ongoing of the usability, technology acceptance, and narrative interview data collected during this study as well as participant engagement with the synchronous telehealth sessions. These analyses are beyond the scope of this article and will be presented in a future publication.

## Conclusion

This randomized, controlled trial contributes to the existing body of knowledge by providing evidence that a HEP based on a system for virtual IADL practice with telehealth support can lead to a clinically important and statistically significant difference in UE motor status in stroke patients with hemiplegia.
