# Methods
Design. This study employed a quasi-experimental design.

Sampling. The sample size was calculated by using the formula for repeated measures design36 based on Naylor et al’s37 study, with a significance level of .05, power of .80, and sample size adjusted for a 10% attrition rate.38 The calculations led to a sample size of 146. Participants were recruited from the list of patients with HF who had follow-up appointments at the medical outpatient department (MOPD) of the concerned hospital between August 2018 and January 2019. The charge nurse introduced the study, and interested patients were asked to meet with the researcher. A computerized randomization program was used, with the control and experimental groups

coded A and B, respectively. Each randomization sequence code was written on a sheet of paper and placed in an opaque envelope according to the allocation orders with an equal allocation ratio of 1:1. The envelopes were concealed. After enrolled participants met the eligibility criteria, the researcher opened the envelopes and assigned the participants to the experimental and control groups according to the code showing in the envelope for each participant.

The main inclusion criteria were: aged 18 or older; NYHA FC II or III diagnosis of HF within the last 12 months; access to a mobile phone and internet connection; and ability to use the mobile app with or without caregiver assistance. The exclusion criteria were: cognitive impairment as determined by Thai Mental State Examination scores <2439; cancer; chronic kidney disease; surgery planned within 2 months; and/or being on dialysis. Participation was discontinued if less than 30 days of self-monitoring data were submitted.

In total, 240 participants were initially screened for eligibility. Ninety-four were excluded because of their diagnosis having been made over 12months previously (n=74); no internet signal (n = 18); or refusal to participate (n = 2). Of the 146 eligible participants, one in the TTP group was removed owing to having submitted self-monitoring data for only 10 days. Thus, data from 72 participants in the TTP group and 73 in the control group were collected and analyzed (Figure 1).

Instruments. All instruments were submitted to 5 clinical experts to determine validity. Using Cronbach’s alpha, internal consistency reliability was tested with 30 individuals who met the same inclusion criteria as the participants.

Measures. At baseline, the demographic questionnaire and Enforced Social Dependency Scale were administered to both groups by a research assistant. Data on functional status and rehospitalization outcomes were collected at 6 and 8 weeks and satisfaction with care at 6 weeks post-enrollment. These specific time points have been shown to allow for physical recovery and facilitate initial emotional adjustments and behavioral/lifestyle modifications.43 Data on outcome variables in both groups were collected by the research assistant who was blinded to the participant’s randomized group. Functional status and satisfaction with care were determined via telephone interviews, and data on rehospitalization were collected via telephone interviews and medical records.

Interventions. All participants received usual care, which included discharge planning and HF-specific education regarding drug use, self-care, and self-management. They also were given a telephone number to contact the nurse at MOPD during work hours (ie, from 8:30 A.M. to 4:30 P.M.) on weekdays for a consultation about health problems whenever needed. However, the experimental group also received the TTP which included two phases of care implemented by the researcher, an experienced advanced practice nurse (APN) in cardiac patient care.

The outpatient discharge planning phase was delivered prior to discharge from the MOPD. First, the APN established a trusting relationship with participants and family caregivers by assessing participants’ health problems; developing a care plan based on individual needs; and collaboratively implementing discharge planning with participants, family caregivers, and healthcare teams. Aside from appropriately managing symptom worsening, the APN provided participants with HF-specific education and counseling. The experimental group received an HF booklet containing information on self-monitoring, management of symptom worsening, dietary sodium control, lifestyle modification, and medication use. Lastly, the APN installed the mobile app— Tid Tam Jai (meaning follow the heart)—on participants’ mobile phones and trained them on its use.

The 6-week home telemonitoring phase began within 24 hours following discharge through the mobile app and telephone calls. The app, modified from the heart failure mHealth system with permission from the developer,44 was used as a channel for health communication and education between participants, APN, and healthcare teams. It was also used to record self-monitoring data: pulse, blood pressure, weight, and HF symptoms (ie, dyspnea, chest pain, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, fatigue, and edema). The app also included an alert system, private messaging, and bulletin board. The alert system notified the APN via email when participants failed to comply with requirements and/ or when the data showed health risks. The private messaging resource was used for HF-specific knowledge—which was submitted by the APN—and the bulletin board provided general HF-related health information for all TTP participants concomitantly. The app also included an emergency assistant for emergency telephone numbers. TTP participants were able to view current and past self-monitoring data.

The TTP group performed self-monitoring by recording their pulse, blood pressure, weight, and HF symptoms. Selfmonitoring procedures were scheduled to be recorded every morning for the first 4 weeks, and on alternating days for the last 2 weeks. Guided by the risk-classification algorithm developed by Alnosayan et al,44 self-monitored data were classified, upon receipt, into health-risk groups:normal, medium, and high risk.These risk classifications were modified whenever necessary.If any self-monitoring data changed patients’ risk level, the alert system would notify the APN, who called such participants to implement appropriate care.45 The TTP group also received infographic and private messages regarding essential information on HF-related symptom worsening signs and HF management.

Statistical analysis. SPSS 18.0 for Windows (Bangkok, Thailand) was used for data analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to describe participants’ baseline data. The independent t-test, a two-proportion Z-test, and repeated measures ANOVA were used to evaluate between-group differences in

outcome variables at 6 and 8 weeks post-intervention. Statistical significance was set at P < .05 for all data.

Ethical considerations. This trial was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee (REC.61-099-19-6), Faculty of Medicine, Prince of Songkla University, Thailand, and was registered with the Thai Clinical Trials Registry (TCTR20200418003). All eligible participants were informed about the study objectives, methods, potential risks/benefits, confidentiality, and their right to withdraw at will. All participants provided written informed consent.

# Results
Baseline demographic and health-related data were similar for both groups (P > .05) (Table 1). Participants’ mean age was 60.99 years (standard deviation (SD) = 11.63) and most were male (71.72%). Mean systolic and diastolic blood pressure was 124.81mmHg (SD=18.97) and 71.11mmHg (SD = 11.47), respectively. Additionally, the mean percentage of left ventricular ejection fraction was 35.39 (SD = 12.12). All participants were classified as either NYHA FC II (56.6%) or III (43.4%). The previously mentioned differences in demographic variables might have affected the outcomes. However, the baseline measurements showed no significant differences between the groups. The absence of statistically significant differences in the baseline assessment assured homogeneity between the groups, thereby indicating that the subject selection and group assignments were appropriate.

At baseline, there were no significant between-group differences in functional status score (t = .32, P = .752). Subsequently, the TTP group showed higher overall functional status at 6 (t=−4.16, P<.001) and 8 weeks (t=−3.51, P<.001) (Table 2). Additionally, two-way repeated-measures ANOVA showed that the mean functional status scores across the 3 time points in the TTP group were significantly different from those in the control group (F=6.17; df=1143; P=.014). Moreover, there were statistically significant effects of the interaction between group and time points on functional status (F=48.18, df=1.67, 239.23, P<.001). Namely, the change in the mean functional status scores of the TTP group across the 3 time points was significantly different from that in the control group (F=107.37, df=1.67, 239.23, P<.001).

Additionally, a pairwise within-group comparison for functional status at different time points using the Bonferroni method (Table 3) revealed significant differences in mean functional status scores of the TTP group at every time point (P < .01). Conversely, no significant difference was observed for the control group between baseline and 6 weeks postintervention (P > .05).

There was a statistically significant difference in rehospitalization rates between the control and TTP groups at 6 (z=10.69, P<.001) and 8 weeks post-intervention (z=8.36, P<.001) (Table 4). Furthermore, the TTP group’s mean satisfaction with
