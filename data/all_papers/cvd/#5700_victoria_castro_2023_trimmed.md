# METHODS
STUDY DESIGN AND OVERSIGHT. Full details of the design were previously published.6 Briefly, this was an open-label, 4-arm, parallel-group, randomized, controlled trial to evaluate the effectiveness of 3 novel digital health technologies vs usual care in improving quality of life for patients with HF. The trial was approved by the Yale School of Medicine Institutional Review Board (Yale IRB number 2000027325), was registered at clinicaltrials.gov (Evaluating Efficacy of Digital Health Technology in the Treatment of Congestive Heart Failure; NCT04394754), and conducted in accordance with the Principles of Helsinki.

PARTICIPANTS AND DATA COLLECTION. Eligible participants were adults aged 18-79 years with a current diagnosis of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) or heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF). Exclusion criteria included individuals with comorbidities that might limit their ability to use a digital health technology. In addition, patients already enrolled in another digital health study were excluded. To avoid excluding individuals with no technologic access, we provided prepaid smartphones with a data plan if necessary. Participants were also guided through e-mail account creation if they did not have an existing e-mail account.

Screening was done through the electronic health record across 11 outpatient HF clinics in the Yale–New Haven Health System. Eligible patients were approached by phone to be informed of the study, if they were interested in participating, a link to the VARA platform (Medullan) was e-mailed to them.7 While on the phone call, participants accessed the link, signed the informed consent form, and

completed a list of sociodemographic questions and a set of baseline surveys. Study coordinators assisted them during the phone call in case they had any questions or needed technologic assistance.

Surveys included the 23-item KCCQ, which evaluates physical limitations, frequency of symptoms, burden generated by symptoms, self-efficacy, quality of life, and social interference related to living with HF.8 The set of surveys also included a medication adherence survey (National Institutes of Health—Patient Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System, NIH PROMIS Medication Adherence Scale), a health literacy assessment (Brief Health Literacy Screen), and a technology literacy assessment that was developed in house

Usual care at the Yale outpatient HF clinics is based on a disease-management program where enrolled patients are followed after a hospitalization or, if they are presenting difficulties with the control of fluid volume, they are promptly prescribed the appropriate guideline-directed medical therapies (GDMTs).

Baseline demographic, comorbidity, and treatment data was collected from review of the electronic health records.12 Baseline surveys were completed by each participant after the informed consent was signed, and 90 days after randomization participants were contacted via e-mail and phone call to complete the surveys a second time. Participants randomized to an intervention arm were directed to complete an additional survey assessing usability and satisfaction with the technology (Central Illustration, top). Clinical data for secondary outcome evaluation were collected during the follow-up phone visit. The technology usability survey included 9 items. The NPS (Net Promoter Score), a measure of patient satisfaction, was calculated according to previously established methods.13 This index, which ranges from 100 to 100, measures the willingness of users to recommend a product. This was evaluated with one of the questions included in the usability survey. Patients randomized to an intervention group were encouraged, but not mandated, to use their technology regularly so as to assess real-world use and

applicability. Of note, although each technology is unique in user interface, design, and symptom assessment, the goal of this trial was not to compare technologies with one another, but rather to assess each intervention in the context of a common framework of a shared population with the baseline of comparison being usual care. Through the use of a shared control group, it was possible to evaluate

multiple technologies in a single efficient clinical trial design.

DIGITAL HEALTH INTERVENTIONS. Participants were randomized to 1 of 3 digital health interventions (Bodyport, Conversa, or Noom) or usual care (Figure 1).

Bodyport. The Bodyport Cardiac Scale is an internetenabled “smart” scale that records markers of fluid

and cardiovascular status, providing the option to personalize HF management.14,15 Bodyport has a selfcontained cellular modem so that users do not need their own internet connection. For this study, an investigational version of the scale was used along with a limited subset of biomarkers including body weight, impedance, and heart rate. Participants randomized to this technology had access to a patient dashboard, where they had the option to see their measurements and complete learning modules. A clinical dashboard allowed the study team to view measurement trends and flags that were triggered based on thresholds established for the study.

Conversa. Conversa is an automated conversational platform designed to engage patients in self-management.16 Participants engaged in an HF-oriented program designed for the study, which included brief chats with pre-scripted responses created to collect data regarding overall health, symptoms, and self-reported blood pressure (BP). Patients were also able to engage in brief post-chat education modules. Noom. Noom is a data-driven smartphone coaching application that uses evidence-based behavior change techniques to coach patients toward better self-management of their comorbidities.17 Participants of the study engaged in an HF-tailored program, with live coaching support and the option of joining a support group with other Noom users. Parameters such as self-reported BP and body weight were analyzed for the study.

Further details regarding the technologies and their implementation in this study are included in the Supplemental Methods.

ALERTING PROVIDERS. When health parameters provided by each of the technologies reached predetermined thresholds indicative of worsening clinical status, technology-specific alerts appeared as red flag warnings in each of the study coordinator–facing technology dashboards. The research team conveyed these warnings to health care providers at each of the clinics daily and suggested (but did not mandate) that the provider make a check-in call to the patient to receive further details about their signs and symptoms relating to HF. The outcomes of those calls and any subsequent changes in care management were collected via provider response and direct chart review.

OUTCOMES. The primary outcome was the change in quality of life as assessed by the KCCQ Overall Summary Score at 90 days after randomization. Secondary outcomes included emergency department (ED) visits, hospital admissions, rates of GDMT use, acute kidney disease development, clinic noshow rates, and mortality. Clinical efficiency was evaluated by assessing the following metrics during the patient’s time in the study: number of clinic visits per patient (in-person and virtual clinic visits), number of remote device checks, number of calls made to clinic with questions for a provider, and number of calls made by a provider to a patient for check-in, explanation of test results, or appointment reminders. Usage metrics outcomes corresponded to patient usability, ease of use, engagement, and understanding of the assigned digital technology (Supplemental Table 1).

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS. Demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were obtained from electronic health records review. Continuous variables are presented as median (IQR) and were compared using the van Elteren test for nonparametric variables and stratified Student’s t-test for normally distributed variables to account for clustering by clinic site. Categoric variables are presented as proportions and were compared with the Mantel-Haenszel chi-square test. For the primary analysis, a mixed-effects model using a random intercept was used, comparing change in KCCQ at 90 days in each of the intervention groups vs the control group. In this model, there was no adjustment for baseline characteristics. All analyses used the intention-to-treat principle, and individual interventions were not compared with each other. The KCCQ composite score ranges from 0 to 100 (with 100 being the best possible score), and the study was powered to detect a change of 10 points as a large and clinically important difference. To detect the difference of a 10-point change in the KCCQ, we estimated that each arm of the trial had to include 40 patients across all clinics for 90% power at an alpha threshold of 0.017 (to account for the 3 comparisons of technologic interventions to usual care). F test of the treatment effect was used to calculate the sample size, which was inflated to 50 individuals per arm to account for loss to follow-up and noncompletion of surveys. Owing to time constraints, the enrollment was terminated when each intervention arm included 46 participants, giving a total enrollment of 182 individuals. Statistical analyses were conducted with the use of Stata version 17, and a value of P < 0 . 0 1 7was considered to be statistically significant for the primary outcome. Secondary outcomes were evaluated at P < 0 . 0 5and as such should be considered exploratory and hypothesis generating.

# RESULTS
BASELINE CHARACTERISTICS. From September 2020 to March 2021, 2,144 patients were screened and assessed for eligibility. During this process, 660 (30.8%) met the inclusion criteria and were contacted through phone calls, e-mails, pre-scripted text messages or electronic health record communications. Of these, 183 (27.7%) agreed to participate in the study; 182 completed their baseline set of surveys and were randomized to 1 of the 4 groups (Figure 2). Baseline characteristics were similar across the groups (Table 1), 37.9% participants were female, and 33.5% were non-Hispanic Black. Median age was 61 years (IQR: 53-69 years). Baseline characteristics also

showed that 70.3% had HFrEF, 103 (56.6%) were in NYHA functional class III, 131 (71.9%) had hypertension, 76 (41.8%) had diabetes mellitus, and 70 (38.5%) had obesity. In addition, 164 (90.1%) were on betablockers and 130 (71.4%) on angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs), or angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors (ARNIs).

PRIMARY OUTCOME. Among the 182 participants randomized in the study, 151 (83%) completed their 90-day surveys. The median KCCQ Overall Summary Score did not change significantly over the 90-day period for any intervention group (Bodyport þ2.1 [IQR: 7.8 to 14.6], Conversa 2.1 [IQR: 9.1 to 6.8], Noom 3.4 [IQR: 2.8 to 15.1]), and no technology led to an improvement beyond usual care ( 0.3 [IQR: 9.2 to 9.4]). Regarding the KCCQ Self-Efficacy Score, which highlights the patient’s confidence and competence in managing their HF, there was also no evidence of significant improvement in any of the technology groups compared with usual care. However, symptoms at 90 days, as assessed by the KCCQ Total Symptom Score, were significantly improved in the Noom group when compared to control ( 4.2 points [IQR: 1 to 16.7] vs 1 points [IQR: 13.5 to 7.8], respectively; P 0.006) (Table 2). In addition, the change in the Clinical Summary Score was higher in the Noom group than in usual care ( 2.8 points [IQR: 1 to 14.6] vs 3.1 points [IQR: 10.2 to 3]; P 0.002) (Central Illustration, bottom). There was no significant change in health literacy, technology literacy, or medication adherence scores in any of the technology groups vs usual care (Table 2).

Median baseline and 90-day survey scores by randomization group are presented in Supplemental Table 2.

SECONDARY OUTCOMES. The frequencies of ED visits and hospitalizations, as well as visits and admissions related to HF, were similar across the study arms, with no statistically significant differences found when compared with the control group (Table 3). At 90-day follow-up, 164 participants (90.1%) were on beta-blockers and 131 (71.9%) on ACE inhibitors, ARBs, or ARNIs, and there were no significant differences in rates of participants using GDMT between the different groups of technologies when compared with the usual care group. Eighteen (9.9%) of all the enrolled participants developed acute kidney disease, 42 (23.1%) did not show for a HF clinic visit during their follow-up period, and 1 participant

from Conversa group died before reaching 90-day follow-up. Clinical efficiency outcomes are presented in Table 3.

ADHERENCE METRICS. During the 90 days of followup, 43 participants (93.5%) in the Bodyport group used the technology at least once. The median use of the smart scale was 53.3% of days (IQR: 30%-83.3%). Twenty-seven participants (58.7%) accessed the Bodyport patient dashboard at least once. In the Conversa group, 33 participants (71.7%) engaged at least once with the conversational platform. Participants randomized to Conversa were encouraged to

interact with the platform once a week. The median use of the conversational platform was 14.4% of days (IQR: 10%-15.6%), and of the participants who used the technology, 11 (33.3%) interacted with it at least once a week over the complete course of the study. Twenty-four participants (52.2%) in the Noom group used the app at least once during their 90 days of follow-up, and of those who used the application, the median use was 43.9% of days (IQR: 7.8%-95.6%). Mean interactions per week with each of the technologies are shown in Supplemental Figure 1. Results of post hoc analysis evaluating the change in quality of life by KCCQ in participants that used the

technology at least once during their follow-up period and completed their 90-day surveys are presented in Supplemental Table 3.

Most participants indicated that they would recommend the technologies to a friend in a similar situation. From the Bodyport group, 39 (84.8%) reported a median of 10 (IQR: 8-10) when asked how

likely they would recommend the technology on a 0-10 scale. From the Conversa group, 38 (82.6%) reported a median of 9 (IQR: 8-10), and from the Noom group, 32 (69.6%) reported a median of 8.5 (IQR: 5-9). The NPS were 69.2 for Bodyport, 57.9 for Conversa, and 10.5 for Noom. Of the participants randomized to Bodyport, 76.1% agreed that this technology was easy

to use, compared with 60.9% of the participants in the Conversa group and 23.9% in the Noom group. Also, in the Bodyport group 50% of participants agreed that the technology helped them to engage better with and feel more in control of their health condition, vs 45.7% of the group randomized to Conversa and 28.3% of the group randomized to Noom.

TECHNOLOGY-SPECIFIC ALERTS. Technology-specific thresholds that generated red flag warnings are presented in Table 4. A total of 126 alerts were generated among participants randomized to a digital health technology. Ninety-nine alerts (78.6%) were from participants randomized to Bodyport, 23 (18.3%) from participants randomized to Conversa, and 4 (3.2%) from participants randomized to Noom.
