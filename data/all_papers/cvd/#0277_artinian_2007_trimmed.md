# Methods
## Design

A randomized, two-group, experimental, longitudinal design was used. The treatment group received nurse-managed TM and the control group received enhanced UC. Data were collected at baseline and 3-, 6-, and 12-month follow-ups. Follow-up periods were chosen to allow some comparison of findings from this study with those from other TM studies that have been reported in the literature (Artinian et al., 2001; Gerin et al., 1998; Mengden et al., 2004).

Because imbalances in the number of participants assigned to each group could occur with simple randomization, block stratified randomization for antihypertensive medication use was performed to ensure an equal number of participants taking antihypertensive medications in each

group. It was important to avoid an imbalance in the number of participants taking antihypertensive medications in one of the groups because an imbalance could confound the effects of the TM intervention.

Sequentially numbered computer-generated randomization assignments were determined prior to the start of data collection, but participants were not notified of the group assignment until after baseline data were collected. To keep data collectors blinded to group assignment, the study’s project manager informed participants of their group assignment by mail or telephone within a week of their baseline interview. Data collectors were trained not to ask participants about group assignment and to ask participants not to reveal their assignment to them. Randomization was done individually within each data collection site to ensure balancing for sites and to ensure that all sites had a fair opportunity to try both interventions. The Wayne State University Human Investigation Committee approved the study protocol. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

## Setting and Sample

African Americans with hypertension were recruited through free BP screenings offered at community centers, thrift stores, drug stores, and grocery stores located on the east side of Detroit. Because the classification of BP is based on at least the average of two or more properly measured, seated BP readings on each of two or more visits, participants were screened for study eligibility three times. The first screening was used to determine if they met the BP inclusion criterion. The second screening, about a week later, was used to determine if individuals met all other eligibility criteria, including the BP criterion. Individuals were screened a third time immediately prior to the baseline interview to verify continued uncontrolled BP.

Inclusion criteria were Q18 years of age; SBP Q140 mm Hg or DBP Q90 mm Hg, unless self-identified as a diabetic or with a history of chronic kidney disease, then SBP Q130 mm Hg or DBP Q80 mm Hg; access to a landbased telephone in own residence (owned or rented); oriented to person, time, and place; English speaking; and intent to remain in Detroit for the next year. Exclusion criteria were arm circumference 917.5 in.; history of dementia, mental illness, terminal cancer, advanced liver disease, or hemodialysis; and self-reported illicit drug use or alcohol abuse as measured by the CAGE (Cut, Annoyed, Guilty, Eye-opener) questions (Buchsbaum, Buchanan, Centor, Schnoll, & Lawton, 1991). Eliminating participants with an arm circumference 917.5 in., thus eliminating individuals likely to be hypertensive, was an unavoidable study limitation due to the unavailability of a proper cuff size to allow accurate BP measurement.

## Intervention: Enhanced UC

Enhanced UC for participants included visits to their primary care provider scheduled at intervals requested by the primary care provider and influenced by the participant’s level of adherence to keeping appointments. Participants who did not have a primary care provider were provided with a list of locations where they could obtain a provider and free or low-cost healthcare. Participants who

could not afford their medications were enrolled in a pharmacy assistance program. Participants also received an AHA pamphlet, Silent Stalker: Our Guide to High Blood Pressure, that identifies causes of HBP, describes how it is diagnosed, and suggests strategies to reduce risk and control HBP.

## Intervention: Nurse-Managed TM

Participants in the TM group received UC plus nursemanaged TM. Specially trained registered nurses delivered the intervention. During a prescheduled appointment, the intervention nurse delivered the BP monitor and TM link device (device that links BP monitor to the telephone) to the participant’s home. At the time of the home visit, an intervention nurse taught participants how to self-monitor BP in accordance with The Seventh Report of the Joint National Committee on Prevention, Detection, Evaluation, and Treatment of High Blood Pressure (JNC-VII) guidelines (Chobanian et al., 2003), set up the home TM system, demonstrated the system, had participants practice using the BP monitor, and answered questions. Given the memory in the BP monitor and that all BPs recorded by the monitor were telephonically sent to care providers and the principal investigator, participants received verbal and written reminders that the BP monitor was exclusively for their use and that no one other than them should use the monitor. The intervention nurse returned to the participant’s home within 24 to 48 hours of the initial home visit for a followup demonstration and to answer additional questions.

LifeLink Monitoring, Inc. (Bearsville, NY) provided TM services for this study. Between baseline and 12-month follow-up, participants were asked to measure their BP three times a week in the morning before they had taken any antihypertension medication. From the digital reading on the automated BP monitor, participants knew their BP reading after each BP measurement.

Telemonitoring participants were also asked to telephonically send their BP readings to the intervention nurse and their care providers once a week during the first 3 months of the study, then once a month between the 4- and 12-month follow-ups. To send stored readings, participants connected their BP monitors to a BPLink Communicator (the Link) and pressed a single button. The Link, which connected to the participant’s telephone, automatically dialed a toll-free number and connected to a server at LifeLink’s headquarters. The server uploaded the stored readings, computed average BPs that could be compared with the target goal pressure, and formatted reports that were sent to the primary care providers and principal investigator by e-mail or fax. The target goal was set at 135/85 mm Hg because persons with an average BP more than 135/85 mm Hg measured at home are generally considered to be hypertensive (Chobanian et al., 2003).

Once the intervention nurses received the BP reports, they telephoned each participant to provide feedback in relation to the target goals and to provide telecounseling about lifestyle modification and medication adherence in accordance with JNC-VII guidelines (Chobanian et al., 2003). Initially, telecounseling calls were more frequent to assist participants to learn hypertension self-care behaviors and incorporate them into their daily routine or to establish

them as habit; calls were gradually weaned away from participants during the follow-up period. Telecounseling calls were made weekly between baseline and 3-month follow-up, monthly between 4 and 6 months, and then once at 8 months. Call attempts were made at varying times of the day and on varying days, including weekend days. For each week, five call attempts were made before the call was considered missed.Do

## Data Collectiond

Most of the data were collected during 2-hour structuredfr face-to-face interviews and brief physical exams, which were conducted by trained interviewers in a private room at one of the project-affiliated neighborhood community centers. Mailed postcards provided interview appointment reminders 1 week before the scheduled interview; telephonel call reminders were made the evening before the interview..co

On average, 22% of the interviews needed to be rescheduled due to no-shows or cancellations despite these reminders. Participants were compensated $25.00 after the completion of each interview. Postinterview telephone calls were also used to follow-up on missing data and to acquire information about interim medication changes.

# Outcome Measurement
The outcome measure of BP was measured using an electronic BP monitor (Omron HEM-737 Intellisense, Omron Healthcare, Inc., Vernon Hills, IL) that has been validated in accordance with the criteria of the British Hypertension Society and the Association for the Advancement of Medical Instrumentation (Dabl Educational Trust, 2005). Blood pressure was measured after a 5-minute rest period; at least two BPs were measured, and the average of all was used for analyses. Participants wore unrestrictive

clothing and sat next to the interviewer’s table, their feet on the floor; their back supported; and their arm abducted, slightly flexed, and supported at heart level by the smooth, firm surface of a table.

## Data Analysis

Analyses were conducted using SAS Version 9.1 (SAS, Cary, NC) and SPSS Version 14.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL). The significance level was set at .05. Analysis of intention to treat was conducted to preserve the baseline comparability between groups achieved by random assignment. Because deviation from the original randomized groups can contaminate the intervention comparison, participants were analyzed according to the assigned intervention (thus ignoring nonadherence to intervention protocol and withdrawal), not the actual intervention received. Descriptive statistics were used to analyze all study variables. Independent t tests and chi-square statistics were used to compare baseline characteristics between the TM and enhanced UC groups. Cross-tabs, repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA), and independent t tests were used to analyze the study’s hypothesis.

# Results
## Description of the Sample

Between June 2002 and December 2004, 394 participants were recruited to participate in the study. Seven participants who did not self-identify as African American were excluded from these analyses. Recruitment and participation in the trial are described in Figure 3. The 3-month sample size was lower compared with the baseline and 6- and 12-month sample sizes. Between baseline and the 3-month follow-up, 22% of the interviews needed to be rescheduled due to no-shows or cancellations. Despite two full-time interviewers, the number of scheduled and rescheduled interviews resulted in an inability to schedule all interviews within the appropriate window of time. Participants who missed their 3-month interview were picked up again at the next follow-up. Over the 12-month study period, the attrition rate was 13%.

The baseline demographic and health characteristics between the enhanced UC and nurse-managed TM groups are compared in Table 1. There were no significant demographic or health characteristic differences between the two study groups, except gender and household density. Regardless of treatment assignment, those living alone had a lower DBP, but living alone did not contribute to any treatment group differences in BP. Repeated measures ANOVA with two between-subjects factors (gender and household density) showed no impact on treatment group differences in BP.

Given the premise that UC was enhanced and this group experienced a type of intervention, an alternate tact for analysis was taken. Using the UC sample as a standard or exemplar of what would be expected with minimal intervention, a linear regression was computed to predict, for DBP and SBP separately, 12-month readings based on baseline readings. The equations were then applied to the TM group. To test the null hypothesis that participation in TM contributes nothing to BP reduction, the predicted outcome measures were compared with obtained outcome measures in the TM group using repeated measures ANOVA, with TM participation as a between-subjects effect. Residuals (12-month obtained minus 12-month predicted) were computed. A negative residual indicates an outcome less than expected, or a decrease in BP; a positive residual indicates an outcome greater than expected, or an increase in BP. A one-sample t test (one tailed, as it is hypothesized that TM would show significant improvement over the UC sample) was used to test the difference between predicted residuals and the expected mean of zero for the TM group. The results were as expectedVthe mean residual was both negative and significantly different from zero, suggesting that the TM group improved more than expected (Table 4).
