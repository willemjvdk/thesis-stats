## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# Methods
## Study Design

This pilot feasibility study used a randomized 3-group repeated-measures design, enhanced usual care, and 2 intervention arms (mHealth and mHealth+). Before the start of the study, institutional review board approval was received.

The mHealth app (Play-It Health) was customized for our study and used to deliver intervention components. Theoretical underpinnings were based on patient activation as conceptualized by Hibbard and colleagues14 and included the activating components of knowledge, self-monitoring skills, and self-efficacy/confidence. Intervention components are described in Table 1. A Bluetooth scale was integrated into the app. Patients were encouraged to access the app daily to weigh and record medications. A daily HF-related informational educational tip as well as reminders to weigh, take medications, and attend scheduled appointments were sent via the app. Patients in the intervention groups received $10 per month for active participation in the interventions. All groups received an additional $10 for each outcome call. All patients received standard of care from the enrolling institution that included evidence-based HF education including an HF resource booklet, HF videos, pharmacist and nutritionist

consultation, and reinforcement phone calls with the HF educator during the first month post discharge.

## Enhanced Usual Care

Patients in the enhanced usual care group were provided training on the app for recording medications and weights. All app reminders and educational tips were disabled. Patients were asked to record daily weights and medications in the app.

## Mobile Health

Patients in the mHealth group were given additional app features: reminders to weigh, take medications, and attend appointments. Intervention components within the app included education, confidence building, self-monitoring, and self-efficacy.

## Mobile Health Plus

In addition, patients in the mHealth+ group received virtual visits with an nurse practitioner/community health worker team via the Zoom (Zoom Video Communications, Inc, San Jose, California) platform for the first 2 months of the 3-month intervention. The nurse practitioner virtual visits occurred at weeks 2, 5, and 8, whereas the community health worker virtual visits occurred at weeks 1, 3, 4, and 6. Strategies used by the nurse practitioner and community health worker were aimed at improving patient activation (knowledge, selfmonitoring skills, confidence, and self-efficacy). The nurse practitioner focused on problem solving and decision making to enhance self-monitoring skill building and symptom management, specifically red flag symptoms (eg, shortness of breath and lower extremity edema). The nurse practitioner developed self-management strategies with the patient, and the community health worker

reinforced these strategies. The community health worker also focused on community resources and barriers to adherence. To promote engagement, reminders were sent before the virtual visit. A full description of the mHealth app and intervention is detailed in another manuscript submitted for publication (Schmaderer et al, unpublished data, January 2021).

## Sample and Setting

Subjects were recruited from inpatient and observational units at Nebraska Medicine Hospital. Of the 80 patients who consented, 6 became ineligible (eg, discharged to an extended care facility, received home healthcare, and implanted with a ventricular assist device). The sample included 74 patients with HF (Figure 1). Patients were included if they were adults (19 years and older) and currently hospitalized with a primary HF diagnosis or an episode of acute decompensated HF. Patients were discharged to home, had access to smart technology (phone or tablet) with Internet access or a phone plan large enough for daily messages and monitoring activities, and were able to hear, speak, and read English. Patients were excluded if they had dementia (Montreal Cognitive Assessment score < 17) or if they had a ventricular assist device. Patients were randomized to enhanced usual care, mHealth, or mHealth+ using a randomization schedule prepared in advance by the biostatistician.

## Procedures

The HF educators, who were employees of Nebraska Medicine and had ethical access to the patients, identified potential participants. After screening for study

inclusion and exclusion criteria, the HF educators asked potential study participants if they were interested in our research study. Ongoing daily communication between research personnel (principal investigator and graduate assistants) and HF educators assisted in identifying potential patients for the study. A spreadsheet of all patients with HF included an anticipated discharge plan (home or facility), access to mobile device, and reasons for declining the study. Research personnel thoroughly explained the study and obtained written informed consent. After consent and randomization, research personnel guided patients through installation of the app and connection of the Bluetooth scale on their smart device. Participants practiced using the app for weighing and recording medications, and the mHealth+ participants practiced virtual visits before hospital discharge. The intervention was started when the patient discharged from the hospital. Baseline data were collected in person after consent and before discharge. Outcome data were collected at month 3 via phone calls.

To ensure treatment fidelity, the principal investigator trained and supervised all research personnel. One nurse practitioner was used for all virtual visits during the study;

5 different community health workers were used for the virtual visits. The principal investigator used a protocol guide for consistent training of all research personnel. To ensure intervention fidelity, the principal investigator monitored virtual visits via Zoom with the nurse practitioner and community health worker monthly. REDCap was used for consistent data collection and documentation of virtual visits.

# Outcome Variables/Measures
## Symptom Assessment

The Heart Failure Symptom Survey is a 14-item clinical symptom assessment that measures both physical and psychological perception of symptoms associated with HF. The Heart Failure Symptom Survey was administered because it was designed specifically for patients with HF and contains common symptoms experienced by most patients with HF. Patients report their subjective perception of shortness of breath at rest; shortness of breath with activity; shortness of breath when lying down; shortness of breath when waking at night; swelling in the feet, ankles, or legs (lower extremity edema) and abdomen;

fatigue; chest pressure or heaviness; irregular heartbeat; cough; dizziness; difficulty sleeping; forgetfulness; and depression. The Heart Failure Symptom Survey was adapted for our study to get a better representation of clinical assessment of shortness of breath; all shortness of breath variables (shortness of breath with activity, shortness of breath at rest, shortness of breath when lying down, and shortness of breath when waking at night) were combined, leaving 11 possible HF symptoms. Averages of the severity and frequency (severity/ frequency) of the 11 symptoms were calculated. In this study, the 11 items' (shortness of breath symptoms combined) Cronbach α for frequency was 0.87 and severity was 0.87. This clinical assessment was not designed to get total symptom experience or a measurable construct but to capture individual perceptions of symptoms.

## Health-Related Quality of Life

The EuroQol-5 Dimension (EQ-5D) is a 2-part instrument: a 5-item well-being index and a visual analog scale that assesses health-related quality of life. The well-being index consists of a weighted sum of 5 dimensions by rating the amount of difficulty with mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/ depression. This scale includes a simple descriptive profile of performance, ranked as follows: no problem, slight problem, moderate, severe problem, and unable to perform. It also has 1 item that rates health on a visual analog scale of 0 to 100 (100 being the best health) for a single index value for health status. The EQ-5D has been shown to be valid and reliable in several chronic populations and several different countries.15 Cronbach α for this study was 0.77 at baseline and 0.83 at 3 months.

## Healthcare Utilization

Medical records were reviewed for health care utilization (emergency department visits and hospitalization) that occurred from hospital discharge to 3 months. Healthcare utilization was collected only if the patient used the index hospitalization organization for their healthcare. It was calculated for the entire 3-month time and recorded as a frequency and a percentage. Emergency department visits that resulted in hospitalization were counted as a hospitalization rather than an emergency department visit.

## Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported as counts (%) and means. Instruments were scored according to the published scoring algorithms. The incidence for aim 1 was calculated as the percentage of patients who stated they experienced the symptom at baseline. Only patients who responded to a symptom or health-related quality of life measure at a specific time point were included in these analyses; therefore, the number reported at each time point varied for aim 2. Change scores were

calculated by subtracting the baseline score from the month 3 score. To determine which intervention had a bigger effect, effect sizes were approximated with Cohen d and calculated with means and standard deviations for the enhanced usual care and mHealth groups and the enhanced usual care and mHealth+ groups. The health care utilization for aim 3 was calculated as counts and analyzed with Fisher exact tests. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 27.0) assuming a type I error of 5%.

# Results
A total of 74 patients who had been admitted for HF met inclusion criteria and were enrolled in the study. Specifically, by groups, there were 26 patients in the enhanced usual care group, 23 patients in the mHealth group, and 25 patients in the mHealth+ group. There were no significant differences in demographic or clinical variables by group, so the sample is reported as the total group (Table 2). The sample had a mean age of 56.3 years, with a range of 26 to 91 years. More than half were female (40, 54%), and half (37) had an educational level of high school or lower. In relation to race, more than half (41, 55.4%) were White and 25 (34%) were Black. Regarding employment status, more patients were employed (35, 47%) versus unemployed (30, 41%) or (9, 12%) retired. Annual income less than $40 000 was reported by more than half of patients (40, 54%), and 14 (19%) reported income less than

$10 000. Most had either private insurance (26, 35%) or Medicare (20, 27%); however, approximately one-fourth of the patients (19, 26%) were uninsured, and 5 (7%) were Medicaid beneficiaries. Most patients, 32 (43%), were NYHA class III, whereas 16 (22%) were NYHA class IV. Most patients (54, 73%) had reduced ejection fraction (<50%), and 8 (16%) had a pacemaker or other implantable cardiac device.

## Symptoms

At baseline, the most common symptoms in all groups were shortness of breath, fatigue, difficulty sleeping, bloated feeling in the abdomen (bloating), and lower extremity edema. Specifically, at baseline, 95% of the patients with HF in all groups experienced shortness of breath and 93% experienced fatigue. Bloating and difficulty sleeping were experienced in 78% of the patients, and 77% experienced lower extremity edema. Six symptoms (dizziness or lightheadedness, worsening cough, forgetfulness, chest pressure, irregular heartbeat, and depression) were also very common and experienced in a range of 54% to 65% of patients (Figure 2).

## Symptom Severity/Frequency

To determine whether there were differences in severity/ frequency by group, only patients who responded to a symptom at 3 months were included in these analyses. Of the 11 symptoms, the mHealth+ group reported having less severity/frequency in shortness of breath, lower extremity edema, fatigue, chest pressure, irregular

heartbeat, and sleep. For these symptoms, the mHealth + group had the lowest mean scores compared with the enhanced usual care and mHealth groups at 3 months. The symptoms most indicative of worsening HF (shortness of breath and lower extremity edema) showed some clinical relevance for differences by group (Figure 3). The mHealth group had lesser severity/frequency in worsening cough, difficulty sleeping, and forgetfulness. The enhanced usual care group reported less severity/frequency in bloating, dizziness, and depression. These symptoms are not as specific to HF as more common symptoms such as shortness of breath and edema, and patients may have had difficulty discerning these symptoms to be indicative of HF because they are general

and common in other chronic diseases. For all groups, most symptom severity/frequency decreased over time.

## Symptom Change Scores

The clinically relevant and frequent symptoms for HF, as depicted in Table 3, show the most potential for efficacy with the self-management intervention (mHealth and mHealth+). At 3 months, shortness of breath shows the most promise for efficacy for both intervention groups with medium effect sizes (d range of 0.60–0.55). Lower extremity edema had small to medium effect sizes (d range of 0.32–0.51). Smaller effects were noted for irregular heartbeat in both mHealth groups (d range of

0.25–0.52) and for chest pressure in the mHealth group (d = 0.15). The enhanced usual care group had greater change in bloating (d range of 0.36–0.40), fatigue (d range of 0.01–0.33), worsening cough (d range of 0.47–0.25), and dizziness (d range of 0.42–0.89) compared with both intervention groups.

## Health-Related Quality of Life (EuroQol-5 Dimension)

For health-related quality of life, there were no significant differences by group in EQ-5D index or visual analog scale at baseline. In addition, there were no

between-group significant differences on the EQ-5D index or visual analog scale at 3 months; however, the EQ-5D visual analog scale scores showed a trend for improvement at month 3 for the mHealth and mHealth + groups (Figure 4). In relation to change scores and effect sizes, the EQ5D visual analog scale had a small effect (d = 0.32) in the mHealth+ group compared with enhanced usual care.

## Healthcare Utilization

## Emergency Department Visits

There were 59 emergency department visits from 24 patients in all groups during the first 90 days after hospital discharge. Several patients had more than 1 emergency department visit. In the enhanced usual care group, 10 of 26 patients (38%) had 22 emergency department visits. In the mHealth group, 9 of 23 patients (39%) had 28 emergency department visits, and in the mHealth+ group, 5 of 25 patients (20%) had 9 emergency department visits (Figure 5). Of the total emergency department visits, 37 of 59 or 63% resulted in a rehospitalization. All emergency department visits that were related to HF symptoms resulted in a hospitalization.

## Hospitalizations

There were 57 hospital readmissions from 30 patients in all groups during the 3-month period. Of those in the enhanced usual care group, 13 of 26 patients

(50%) had 21 hospitalizations. In the mHealth group, 13 of 23 patients (56%) had 28 hospitalizations, and in the mHealth+ group, 4 of 25 patients (16%) had 8 hospitalizations (Figure 5). The mHealth+ group had significantly less rehospitalizations than the enhanced usual care group (Fisher exact test statistic = 0.017, P = .02).

# Emergency Department Visits That Did Not Result in Hospitalization
Patient emergency department visits that resulted in a hospitalization were counted as a rehospitalization rather than an emergency department visit; thus, 12 different patients visited the emergency department and were discharged without hospital admission: 5 of 26 (19%) in the enhanced usual care group, 4 of 23 (17%) in the mHealth group, and 3 of 25 (12%) in the mHealth+ group in the first 3-month period. These counts were not significantly different.
