## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# METHODS
Hypertension and home monitoring experience (HoME) study was a proofof-concept randomized clinical trial conducted to evaluate an automated communication program supporting systematic HBPM. Enrollment began in September 2018 and data collection was completed in August 2019. The trial was approved by the University of Pittsburgh Investigational Review board and was registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT03650166). Individuals who completed the study received a $40 debit card and kept the home BP monitor provided for the study.

## Recruitment and participants

Investigators and staff met with several community primary care practices in and surrounding the city of Pittsburgh to explain the study and solicit their involvement. A research study recruiter then worked onsite with clinical and administrative staff to identify potentially eligible participants. In-person screening, verification of eligibility, and study explanation followed. Signed consent for study procedures and permission to access their medical records was obtained from eligible volunteers.

Participants were required to be community-dwelling adults 55 years of age or older whose most recent office systolic BP was ≥150 mm Hg, who had a mobile device and at least occasional internet access. Office BP readings are taken from the electronic health record. Participants were given the option of having the text messages sent to their spouses or other caregivers. This design feature allows a significant other to assist in HBPM. In those instances, signed consent was also obtained from the caregiver. At enrollment, participants received the study home BP monitor (A&D Medical UA-767FAC; San Jose, CA) [27] and usage demonstration with oral and written instruction for proper HBPM technique. If necessary, participants were instructed on basic texting.

During the week following enrollment, two phone calls were completed with the participant in order to collect baseline questionnaire data and four baselines phone-supervised home BP readings (see below). Based upon the average of the four readings, participants were classified as having either sustained hypertension (baseline home systolic BP ≥ 135 or diastolic BP ≥ 85) or white-coat hypertension (baseline home BP < 135/ 85 mm Hg).

## Randomization

At the end of the second baseline BP phone call, participants were randomized to active intervention (MyBP plus a home BP cuff) or treatment-as-usual (home BP cuff alone). A 2:1 ratio was used to permit the evaluation of heterogeneous responses to MyBP. Randomization to intervention or control conditions was completed using computergenerated, random permuted blocks within strata using a block size of 6 (e.g., I-C-I-I-I-C), and stratification by sustained/white-coat hypertension and presence/absence of a caregiver. Allocation concealment was achieved by enclosing intervention assignments in opaque envelopes, numerically ordered and sealed. The study statistician (JY) generated the allocation sequence, study staff enrolled participants (JE), and a clinical investigator assigned participants (MM).

## Intervention

Upon randomization, intervention group participants received oral and mailed instructions, as well as a refrigerator magnet diagram summarizing how to interact with MyBP. Participants were instructed to watch six educational videos focused on the basics of hypertension and HBPM techniques accessed through the study website (Hypertension v2.0 by Emmi Solutions, LLC; online Supplement). Each participant chose days and times for two weekly morning BP readings and two weekly evening readings.

As scheduled, the program’s automated, bi-directional SMS system prompted participants to take a BP reading and to text it back. Immediately following each submitted reading, MyBP replied with their updated BP average. When participants reported an extremely high value, defined as systolic BP ≥ 180 mm Hg or greater, or a diastolic BP ≥ 110, they received an automated message advising them to sit for 2 min and recheck their blood pressure. If the reading was still extreme on resubmission, participants were advised to contact their provider or to call 911 if they were having new symptoms such as chest pain or a severe headache. Study staff periodically reviewed participant activity via a front-end database. In instances of low engagement (e.g., 0–1 BP reading over 2 weeks) or unsolicited text submissions, study staff called to review the basic functions of the program and to conduct general trouble-shooting. If participants submitted readings outside of the scheduled time windows, they were reminded to submit readings only at their chosen times and offered the option of changing their schedule.

Monitoring was divided into successive 2-week periods during which eight mornings and evening BP readings were requested. This was based upon research showing that between four and eight readings sampled over several days generate a reliable average BP [28, 29]. At the end of each 2-week monitoring period, participants received a summary report via SMS including their adherence to BP prompts, current 2-week BP average, and change in BP since the previous 2-week period. Program instructions and recurring feedback encouraged participants to submit at least four of eight requested BP readings each week.

Following each 2-week BP report, participants were given the option of continuing the monitoring protocol or taking a 2-week break. This was an intentional design feature to increase patient autonomy and was theorized to be important for long-term engagement. In addition, every 2 weeks the program sent a hypertension tip or control strategy (e.g., Did you know… A BP medicine start working within 1–2 days and stop working within 1–2 days after quitting. So, it’s important to take them every day.). Every month, MyBP sent a report to the provider via automated fax. We chose fax delivery because at the time of the trial this modality of information exchange was still common in primary care offices. The reports contained a table of average BP, BP range, and the number of readings submitted for each 2- week period (e.g., 6 of 8).

Participants assigned to the control condition received the home BP monitor with basic operating instructions. No advice was given regarding BP monitoring.

## Baseline measures

Sociodemographic and medical variables. Demographics and medical information were gathered from participants and through review of a review of electronic medical records. Gait speed over five meters was measured to assess physiologic aging, as it may be related to the use of mHealth technologies [30].

Mobile phone utilization. At baseline, each participant reported how often they use text messages and completed the Mobile Device Proficiency Questionnaire [31].

Co-morbidities, drug therapy, and adverse events. We reviewed the electronic medical record for each participant at baseline to assess medical comorbidities and current antihypertensive medications. At study completion, this review was repeated and we documented major cardiovascular events, emergency department evaluations, and hospitalizations since randomization. Antihypertensive pharmacotherapy intensity was calculated based on a number of medications and relative dose, as described by Wan et al. [32].

# Feasibility and engagement outcomes
Utilization of and engagement with MyBP. Engagement with the MyBP program was operationally defined as adherence to BP reminders. During

each 2-week period, the MyBP program sent eight SMS prompts. The proportion of SMS BP prompts to which the participant responded with a BP reading was calculated for each 2-week period and also averaged across periods for assessment of overall engagement. Because we encouraged participants to respond to at least four prompts, engagement was also defined as submission of at least 4 BP readings during each 2- week period. No prompts were sent during periods when the participant elected to take a 2-week break; these breaks were not included in engagement calculations.

Exit interview. At the end of the intervention period, participants assigned to MyBP underwent a telephone-administered, semi-structured interview asking open-ended questions about usability and usefulness of the MyBP program, and how it may have affected their perceptions, confidence, or behaviors surrounding any aspect of hypertension self-management. Analyses of the interviews will be conducted and reported separately.

# Exploratory efficacy outcomes
Hypertension self-efficacy. As shown in Table 2, overall hypertension self-efficacy improved in the intervention group compared to controls. Answers to other questions about hypertension selfmanagement were not significantly affected by group assignment.

Blood pressure and pharmacotherapy. BP was collected in all subjects using multiple, telephone-supervised readings. As reported in Table 3, systolic and diastolic BP declined by an

average of 12 (SD = 17) and 5 (SD = 7) mm Hg, respectively, and did not differ between the two treatment groups. Based on linear regression analysis adjusting for baseline BP MyBP effect estimates [95% CI] on SBP and DBP were, respectively, −3.4 [−11.5,4.6] and 0.3 [−3.7,4.4]. For systolic BP, regression analyses revealed a significant interaction between baseline systolic BP and change in systolic BP by group assignment (interaction effect estimate [95% CI] −0.56 [−0.96,−0.17]). As shown in Fig. 3, higher baseline systolic BP was associated with a greater decline in participants assigned to MyBP. In robust regression analyses conducted to mitigate potential violations in assumptions, this moderation effect was less precise (−0.56 [−1.15,0.03]). No significant moderation was noted for diastolic BP (interaction effect estimate from robust regression −0.29 [−0.69,0.11]). In the intervention group, there was no association between program adherence and decline in BP (0.06 [−0.12,0.23]).

At study completion, the number of medications had not changed in either the intervention and control groups (mean [SD] −0.17 [0.75] and −0.48 [0.75], respectively; effect estimate 0.35 [−0.12,0.82]). Similarly, pharmacotherapy intensity at study completion did not differ in intervention and control participants (mean [SD] 0.01 [0.40] and −0.04 [0.65], respectively; effect estimate 0.05 [−0.24,0.34]).

## Safety and adverse events

A systolic BP ≥ 180, diastolic BP ≥ 110, or both were reported 57 times (1.4%), and a systolic BP < 90 mm Hg was reported three times (0.7%). There were no myocardial infarctions, strokes, revascularization procedures, or deaths but there were three instances of evaluation in an emergency department or hospital admission. These included admission for atypical pneumonia, acute kidney injury, and hypertensive urgency (MyBP group), an emergency department evaluation for palpitations (MyBP group), and an emergency department evaluation for asymptomatic BP elevation (control group).

# RESULTS
We identified 69 older adults who met preliminary eligibility and consented to participate in the HoME study, of whom 62 (90%; 95% CI: 80-96%) were randomized. Table 1 provides descriptive statistics on participants. Nearly two-thirds were women, and slightly more than half were African-American. Many were obese, diabetic, or lacked a college degree. At enrollment, the average number of prescribed antihypertensive medications was 2.6, and the average medical office BP was 164/91 (SD 14/12) mm Hg. The four baselines BP readings collected via phone-supervised home measurement averaged 146/85 (SD 21/10) mm Hg, which were much lower than the office readings (mean difference −17/-6 95% CI [−22, −12]/[−9,−3]). Randomization generated comparable groups.

The mean follow-up period was 23 (SD = 7) weeks. We sought to maximize study duration within our 12-month funding period, so individuals who began the study first were followed for 26–34 weeks while the majority were followed for 20–25 weeks. Six participants were lost to follow-up, all in the MyBP group. Two did not initiate the MyBP program, one never responded to any BP prompts, two ended their participation at week 8 and 22, respectively, and one discontinued their cell phone service during week 18 without informing us. These six participants could not be reached for final data collection. Their outcomes were multipleimputed for ITT analyses. See online Supplement Consort Flow Chart for additional details.

## Engagement

Among the 39 of 41 intervention arm participants who launched the program, the average intervention period was 24.1 weeks (SD = 4.3) during which participants submitted 4,086 mornings and evening BP readings. Using BP submission as indicating engagement and including all 41 participants randomized to MyBP, the mean and median overall engagement was 68.4% (SD = 30.7) and 82.7% (Q1 = 52.5, Q3 = 89.6), respectively. Figure 1 displays each individual’s engagement. Participants were instructed to provide at least four readings during each 2-week period, and they reached this threshold for 83% (95% CI = 68–93%) of 2-week periods.

The engagement was not significantly associated with age (Spearman r [95%CI] = 0.07 [−0.24,0.37]) or frailty, indexed as gait speed (Spearman r [95% CI] = −0.22 [−0.50,0.09]). Engagement was also not significantly associated with education (with associate degree or higher 65.1% vs other 69.9%, mean difference [95%CI] = −4.8% [−26.3%,16.8%]), race (whites 7.4.0% vs blacks 64.0%, mean difference [95%CI] = −9.9% [−29.4%,9.5%]), or sex (women 72.7% vs men 58.1% mean difference [95%CI] = 14.6% [−8.7%,37.9%]), as demonstrated by robust linear regression analyses. Engagement was positively associated with daily use of texting (0–1 messages 58.4% vs 2–5 messages 71.0% vs >5 messages 83.0%, mean difference [95%CI] for >5 vs 0–1 messages = 24.6% [1.2%, 48.1%]) but was unrelated to mobile device proficiency (Spearman r [95%CI] = 0.12 [−0.20,0.41]). Twoweek breaks were utilized by 18 participants (44%), ranging from once to seven times. Engagement was not significantly

correlated with the number of 2-week breaks used (Spearman r [95%CI] = −0.13 [−0.43,0.19]).

Figure 2 displays engagement to MyBP prompts over time, excluding the two individuals who did not initiate the program with a GO response. The median portion of BP readings submitted and how frequently participants submitted at least four readings were both generally over 80% and showed no decrement over 24 weeks.

With respect to perceived usefulness, we asked 34 of the 35 participants who completed the active intervention arm about the overall value of MyBP. The large majority rated it as having either some (n = 5 [15%]) or high value (n = 27 [79%]). In addition, 32 (94%) stated they would like to use MyBP outside of the study setting.
