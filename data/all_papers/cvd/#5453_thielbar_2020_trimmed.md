# Methods
## Participants

A total of 24 survivors of stroke were enrolled by study staff in this home-based study. Participants were included if they experienced a single, unilateral stroke at least 6 months prior to enrollment (mean time post stroke, 6.54.6y; range, 1-19y) and had moderate arm impairment (all participants were stage 3-5 on Stage of Arm of Chedoke-McMaster Stroke Assessment27). Participants were excluded if they had vision or cognitive deficits preventing use of the VR system; had received a botu linum toxin injection in the upper extremity within the previous 6 months; had moderate or severe aphasia that interfered with study tasks; or had a history of other neurologic, neuromuscular, or orthopedic disease. The Behavioral Regulation in Exercise Questionnaire (BREQ-2)28 was administered prior to initiation of training in order to assess each subject’s source of motivation leading to exercise behavior. Based on his or her responses to the 19 questions, the participant receives a score (1-5) on each of 5 constructs, spanning a motivational continuum from self-driven (Intrinsic, Identified, Introjected) to externally controlled (External) to not motivated to exercise (Amotivation). A higher score indicates that the construct is a stronger source of motivation.

## System

The VERGE system was used for in-home therapy. In this system each user controls an avatar by movement of the corresponding body parts, as measured with a Kinectb (Microsoft Inc.).26 Kine matic data are transmitted to the VR software, written in Unity 3Dc (Unity Technologies) and C#.29 Three different exercises were created, spanning 2 different virtual environments (fig 1). Described in detail elsewhere,26 the Ball Bump, Trajectory Trace, and Food Fight exercises were developed previously in the Hand Rehabilitation Laboratory to encourage movement of the arm away from the body through manipulation of virtual objects within the environment. All of the users can interact with the same virtual object (eg, hitting a single ball back and forth during Ball Bump), thereby creating a sense of proximity among the users. Each participant has a local computer, Kinect, and wireless mouse. The VERGE system also allows for 2-way voice communication through the microphone within the Kinect. The local computers communicate with a remote central server that coordinates scene updates. We optimized the VERGE system to run on typical hardware and a basic broadband network connection to facilitate installation and operation within the user’s home. Virtual private network communication, established between the home laptop and computers in the laboratory, permitted the transfer of data and remote technical assistance while running VERGE. We used a custom Google Cloud Virtual Machine as the server to enable use of a static IP address, thereby facilitating log-on procedures for participants and providing the stability of a server that was always available.

For this study, SU versions of these exercises were created (see fig 1, right column) to allow direct comparison of the MU and SU modes. For Ball Bump, a net was introduced across the table to return the ball to the user; in Trajectory Trace, the user created his or her own hand path and then attempted to erase it; and in Food Fight, the user aimed for specific targets with the food items, such as the open window.

## Experimental procedure

All participants took part in a 4-week, 2-arm, crossover, interventional study. Each user started the study with a brief 2- or 3-day orientation with the VERGE system in the laboratory to become familiar with the virtual environment and how to operate the hardware and software. Each participant was given a document outlining the procedures for operating and troubleshooting the system.

After orientation, a member of the research team installed the laptop, wireless mouse, and Kinect for the VERGE system in the individual’s home. To increase the size of the display, the VR scene was projected onto either a secondary monitor or the participant’s own television.

Across the 4 weeks of treatment, each participant used the SU mode for 2 weeks and the MU mode for 2 weeks. Treatment sequence was assigned by simple randomization procedures; study

staff drew lots at the time of consent. The First SU group trained first in the SU mode, while the First MU group trained first with the MU mode. For the SU mode, participants were instructed to complete four 1-hour practice sessions per week, for a total of 8 sessions. Users kept a log of system use. For the MU mode, research personnel and participants scheduled 8 appointments in advance.

# Outcome measures
The VERGE system recorded data from each training session in the participant’s home. These data included amount of time spent training with each exercise; specific exercise scores; and total hand, arm, and shoulder movement calculated from the Kinect skeleton data within VERGE. Total arm displacement for each session was calculated by subtracting total shoulder displacement

from total hand displacement to account for arm displacement due solely to trunk movement. All data were written to a unique log file, later analyzed in the laboratory. Total arm displacement during each session and session duration were the primary outcome measures.

Clinical data were collected in the laboratory (in a rehabilitation hospital) during 3 evaluation sessions. These were performed prior to initiation of training (Pre), after the first 2 weeks of home therapy (Midpoint), and after the next 2 weeks of home therapy (Post). The Fugl-Meyer Assessment of Motor Recovery After Stroke for the Upper Extremity (FMA-UE)30 was administered by a research therapist at each session. To measure the level of engagement with the MU and SU modes, a therapist also administered the Intrinsic Motivation Inventory31 (IMI), a questionnaire using the Likert scale,32,33 at the Midpoint and Post evaluation sessions. For the IMI, scores from all items (each rated on a Likert scale from 1-7, with 7 being the most positive) pertaining to 1 of the 4 components (Interest/Enjoyment, Value/ Usefulness, Effort/Importance, Perceived Competence) were averaged to create a single value for that component.

## Analysis

Total time training each session was computed by summing the times spent training with each exercise. All data are represented as mean  95% CI.

A repeated measures multivariate analysis of variance (SPSS Statistics 21d ) was performed to examine the effect of training mode (SU/MU) and group (First SU/First MU) on time spent training and total arm displacement performed across the 8 sessions. Only data from completed sessions were included in the final analysis.

Findings of a statistically significant effect of the independent variables would lead to post hoc repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) testing of each dependent variable. As a secondary analysis, a repeated measures ANOVA was conducted to examine the effects of the training on FMA-UE score. The withinsubject variable for this analysis was testing session (Pre, Midpoint, Post). Significance was set at aZ0.05 for all analyses.

# Results
A total of 64 subjects with chronic upper extremity hemiparesis subsequent to stroke were screened from January 2017 to February of 2018 (fig 2). Of the total of 24 subjects enrolled in the study, 20 were allocated to treatment groups (failed screenings and dropouts are described in fig 2). All 20 subjects assigned to a group (10 in the First SU group and 10 in the First MU group) completed the study. Characteristics for these 20 subjects are shown in table 1. All participants were included in all analyses, with the exception of 2 participants who were excluded from IMI analysis because of missing data that arose from researcher error (see fig 2).

Results from the BREQ-2 in this cohort showed that participants most strongly described their exercise motivation as Identified Regulation (3.9) or Intrinsic Regulation (3.8), the constructs that indicate more internally controlled motivation (engaging in exercise for internal reward, such as enjoyment in the activity and improved self-worth). There were no significant differences between the groups in terms of age, time since stroke, initial FMA-UE score, or BREQ-2 scores (ANOVA analysis found \mathrm { F } _ { 1 , 1 8 } { < } 2 . 2 4 5 ; P { > } . 1 5for all measures).

## Clinical measures

Participants made significant gains on the FMA-UE from Pre to Post across all groups and training modes (DFMA-UEZ3.2, PZ.001). Both groups showed greater mean improvement during the MU portion, regardless of group (fig 5), although this difference was not significant (MU: 1.90.6 points, SU: 1.20.6 points, PZ.477).

## Questionnaire

For both modes, IMI scores were generally quite high (table 2), indicating that the participants found value in the exercises. Mean values for the Value/Usefulness and Effort/Importance were 6.6 and 6.4 on the 7-point scale, respectively (a higher value indicates greater value or effort). Scores for the Interest/ Enjoyment and Perceived Competence sections were over 5.5. Interest/Enjoyment was rated higher for the MU mode than the SU mode, but no statistically significant differences were present between groups.
