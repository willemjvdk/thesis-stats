# Materials and Methods
## SAMPLE AND PROCEDURE

Ten Medicare-certified home health agencies (HHAs) across the United States participated in the study, which began in January 2006. Managers from the HHAs attended a training session to learn study objectives, review recruitment and enrollment procedures, and discuss potential challenges and opportunities associated with study. Patients were recruited when they were admitted to one of the participating HHAs, using the following eligibility criteria:

Patients who were cognitively impaired or diagnosed with a mental illness that limits their ability to respond appropriately to the monitoring system were eligible if a caregiver was available to par-

ticipate in the disease management program. Informed consent was obtained from all patients who agreed to participate.

## PHASE 1

Eligible patients were enrolled in the study upon admission to the HHA. The time period from admission to discharge from formal services was designated ‘‘Phase 1.’’ Upon enrollment, patients received the Health Buddy and used it for the duration of home health services. The Health Buddy was programmed to ask patients questions related to HF including symptoms, self-care practices, and medication compliance. During the formal episode of care, all patients received standard care, i.e., the home health nurse assessed patient vital signs, weight, heart sounds, lung sounds, mental status, emotional status, ankle swelling=edema, temperature, pain= discomfort, and dyspnea; reviewed medication schedule, assessed other body systems, instructed the patient on disease process, signs=symptoms to report, diet, and fluid restriction, and performed any other treatments ordered by the physician.

Nursing care was based on an evidence-based clinical pathway (protocol) for management of HF. The pathway identifies goals, nursing interventions for each visit, and milestones that must be reached before patients are discharged. All participating HHAs used the same pathway. The sample size was 108 patients at the end of Phase 1, with 64 in the telehealth group and 44 in the control group.

## PHASE 2

Phase 2 was defined as the 180 days following discharge from formal home health services. Upon discharge, the patient was randomly assigned to either the telehealth group or the control group. Patients in the telehealth group continued to use the Health Buddy for an additional 180 days, under the supervision of the HHA. The Health Buddy was programmed to ask patients questions similar to those in Phase 1.

Patients in the control group received no further telehealth or home health services. Since patients in the control group did not have a Health Buddy during Phase 2, a shortened list of similar questions was asked through telephone interviews conducted by a home health nurse at the end of the 180 days.

## MEASURES

Data on patient characteristics were obtained from HHA records and included age, payment source, and living with others. Health status measures were based on nursing assessment, using OASIS scales and data. The OASIS instrument is the assessment tool man-

dated by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services and used by Medicare-certified HHAs (www.cms.hhs.gov=HomeHealthQuality Inits=12_HHQIOASISDataSet.asp). Additionally, one self-reported measure, the New York Heart Association (NYHA) symptom score, was used. Clinical measures obtained from the OASIS were shortness of breath and frequency of respiratory treatments. These measures had response categories of 0–4. Functional status measures, also obtained from the OASIS assessment, reflected ability to perform the following activities of daily living (ADLs): ambulation, meal preparation, housekeeping, management of oral medications, and management of inhalant medications. These measures had response categories of 0–4. The OASIS data were obtained at the beginning of Phase 1 and the end of Phase 2.

All patients were asked ‘‘When do you feel fatigue or shortness of breath?’’ using the NYHA measure, at the end of Phase 1. Responses ranged from 1 (‘‘I don’t feel fatigued or short of breath’’) to 4 (‘‘All the time, even when I’m resting’’). At the end of Phase 2, telehealth patients were asked this question via the Health Buddy, while control group patients were questioned during the telephone interview.

Questions on self-management of HF were programmed into the Health Buddy and included the following:

Responses to these questions were collected from the Health Buddy for all patients at the end of Phase 1, and for telehealth patients at the end of Phase 2. Control group patients were asked the same questions during the telephone interview at the end of Phase 2.

Data on utilization of health services included the following: number of days in home health care, number of skilled nursing visits, number of visits for all home health services, number of inpatient admissions with HF diagnosis, and number of visits for emergent care. These data were obtained from HHA records at the end of Phase 1. The same measures were collected at the end of Phase 2, from the HHA records or from the patients themselves (if they were in the control group).

Responses to questions on patient satisfaction with the telehealth system were obtained at the end of Phase 2 from the Health Buddy.

# Results
## SAMPLE DESCRIPTION

The number of patients from each HHA ranged from 2 to 20. Overall, patients ranged in age from 22 years to 98 years, with an average age of 77.7. The majority (89%) of patients were Medicare beneficiaries; 8.1% of patients were enrolled in private insurance (traditional or HMO) and 1.8% of patients were covered through Medicaid. Using percentage of patients as the measured variable, we found no significant differences in age, payment source, or living status between patients in the intervention group and patients in the control group. Descriptive information for the sample is shown in Table 1.

## HEALTH AND FUNCTIONAL STATUS

Statistics were calculated on changes in symptom status and functional ability from the end of Phase 1 to the end of Phase 2. The change in symptom status was determined by calculating the difference in OASIS scores. For example, the same OASIS score at both time periods would result in a calculation of no change. Patients in the telehealth group showed greater improvements in symptom status, as illustrated in Table 2.

Responses to the NYHA question, ‘‘When do you feel fatigue or shortness of breath?’’ were analyzed at the end of Phase 2. The means for the two groups were compared, using the t-test for equality of means. Results show that telehealth patients reported lower scores (x ¼ 2.16, p < 0.001) for this measure, compared to control group patients who had an average score of 3.15. This statistically significant finding corroborates the nursing assessment of shortness of breath with patients’ self-reports of symptoms (Table 2).

Due to the small sample size, response categories for ADLs were combined. Ambulation was coded ‘‘0’’ (able to walk independently) or ‘‘1’’ (requires assistance or unable). Meal preparation was coded ‘‘0’’ (able to independently plan and prepare) or ‘‘1’’ (requires assistance or unable). Housekeeping was coded ‘‘0’’ (able to perform housekeeping tasks independently) or ‘‘1’’ (requires assistance or unable). Management of oral medications was coded ‘‘0’’ (able to take medicines independently) or ‘‘1’’ (requires assistance or unable). Results show that patients in the telehealth group improved in three ADL categories: ambulation ability, housekeeping ability, and management of oral medications. Only one change score, management of oral medications, was statistically significant, however. Patients in the control group showed

a greater improvement in one category: ability to prepare meals. These are illustrated in Table 3.

## SELF-MANAGEMENT OF HF

Responses to the questions on self-management were compared using the t-test for equality of means. Results showed that patients in the telehealth group scored more favorably than the control group patients for five of the eight items: increase in diuretic following an episode of shortness of breath, increase in diuretic following an episode of ankle swelling, increase in diuretic following an episode of sudden weight increase, performance of daily weights, and weight measurement on the actual day of questioning. The differences between means for these items were statistically significant. There were

no significant differences between the telehealth patients and control group patients for three items: use of a salt shaker, refilling medications before the bottles are empty, and taking medications exactly as ordered by the physician. Results of these tests are shown in Table 4.

## HEALTH SERVICES UTILIZATION

Tests of significance were performed on three health services utilization measures: home health services, inpatient hospitalization, and emergent care. Results showed no significant differences between groups for health services utilization during Phase 1, confirming that the two groups were equivalent during the episode of formal home healthcare. However, we found that the groups differed in their use of home health services during Phase 2. Patients in the telehealth group received, on average, 1.94 visits by a registered nurse (RN) during the 180-day follow-up homecare period. Patients in the control group received 0.74 RN visits during this period. This difference is consistent with the protocol that patients using the Health Buddy also receive at least one RN home visit.

Results found that continued use of the Health Buddy for 180 days had a significant effect on the number of inpatient hospital admissions. Among the 64 patients in the telehealth group, none (0%) reported any inpatient admissions, compared to 13 (28.3%) patients in the control group. This difference was statistically significant ( p < 0.001). Patients in the telehealth group were also less likely to receive emergent care, with no patients reporting any ED visits, while 12 (26.1%) patients in the control group reported one or more ED visit. This difference was statistically significant ( p < 0.001). Additionally, patients in the telehealth group were more likely to remain in the community after completion of the study; 89.3% of telehealth patients remained at home compared to 80% of control group patients. Results showing the differences in health services utilization are illustrated in Table 5.

## EXPERIENCE WITH HEALTH BUDDY

Patients who had used the Health Buddy for the entire 180 days were asked to evaluate their experience with using the system. Overall, patients were satisfied with the Health Buddy; 93.1% of patients reported that they were ‘‘very satisfied’’ or ‘‘satisfied.’’ This experience also reflected a perceived ease of use with the system; 96.6% of patients reported that the system was ‘‘very easy’’ or ‘‘easy’’ to use. These results are shown in Table 6.
