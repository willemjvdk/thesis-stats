## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# Methods
## Study design

This study followed a randomized, single-blind, 2-sequence, crossover design with the order of both home-based treatments reversed after a 12-week washout period. Two evaluators, 1 of them an occupational therapist and the other a technician who were both blinded to the treatment, conducted the pre- and posttreatment evaluations.

## Participants

Given the effects on the motor function of the upper extremities of stroke patients that we have observed in our previous research on a rehabilitation technique using a TIGER,14 an effect size (d) of 1.30 was estimated. At least 8 participants had to be recruited for each treatment condition to verify the results of this 2-period crossover study with a 2-tailed alpha score of 0.05 and a power score of 0.8. The inclusion criteria were (1) being the victim of a unilateral stroke aged 20-79 years; (2) at least 6 months post-stroke; (3) scoring between 15 and 56 on the Fugl-Meyer upper extremity motor assessment (FMA-UE), corresponding to mild and severe arm-hand impairment, respectively;17 (4) suffering one’s first-ever stroke; and (5) having experience using smart devices. The exclusion criteria were (1) having a contracture or other deformity of a joint of the affected UL; (2) suffering wrist pain; (3) having shoulder-hand syndrome; (4) scoring lower than 24 on the Mini-Mental State Examination; and (5) suffering from Wernicke’s aphasia or global aphasia, which would make it difficult to follow instructions. The patients who met the inclusion criteria were referred by the Department of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation at a university hospital in southern Taiwan. A diagram of the progression of the participants through the stages of the research, from the enrollment to the data analysis, is presented in figure 1.

## Randomization and allocation concealment

The participants were randomly assigned to undergo home-based program with either the IoT-assisted rehabilitation program or TSMT first with the help of computer-generated numbers which were contained in sealed opaque envelopes.

## System

The training system consisted of low-cost exoskeleton hardware (figure 2), a control system, and an IoT system. Details are presented in appendix 1.

## Interventions

In addition to receiving occupational and physical therapy in hospital for 2 hours a week as standard of care, the participants underwent a 30-minute home-based training session twice a day, 5 days a week, for 4 weeks. The patients in Group 1 were given TIGERbased motor training during the first intervention period. The exercise mode of the TIGER-based training sessions involved continual wrist flexion/extension and the gripping of pegs with a tenodesis grip with offering constant assistance. Each dose with the TIGER consisted of 225 robot-facilitated repetitions of the wrist flexion/ extension as well as 90 repetitions with the tenodesis grip. Each patient was asked to deliberately perform a wrist motion while wearing the TIGER 3 times a week in order to inform the therapist

of the motor status of the patient’s wrist. These data were then used as a control parameter for modifying the initial position of wrist joint while conducting pegs gripping, the ratio between motion arc of wrist extension and flexion in each movement cycle, and the magnitude of wrist joint motion administered with the help of the IoT-assisted TIGER. During the second period, they were given home-based TSMT, which included tasks requiring reaching, grasping, and releasing an object through specific movement components of the wrist and hand (described in a previous study18).

The patients in Group 2 were first given TSMT, and after the washout period, they received the TIGER-based treatment. The home-based program of TSMT was provided and guided by a qualified therapist. Every week, 3 telephone interviews were

conducted to assess the patient’s progress and to remind them to implement these programs. Compliance was recorded based on the patient’s subjective responses (scored on a scale of 0-10). The scores of 0-3, 4-6, and 7-10 referred to a low, medium, and high degree of compliance, respectively. All the participants received hospital delivered therapy during the washout period.

# Primary outcomes
Box and block test (BBT): The BBT measures manual dexterity of the UL with high reliability and validity19 and is feasible for use

in clinical practice.20 The established minimal detectable change (MDC) value for the BBT is 5.5 blocks per minute for chronic stroke patients.21

# Secondary outcomes
Fugl-Meyer assessment for upper extremity (FMA-UE): This scale is a valid test for assessing the motor performance of an affected UL through the direct observation of movements.22 The test includes 33 items assessed on a 3-point ordinal scale, with scores ranging between 0 and 66 and an MDC of 5.2.23

Active range of motion (AROM) in the wrist joint: The active flexion and extension of the joint of the affected wrist was measured with a goniometer at the end of each 4-week treatment period. The mean values of 74.2 and 71.1 have been determined

for the flexion and extension, respectively, of the wrist of healthy adults.24

Modified Ashworth scale (MAS): The MAS is capable of measuring spasticity in the upper extremities during passive soft-tissue stretching with good reliability.25 It is scored using a 6-point numerical scale with a range of 0-4, with 0 representing no increase in muscle tone during passive joint stretching and 4 reflecting limb rigidity.

Motor activity log (MAL): The MAL is a semi-structured interview, a 6-point ordinal scale ranging from 0 to 5, which has been shown to have reasonable validity and reliability in a study assessing the amount of use (AOU) and quality of movement (QOM) of the affected arm of chronic stroke patients.26

Semmes-Weinstein monofilament (SWM) test: The SWM is a screening tool measuring touch-pressure thresholds that is capable of assessing sensory deficits with high sensitivity and specificity.27 The score was recorded as the finest filament that the participant could detect.

Patient Clinical Global Impressions-Improvements (PCGI-I) scale: The PCGI-I is a self-report assessment tool which could be used to prove how much the patient’s symptoms have been improved related to an intervention.28 Its evaluation has been shown to correlate highly with assessments provided by clinicians,29 using a 7-point scale, with responses rated from 1 (very much improved) to 7 (very much worse).

## Ethical statement

All procedures performed in studies involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Cheng Kung University Hospital (B-BR-107-084). Prior to participating in the study, each participant was informed of the aims and the related experimental procedures, and then signed an informed consent form.

## Adverse effects study report protocol

For the purposes of this study, an injury to the wrist and/or hand of the affected UL that was attributable to the interventions that we conducted and required a visit to a hospital was defined as an adverse event. The slight possibility of such a risk was mentioned on the informed consent form.

## Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out with the help of the IBM SPSS Statistics 20.0a software for Windows. Descriptive statistics were used to determine the means and standard deviations of the dependent variables, which were examined by means of the FMA-UE, the MAL, the BBT, the MAS, the AROM of the wrist joint, and the SWM. The x 2 and the independent t test were used to evaluate any differences in demographic and baseline characteristics between Groups 1 and 2. A pre-test was conducted to preclude the possibility of carry-over effects by calculating the sum of the scores measured during the 2 periods for each patient and comparing them with an unpaired t test.30 Afterward, a repeated-measures analysis of variance was carried out to examine both the withinsubject differences in the outcome measures of each treatment

condition and the differences in the treatment effects between the 2 intervention methods and participant groups. If there was a significant group-by-time interaction effect, a subgroup repeatedmeasures was used to examine the treatment-by-time effect for each group. The level of significance was set at P<.05.

# Results
Eighteen patients (8 male and 10 female) were recruited for this study (table 1). The patients were equally divided into 2 groups, 9 each for Groups 1 and 2. All participants in Group 1 and 8 of the participants in Group 2 completed all the pre- and post-tests in the study. The remaining member of Group 2 discontinued the second phase of the treatment because of a change of residence. The differences in demographic and clinical characteristics between the participants in the 2 groups are presented in table 2. No safety concerns or adverse events resulted from their participation in this study.

Statistical analyses revealed no differential carry-over effects for any outcome measurements in this crossover study (BBT:

P=.342; FMA-UE: P=.443; MAS of wrist: P=.254; SWM: P=.170; AOU of MAL: P=.728; QOM of MAL P=.656; AROM of wrist flexion: P=.294; and AROM of wrist extension: P=.085).

Table 3 summarizes the descriptive statistics and the changes in scores for the primary and secondary outcomes that were administered to the patients when they underwent both home-based treatment programs 2 times: before the treatment and immediately after the treatment. The mean change in the BBT score for the TIGERbased training was 1.4§2.7, and a significant treatment-by-time interaction effect (F(1.31)=5.212 and P=.022) was observed. In addition, significant treatment-by-time interaction effects were observed for the change in the FMA-UE score (F(1.31)=6.807 and P=.042) and in the AROM of the wrist joint (F(1.31)=8.618 and P=.009). Compared with TSMT intervention, the participants who received the TIGER-based treatment showed more marked improvements of the motor function of their affected UL.

There were no significant group-by-time interaction effects for almost all outcome measures. The exception to this was the AROM of the extension of the wrist, for which the treatment effect was further tested separately for both Groups 1 and 2. The results showed a significant treatment-by-time interaction effect (F(1.16)

=10.019 and P=.006) for the patients in Group1, which was not observed for Group 2 (F(1.15)=1.207 and P=.289). For the patients in Group 1, the improvement of the ROM of the wrist extension was significantly better in the treatment using the TIGER than it was using TSMT.

Participants showed significant improvements in their performance on the BBT (P=.046), the FMA-UE (P=.018), and the AROM of the extension (P=.0033) and flexion (P=.002) of the wrist after receiving TIGER-assisted UL training but no significant changes after receiving TSMT (P>.05). No statistically significant within-subject effect on the scores on the SWM and MAS in the assessment of the wrist motion was observed for either treatment condition.

Regarding the evaluation of the functional use of the affected limbs, the AOU and QOM, as assessed with the MAL, showed neither a significant treatment-by-time interaction effect (F (1.31) = 2.416, P=.130; and F(1.31) = 2.277, P=.141 for the AOU and QOM, respectively) nor within-group effects in either the TIGER-assisted or TSMT (P>.05).

The PCGI-I score was 2.8§0.9 and 3.9§0.3 for the TIGERassisted and TSMT intervention, respectively, showing a significant between-treatment difference (P<.001). Thirteen participants (76.5%) in the TIGER group reported a clinical global improvement (ie, a PCGI-I score≤3), whereas only 11.1% of the participants in the TSMT group reported such an improvement. The mean compliance score for the participants who underwent the home-based TSMT was 7.8 08.
