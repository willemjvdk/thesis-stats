# Effects of Transitional Care Model–Based Interventions for Stroke Patients and Caregivers on Caregivers' Competence and Patient Outcomes Randomized Controlled Trial
Yasemin Demir Avci, PhD, RN, Sebahat Gözüm, PhD, RN

This study evaluated the effectiveness of the Transitional Care Model Stroke Turkey for stroke patients and caregivers as regards the competence of caregivers and patient outcomes. It is a parallel-group, assessor-blinded monocenter conducted with 126 participants in total (66 intervention included 33 stroke patients and 33 caregivers; 60 control groups included 30 stroke patients and 30 caregivers), between March and August 2018. The Transitional Care Model Stroke Turkey program lasts for 13 to 20 weeks. It includes a 12-week follow-up after discharge, a minimum of three hospital visits, one home visit, minimum 18 phone calls, and Web-based training. The intervention group exhibited better caregiver competence (13.48 ± 2.31), preparation for care ( 2 8 . 4 8 \pm 4 . 7 4 ), and e-health literacy (34.42 ± 4.74) than the control group (respectively, 11.37 ± 2.48, 20.93 ± 7.10, 26.93 ± 8.53) ( P < .001). Emotional exhaustion and depersonalization increased in the control group, but remained the same in the intervention group. Personal accomplishment decreased in the intervention group, unlike in the control group. Within 12 weeks of discharge, five patients from the intervention group and seven patients from the control group were rehospitalized. There was no statistical difference between stroke patients in either group in terms of having previously used home healthcare services. The Transitional Care Model Stroke Turkey is a practical model for stroke patients transitioning from hospital to home.

Patients need intense treatment and to be closely followed up in order to prevent them from having a secondary stroke. Family caregivers therefore play a key role in poststroke patient care.1

Families of stroke patients may play an obligatory role in caregiving. They may find themselves helping their patients with their treatment, hospital appointments, transport, safety, and doctor reports, as well as day-to-day tasks (eg, excretion, feeding, hygiene).2 They often lack the preparedness and competence to carry out all of these roles, however. If caregivers do not receive relevant training after discharge, they often learn how to take care of the patient through trial and error.3 After patients are discharged from hospital, their families might have to see the family physician for the prescription of medicines, apply to home care units to procure dressings and catheters, visit neurologists with the patient for stroke check-ups, and see physiotherapists to ensure their patient receives physical treatment. In Turkey, health and social support institutions act independently, and therefore, the caregiver may have difficulty in managing the hospital-to-home transition process on their own.2 Many hospital-to-home transition models have been proposed to manage this process.

Evidence-based applications can help solve many of a caregiver's problems.5,6 Such models are known to boost the competence of caregivers, and in turn lower the risk of rehospitalization.7 Common models include the Transitional Care Model (TCM),4 Better Outcomes by Optimizing Safe Transitions,7 Coleman Care Transitions Intervention,8 Re Engineered Discharge,9 and the Interventions to Reduce Acute Care Transfers.10 The TCM, which focuses on improving care and is led by a nurse, is the most widely used and aims to improve the outcomes of patients and their caregivers.4

Several Turkish studies look at how family caregivers monitor and support their stroke patients, especially after they have been discharged from hospital.2,10,11 These studies have not examined the whole process of the hospital-tohome transition. This study aims to examine how TCM affects stroke patients and their family caregivers.

Transitional Care Model is a discharge transition model that starts at the moment of the patient's hospitalization under the guidance of a nurse and continues with planning for discharge, house visits, and phone calls for nearly 2 months afterward.4 It is commonly used in the United States, China, and Japan. This study will introduce the model to Turkey for the first time. The purpose of this model is to inform caregivers and their patients, bring skills to them, realize the aims of family caregivers, and thus improve their quality of life and healthcare outcomes. It moreover prevents patients from having chronic disease–associated complications (eg, pressure ulcers) and experiencing repeated hospitalization.6 One study conducted to determine the efficiency of discharge planning demonstrated that hospital support on discharge ultimately shortened the length of a stroke patient's readmission.12 A meta-analysis examining how transitional care interventions affected stroke patients reported that these interventions reduced their dependence levels and lowered mortality rates (0.86; 95% confidence interval, 0.75–0.98).13

Transitional Care Model solves various problems, such as inadequate discharge procedures, a lack of communication with medical personnel, lack of training among patients and their caregivers, failure to access home care services, limited care, and a failure to involve patients and caregivers in the whole decision-making process.14 Language barriers, illiteracy, and cultural differences further lead to the need for a transitional model.4 This study featured Web-based training alongside hospital-home visits and phone calls within the scope of the model, unlike other TCM-based examples in the world. Furthermore, the caregivers had 24/7 access to information after their patients had been discharged.

## Aim

This study was conducted to assess how TEMpEST (Transitional Care Model Stroke Turkey), which is based on TCM for stroke patients and their caregivers, affected the caregiver's competence, increasing their preparedness for caregiving and e-health literacy, decreasing their level of burnout, decreasing the readmission of patients, and reducing pressure ulcers among patients. The “superiority hypothesis” was used.

# METHODS
## Study Design

This study is a parallel-group, assessor-blind, monocenter randomized controlled trial (ClinicalTrials.gov reference no. NCT03708835). Details of the study method are provided in the study protocol.15

## Study Setting

This study included stroke patients who were staying in the neurology clinic of a university hospital alongside their caregivers. The patients and their caregivers were monitored for 3 months between March and May 2018. Follow-ups, likewise, were carried out between March and August 2018. The study took place both at the hospital in question and at each patient's home after discharge.

## Inclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria for caregivers were as follows: volunteering to participate in the study, being the primary caregiver of a patient (ie, they helped the patient throughout the intervention), caring for a stroke patient for the first time, having Internet access, and knowing at least one person in their family who was able to use the Internet. If more than one caregiver agreed to participate in the study, then only the most willing caregiver was included. The inclusion criteria for patients were as follows: residing in the same house or apartment with their caregiver, having a stroke for the first time, being at level 3 or 4 of the Modified Rankin Scale, being at least semidependent according to the Functional Independence Scale,16 and obtaining ≥10 points on the LACE Index.17

The Modified Rankin Scale with six items was used to find out how bedridden each patient was; a score of ≤2 points indicates a good result, whereas a score of >2 points indicates a poor result.18

The Functional Independence Scale was used to assess how well each patient could function. The scale was developed in 1986 to assess the functional status of individuals and was adapted to Turkish by Küçükdeveci and Yavuzer.16 The scale's ranking system works as follows: 1 to 2 points = dependent, 3 to 5 points = semi-dependent, and 6 to 7 points = independent. The lowest and highest scores of the scale are 18 and 126, respectively.

## Exclusion Criteria

Patients were excluded from the study if they had any previous history of functional dependence (non–stroke related). Caregivers were excluded from the study if they lacked Internet access and/or did not know how to use the Internet, accepted payment in exchange for their caregiving, were not a relative of the patient, did not reside in the same house (or at least did not live close by) as their patients, were replaced by someone else during the intervention, or could not be reached within the first week of discharge.

## Sample Size

The sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.2 (Heinrich Heine University, Dusseldorf, Germany) based on the Preparedness for Caregiving Scale19 (used in a similar study),20 and the effect size was found to be 0.58. The sample size was calculated to be 52 subjects (26 patients and 26 caregivers) for each group at a power of 80% and significance level of .05. Given that there may be losses during both the intervention and follow-up process, each of the intervention and control groups included 70 patients and 70 caregivers. The number of subjects in the intervention group fell to 33 patients and 33 caregivers after the follow-up, and the number of subjects in the control group fell to 30 patients and 30 caregivers (Figure 1). An intention-to-treat analysis was performed to measure the effect of losses.

## Randomization

Simple randomization was used to randomly select caregivers for the sample and to assign them impartially to the intervention and control groups. All the nurses at the clinic were informed about the study beforehand. The researcher visited the patients after they were hospitalized. Those who met the inclusion criteria were included in the study. The nurses were able to access all information about the hospitalization history of each patient. The researcher and a nurse working in the clinic selected each patient. The researcher interviewed the caregivers who agreed to participate in the study, asked them to sign an informed consent form, and then

applied a pretest. Randomization took place on the computer via https://www.randomizer.org/ (Research Randomizer version 4.0, Lancaster, PA, USA). Patient rooms were meant for two people; however, patients from the intervention group did not share their rooms with those from the control group.

## Blinding and Bias Prevention

Because of the nature of the intervention, the researcher and the caregivers (ie, the relatives of each patient) could not be blinded; however, measures were taken to collect posttests, encode data, and evaluate statistics to prevent bias in the outcome measurements. The researcher collected the pretests. The data collection instruments were uploaded to a special Web site developed for the caregivers (hastayakini.net). The caregivers in the intervention group were asked to fill out the posttest forms on the Web site. Whereas those in the intervention group filled out these forms using WhatsApp and hastayakini.net, those in the control group did so only over WhatsApp. Screenshots of the forms were sent to the caregivers via an online self-report (Web site, e-mail, or WhatsApp). The caregivers submitted their answers via an online self-report when they had a spare moment to do so.

## Primary and Secondary Findings

The results included measurements for both caregivers and patients. Primary findings about the caregivers included their levels of competence, preparedness, e-health literacy, and burnout. The secondary finding was about their ability to use the Web site. The primary findings for patients were related to readmission and the frequency of pressure ulcers.

## Data Collection Instruments

The data were collected using the Sociodemographic Characteristics Questionnaire for Caregivers, Caregiving Competence Scale, Preparedness for Caregiving Scale, e-Health Literacy Scale, Maslach Burnout Inventory—General Form, the System Usability Scale, Sociodemographic Questionnaire for Patients, and the form for patient outcome measures. The baseline data were gathered by the researcher through face-to-face interviews at the hospital. The final data were collected via online self-report by caregivers.

## Sociodemographic Characteristics Questionnaire for Caregivers

All of the caregivers filled out this questionnaire, which included questions about their age, sex, educational level, marital status, employment status, degree of affinity, patient care experience, the presence of other persons for whom they were responsible, and the duration of time spent caregiving (in hours).

## Caregiving Competence Scale

The Caregiving Competence Scale was developed by Pearlin et al21 and adapted to Turkish by Demir Avcı and

Gözüm.22 It is a Likert-type scale with four items. The lowest and highest scores on the scale are 4 and 16, respectively. The higher the score, the more competent the caregiver.

## Preparedness for Caregiving Scale

The Preparedness for Caregiving Scale was developed by Archbold et al23 and adapted to Turkish by Karaman and Karadakovan.24 It is a Likert-type scale with nine items. The lowest and highest scores on the scale are 0 and 32, respectively. The higher the score, the more prepared the caregiver.

## e-Health Literacy Scale

The e-Health Literacy Scale was developed by Norman and Skinner25 and adapted to Turkish by Coşkun and Bebiş. 26 It consists of eight items—two that measure the respondent's ability to use the Internet and six that measure their attitudes toward the Internet. The lowest and highest scores on the scale are 8 and 40, respectively.

## Maslach Burnout Inventory—General Form

The Maslach Burnout Inventory—General Form (MBE-GF) was developed by Schaufeli et al27 and adapted to Turkish by Gündüz et al.28 It is a Likert-type scale that has 15 items and

three subscales: emotional exhaustion (items 1, 4, 7, 10, and 13), depersonalization (items 2, 5, 8, and 11), and personal accomplishment (items 3, 6, 9, 12, 14, and 15). Earning a high score on the emotional exhaustion and depersonalization subscales and a low score in the personal accomplishment (reverse scored) subscale indicates that one is burned out. Each respondent earns three separate burnout scores.

## The System Usability Scale

This was developed by Brooke29 and adapted to Turkish by Kadirhan et al.30 It is a Likert-type scale consisting of 10 items. Items 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 are reversely scored. One can earn a score between 0 and 100, and that score is then multiplied by 2.5. Higher scores signify that the Web site is more usable. A score of between 65 and 70 indicates that the Web site is usable at an acceptable standard.

## Sociodemographic Characteristics Questionnaire for Patients

This form was prepared by the researchers. All the patients also filled out the Sociodemographic Characteristics Questionnaire including questions about patient sex, type of stroke, and history of chronic diseases.

# Form for Patient Outcome Measures
This form was also prepared by the researchers. It consists of two questions. The first question addresses emergency room visits after discharge, and the second question asks about whether the patient developed any pressure ulcers after discharge.

## Intervention Protocol

This study followed a protocol consisting of four basic interventions—briefly, TEMpEST (Transitional Care Model Stroke Turkey) (Supplemental Figure 1, http:// links.lww.com/CIN/A223).

## Interview at the Hospital

First, an interview was held with caregivers within 24 to 48 hours of their patients being hospitalized. During the first interview, the caregivers were given a pretest to fill out and informed about strokes. The second interview was held 1 day after the hospitalization. The subjects were identified in the first interview and informed about the use of the Web site that had been specially designed for them. Further interviews were carried out every day until the patient was discharged from hospital. The caregivers were also taught how to use the Web site. The interviews were conducted only when the subjects were available (between 9 AM and 6 PM). Each interview took between 15 and 30 minutes—albeit sometimes longer when necessary. In order to determine the needs of caregivers whose patients had reached the

discharge stage, they filled out a list of their poststroke information needs. The list prepared by the researchers included 23 questions (22 yes/no and one open-ended) about the patients' day-to-day activities and the stroke complications.

## Home Visits

Either the researcher or someone from the coordination center visited the patients and caregivers at their homes within the first 5 working days of discharge. House visit planning was carried out either by the researcher or caregiver upon calling the Provincial Health Directorate Home Health Coordination Center Unit or only by the researcher. The home environment was inspected in terms of patient safety during visits. Issues related to patient care and the caregivers were also discussed. The researcher used the Evaluation Form of Domestic Environmental Properties in Terms of Falling, originally developed by Akın and Lök.31 It comprises six sections, one for each part of a person's home (ie, living room, kitchen, bedroom, bathroom, stairs, and hallway), and 39 questions.

## Phone Calls

The caregivers were called up after their patients were discharged from the hospital. Starting within the first week of discharge, the researcher called the caregivers at a certain hour every week between the second and 12th weeks, at least 18 times in total. A new phone line was used for phone calls. During the 3-month period, the researcher called the caregivers between 9 AM and 11 PM, sent text messages to them, or contacted them over WhatsApp whenever they needed to communicate. The caregiver specified the times they wanted to be called. A form was prepared by the researchers to be used for the phone interviews. At each interview, the participants were asked about the development of symptoms requiring emergency intervention, as well as complications that had not yet been seen in the patient, such as pressure ulcers. The most frequently asked questions during the interviews dealt with outpatient clinic appointment hours, the location of outpatient clinics in the hospital, medications, reports, rehabilitation centers, the stroke, other health problems experienced by the patient and the caregivers, and the patient's recovery process.

## Web-Based Training

The Web site used in this study was assessed by 10 experts using the Quality of Criteria for Consumer Health Information measurement instrument.32 Its content and technique were revised in line with suggestions by the experts. The Quality of Criteria for Consumer Health Information total score for the Web site was 72.70 ± 3.62 (of 75). This high score indicated that the Web site was of good quality. The expert evaluations were compatible with one another (W: 0.401, P = .000). The Web site provided the caregivers and patients with information about home care. One had

to log into the Web site via a username and a password. The system kept records about how many times, on which days, and for how long the caregivers used the Web site. The intervention group was asked to evaluate the usability of the Web site using the System Usability Scale. This high score demonstrated that its usability improved.29

## Intervention Implemented With the Control Group

The clinic's training nurse prepared the control group for discharge according to the routine program of the hospital, as well as booking their future appointments and organizing their medications. As soon as the discharge process started, the nurse provided routine training sessions. Both groups filled out the pretest during their first interview, and then the posttest via WhatsApp and e-mail 3 months after discharge. After the posttests were collected, the Web site was activated for the control group to use.

## Ethical Considerations

Approval was obtained from the ethics committee of the university hospital (June 1, 2017: 10707), as well as permission from the neurology clinic and the home healthcare unit in question. Written consent was also obtained from the caregivers and their patients. The authors of the scales were asked via e-mail for permission for their use in the study.

## Data Analysis

The Statistical Package of Social Science (SPSS 23) (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used to analyze the data. Cronbach's α coefficients, means, and minimum-maximum values were calculated for the pretest and posttest results of both groups. The intention-totreat analysis was conducted to assess losses in the sample. Caregiver levels of competence, preparedness, e-health literacy, and burnout were determined using t tests (paired-samples t test or Wilcoxon signed rank test). Both groups were compared using an independent-samples t test or Mann-Whitney U test. The usability of the Web site was assessed using mean scores.

# RESULTS
The sociodemographic characteristics of the caregivers at baseline are presented in Supplemental Table 1, http:// links.lww.com/CIN/A224. There was no statistically significant difference between the caregivers in both groups in terms of sociodemographic characteristics such as age, education level, marital status, and employment status, with the exception of patient care experience ( P = .003). The baseline characteristics of the stroke patients are presented in Supplemental Table 2, http://links.lww.com/CIN/A225. The majority of the patients in both groups (intervention: 87.9% [n = 29], control 93.3% [n = 28]) were diagnosed with ischemic stroke. Thirteen patients (39.4%) from the intervention group and 12 patients (34.3%) from the control group also

suffered from diabetes and hypertension. The mean age of the patients was 35.48 ± 10.77 years in the intervention group and 36.07 ± 10.88 years in the control group. There was no statistically significant difference ( P > .05) between the groups in terms of LACE Index,19 Modified Rankin Scale,20 Braden Risk Assessment Scale,33 and Functional Independence Scale scores18 (Supplemental Table 2, http://links.lww.com/CIN/A225).

The caregiver results are shown in Table 1 and Table 2. The mean scores for the ability to provide care, their preparedness to provide care, and electronic health literacy were higher in the intervention group than the control group after the intervention—this difference was statistically significant ( P < .05) (Table 1). The control group's posttest MBE-GF emotional exhaustion mean score was 8.90 ± 3.58. This was higher than their pretest mean score (7.24 ± 3.27). This difference was statistically significant. The MBE-GF score of the control group was significantly higher than the posttest of the intervention group ( P = .001). The TEMpEST program did not change the emotional exhaustion and depersonalization in family caregivers in the intervention group and decreased their personal accomplishment when compared with the control group (Table 2). Hypotheses H1a, H1b, and H1c were accepted. H1d was partially accepted.

The opinions of the caregivers in the intervention group regarding the usability of the Web site and its frequency of use are presented in Supplementary Table 3, http://links. lww.com/CIN/A226. The System Usability Scale score was 76.27 of 100. Caregivers paid on average 29 visits to the Web site between March and August 2018 (Supplemental Table 3, http://links.lww.com/CIN/A226).

Patient results are presented in Table 3. Ten stroke patients from the intervention group, who required home healthcare services, were able to benefit from them; whereas only three of nine patients in the control group were able to benefit from these services. The readmission rate of the patients was clinically significant; however, statistical analysis could not be undertaken because the sample size was small. Three patients from the control group were rehospitalized twice. Hypotheses H1e and H1f were rejected (Table 3).

# Patient Outcomes
All the patients included in this study were at a high risk of readmission, according to the LACE index. It was therefore not possible to prevent them from returning to the hospital completely, but the rate of return can be lowered. Even if the difference between the groups was not statistically significant in this study, the cost value of the two patients not returning to the hospital is important. Reasons for the intervention group returning to hospital were urinary incontinence, nausea, sudden high blood pressure, and respiratory distress. The reasons given by the control group included infection, pain, high glucose, and low blood pressure. Two patients from the intervention group and three patients from the control group resorted to the emergency room twice. Patients may return to the hospital for problems they cannot solve on their own at home.13 One of the reasons that the Transition Care Model was followed is that it is cost-efficient, because it decreases the rate of return to the hospital. Some nurse-led studies have reported that discharge interventions lower a patient's risk of returning to the emergency room,13,48 not to mention also enhancing their quality of life, satisfaction, and self-confidence.44 The TEMpEST program appears to have promising findings, but nevertheless, long-term follow-up studies should be carried out to evaluate how effective it is and to conduct TEMpEST interventions with patients from different levels of chronic disease in future studies.

One patient from the intervention group and two patients from the control group developed pressure ulcers. Care provided by family members who are poor-informed will adversely affect patient outcomes. Patients who do not receive a good care may suffer from pressure ulcers.49 A detailed section highlighting how to provide care to stroke patients was made available on the Web site prepared within the scope of TEMpEST, and the caregivers in the intervention group therefore had the opportunity to learn in advance about the possible problems of their patients. During all phone calls, they were asked whether pressure ulcers developed and whether they worked on in-room exercises with the patient. One patient developed pressure ulcers due to irreversible risk factors including age, sex, and dependency level. Eliminating this even in a single patient is an important result.

# Process Results
Upon implementing an application on the home health service, the related team is required to inform the patient's relatives by phone or through house visits within 1 week. Even if \mathrm { H _ { l g } }is not accepted because of the standardized functioning used to

evaluate the process results of TCM, it can be accepted because the patients from the intervention group benefit more from the existing processes. Benefitting from the home healthcare services was effective in lowering rates of rehospitalization in the intervention group. Improving the competence of caregivers in this process also enabled them to more effectively use home healthcare services. One of the implementation steps of TCM was home visits,45 which is why all the patients from the intervention group were granted access to home healthcare services.

## LIMITATIONS

A limitation of the study is that no blinding was used for group assignment because the neurology clinic was not deemed suitable for blinding patients, and caregivers may talk among themselves during their stay in the hospital. This is a potential source of bias and needs to be addressed. TEMpEST was applied to only stroke patients and their caregivers. Another limitation of this study is that the number of caregivers with patient care experience was higher in the control group than in the intervention group. This situation may affect the results related to the competence of caregivers. On the other hand, it is an advantage for the control group. Strengths of the study included the lack of difference between the two groups in terms of other variables such as age, education, working status, degree of affinity, presence of dependence that may affect the measurement results of caregivers, and variables such as the patient's degree of functional independence and hospitalization risk level.

## CONCLUSIONS

Interventions based on the TCM in our study increased caregiver competence, prepared them for caregiving and e-health literacy, and prevented the progression of burnout. Transitional Care Model–based interventions also improved patient outcomes. Readmission and pressure ulcers were less common in stroke patients in the intervention group compared with the control group. Transitional Care Model– based interventions are also promising in improving process outcomes. TEMpEST intervention enabled the caregivers to benefit effectively from existing care support.

Our experiences in the study lead us to recommend deciding on the number of phone calls and home visits among the intervention types according to a patient's dependency level. The TEMpEST program can be tried in all dependent patient groups who need family care. Cost-effectiveness studies should be run regarding the effect of the TEMpEST program.
