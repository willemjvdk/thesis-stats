## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# Methods
## Research design and sample

The research design of this study was a prospective RCT that used a quantitative approach to collect and analyze data. Inclusion criteria were: (a) ≥21 years of age, (b) hospitalized for non-ST elevation MI, ST elevation MI, or percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), (c) prescribed antiplatelet and statin medications, (d) owned a mobile phone with text messaging capability, and (e) were able to speak, read, and understand English. Exclusion criteria included: (a) cognitive impairment that limited ability to

understand and complete questionnaires, and (b) inability to operate a mobile phone as evidenced by fine motor skills.

## Procedure

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the designated medical center for recruitment as well as the academic institution that sponsored the study. The recruitment center was a non-profit, community hospital in Northern California, USA. Patient recruitment took place between April 2012–March 2013 until the final sample size was obtained. The study observed the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients were told that participation was voluntary and were guaranteed confidentiality. Eligible patients were first introduced to the research study by other health care providers (cardiologists and nurses) and then interested patients were given details of the study by the principal investigator (PI).

Patients who provided written informed consent opened sealed opaque envelopes that contained the assignment to one of three groups (TM Reminders + TM Education, TM Education Alone, or No TM). Group assignment was generated by a random allocation sequence using blocks of six that was prepared by a biostatistician. The PI assigned patients to their groups by distributing envelopes in consecutive, numbered order. Due to the nature of the study design, the PI and patients could not be blinded to the intervention once group assignment was determined. All subjects were given a $20 gift card as well as additional reimbursement if their wireless agreement did not include text messaging.

## Instruments

The Self-Efficacy for Appropriate Medication Use (SEAMS) scale was used to assess patients’ ability to adhere to medications in chronic disease management.31 The 13-item scale had good reliability with a Cronbach alpha of 0.89 and was administered at baseline and at 30 days follow-up.31

The first psychosocial variable of social support was evaluated at baseline using the 20-item Social Support Survey of the Medical Outcomes Study (Cronbach’s alpha of 0.91).32 The second psychosocial variable of depressive symptoms was measured by the 21-item Beck Depression Inventory,33 which had a mean alpha coefficient of 0.81; the range being 0.73–0.92 within 15 nonpsychiatric samples.34 The psychosocial variables were assessed at baseline.

# Intervention and outcomes
The primary intervention for the TM Reminders + TM Education group was TM reminders to take antiplatelet

and statin medications for 30 days after hospitalization. The TM Reminders + TM Education group received personalized, two-way TM reminders corresponding with the dosing regimen of their medications (i.e. daily or twice daily) at times designated by patients’ preferences. Twoway messages required patients to respond back with a number to confirm receipt and medication intake. An example of a medication reminder was, “John, take Plavix 75 mg at 9:00 AM. Respond with 1.” Text messages were provided from a customizable program through CareSpeak Communications mobile Health manager platform (New Jersey).

Both of the experimental groups (TM Reminders + TM Education and TM Education Alone) also received oneway health messages (i.e. not requiring response) on cardiac risk reduction on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday at 14:00. The purpose of the cardiovascular risk reduction messages was to determine whether patient education would promote self-efficacy and medication adherence. Health education messages included various topics such as diet, exercise, sleep health, depression, etc. An example was, “Patients with heart disease are at high-risk for depression. Talk with your provider if you have feelings of sadness.” The outcome measures included medication self-efficacy as well as variables related to medication adherence. The control group did not receive TMs and had usual care.

## Statistical analyses

In order to compare self-efficacy among groups, a repeated measures analysis of variance (RMANOVA) analysis was conducted to detect a change in selfefficacy between baseline and 30 days among the three groups. A RMANOVA was also conducted to compare medication adherence over time. Missing data were assumed to be missing completely at random. The criterion for significance was set a priori as α= 0.05. SPSS 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA) was used for all analyses.

To identify the variables that were associated with medication adherence at baseline, individual relationships were analyzed between each personal (sociodemographic and clinical characteristics as listed in Table 1) and psychosocial factor (self-efficacy, depression, social support) with medication adherence. Correlations were obtained for continuous and dichotomous variables while one-way analyses of variance (ANOVAs) were performed for categorical independent variables with more than two levels. Variables that showed at least a moderate correlation and had a significant relationship to medication adherence with a p-value less than 0.10 were included in a multiple regression analysis. The multiple regression analysis provided the optimal combination of the variables that explained the total percent of variance in medication

adherence as well as indicating the unique contribution of each variable to the regression model.

Medication adherence was measured by self-report with the Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-8), two-way text message responses, and Medication Event Monitoring System (MEMS) electronic pill bottles.35

# Results
## Characteristics of study participants

A convenience sample of 90 patients was recruited and completed baseline questionnaires. Six patients withdrew or were lost to follow-up. Figure 1 displays patient screening

and recruitment according to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines.36 There were no significant differences in sociodemographic or clinical characteristics among groups at study entry (Table 1). Overall, the sample had a mean age of 59.2 years (standard deviation (SD) 9.4, range 35–83), 24% were female, and 22% were non-White. Thirty-seven percent had a history of CHD and 30% had prior coronary revascularization.

## Medication self-efficacy

Medication self-efficacy among the three groups was not significantly different at baseline (p=0.49) or in followup after 30 days (p=0.72, n=82). Over time, total scores for medication self-efficacy improved (F(1,114.62)=8.32, p=0.01); however, there was no significant difference in this improvement as a function of the different treatment groups over time (F(2,6.24)=0.45, p=0.64, n=82) (Table 2).

## Variables associated with medication adherence

Age, gender, education, depression, and social support were included in a multiple regression analysis with self-reported medication adherence as the dependent variable (n=87). A total variance of 33% in medication adherence was explained by the optimally weighted combination of the five independent variables (R2=0.33, F(5,81)=7.99, p<0.001). In the multivariate analysis, male gender (β= –0.924, p=0.02), less depression (β= –0.101, p=0.03), and higher social support (β=0.042, p=0.002) were significantly associated with medication adherence, after controlling for other variables in the model. Age and education did not contribute significantly to the final multiple regression model (Table 3).

## Predictors of medication adherence

Medication adherence scores were analyzed to determine the variables that predicted medication adherence after 30

days of the text messaging intervention (n=82). With the exception of gender, the same personal and psychosocial factors identified at baseline were significantly correlated with self-reported medication adherence at 30 days follow-up at p<0.10. Group assignment was not significantly correlated with medication adherence and was not added to the model. Age, years of education, depression, and social support explained 26% of the total variance in medication adherence at 30 days (R2=0.26, F(5,76)=5.25, p<0.001). Less depression (β= –0.080, p=0.004) and higher social support (β=0.026, p=0.02) positively predicted higher medication adherence in the final model, after controlling for the other variables (Table 3).

## Medication adherence

Complete results of medication adherence are reported elsewhere.37 MEMS revealed a significant difference among the three groups for antiplatelet medication use in the percentage of prescribed number of doses taken, percentage of correct doses taken, and percentage of

prescribed doses taken on schedule (F(2,42)= 3.84, p=0.03; F(2,41)=3.29, p=0.047; F(2,41)=3.53, p=0.04, respectively). MEMS did not reveal significant differences in statin use between groups. TM response rates were higher for antiplatelets than statins (t(26)=3.1, p=0.01). Self-reported adherence revealed no significant differences among groups.
