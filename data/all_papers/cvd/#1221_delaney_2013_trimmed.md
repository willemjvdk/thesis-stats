## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# Method
## Research Design

A randomized controlled trial (RCT) was used to test the hypothesis that patients in the experimental group would show fewer hospital readmissions, improved QOL, and increased HF knowledge at 90 days than would patients in the control group

## Research Aims and Hypotheses

Specific Aim No. 1: Determine the effectiveness of the telemomitoring and self-care education intervention in reducing hospital readmission rates.

Hypothesis 1: Patients receiving the telemomitoring and self-care education intervention will demonstrate a decrease in hospital readmission rates compared to the control group.

Specific Aim No. 2: Determine the effectiveness of the telemomitoring and self-care education intervention in improving self-reported quality of life.

Hypothesis 2: Patients receiving the telemomitoring and self-care education intervention will report improved self-reported quality of life compared to the control group.

Specific Aim No. 3: Determine the effectiveness of the telemonitoring and self-care education intervention in improving patients knowledge of HF.

Hypothesis 3: Patients receiving the telemomitoring and self-care education intervention will demonstrate improved HF knowledge compared to the control group.

## Setting and Sample

The study was conducted in a large multibranch home care agency in Connecticut. The study was reviewed and approved by the Internal Review Board at the university. Potential participants who were referred to the agency following a HF hospitalization were identified by the cardiac program manager of the home care agency. Prior to recruitment in this study, all patients received 4 to 8 weeks of home care services from the home care agency participating in the study The agency had introduced TM about 5 years earlier

with the objective of improving HF care. Telemonitoring is now offered to all patients with a primary diagnosis of HF as an addition to traditional home care and had become so common that it is considered usual care. All study patients had TM during formal home care services and received skilled nursing care. Nursing care was based on evidence-based clinical guidelines for management of HF. The guidelines identify assessment parameters, educational topics, and expected outcomes for each visit.

## Eligibility Criteria

HF patients recently discharged from home care were eligible to participate in this study if they were (a) Englishspeaking adults age 21 years or older, (b) primary diagnosis of a HF, (c) hospitalized for HF excacerbation prior to home care admission, (d) living at home, and (e) had access to inhome phone line.

## Telemonitoring and Self-Care Education Intervention

Participants in the intervention group continued use of a telemonitor that was installed in the patient’s home for an additional 90 days post–home care discharge. The HomMed Health Monitor is an FDA Class II Medical Device. The telemonitor records weight, blood pressure, heart rate, and oxygen saturation. The monitor is programmed to ask patients to respond to five questions such as “are you short of breath?” and “have you had any chest pain?” Information is then transmitted over phone lines to a central server at the home care agency. The TM Program manager receives this information daily and reviews data each morning and contacts the patient if indicated. Patients are informed that this is not a 24-hr or emergency service and are instructed to go to the ER or contact their MD with symptoms of HF or chest pain.

A HF self-care booklet developed specifically for this study that incorporated ACC/AHA guidelines,24 current nursing guidelines31 and reprinted American Association of Heart Failure Nurses materials with permission was given to intervention patients.32 Content included information related to HF etiology, symptoms to monitor, diet, fluids and weight, medications, exercise, and stress management. The study nurses reviewed all materials with patients during a 1-hr session following enrollment. The purpose of this education session was to reinforce and expand on previous HF education during home care.

## Attention Control Condition

Patients were provided with a Medication Tracker diary to record their medications, any missed doses, and/or symptoms each day for 90 days. Control group patients received usual care discharge instructions. Their TM was removed on home care discharge.

## Variables and Measures

Patient outcome data were collected at home care discharge and 90 days post–home care discharge. Hospital readmission was recorded from patient self-report (validated with agency report). Hospital readmission data was assessed at 2 time points, 30 days post–home care discharge and 90 days post–home care discharge. All cause 90-day hospitalization following formal home care services was selected as the primary outcome to allow us to fully evaluate the effect of the 3-month TM and self-care education intervention. Secondary outcomes were 30-day hospitalization post– home care discharge, QOL, and HF knowledge.

QOL was evaluated with The Minnesota Living with Heart Failure (MLHF) questionnaire,33 a 21-item, selfadministered questionnaire designed to measure the effects of heart failure and treatments for heart failure on an individual’s quality of life. To measure the effects of symptoms, functional limitations, and psychological distress on an individual’s QOL, the MLHF questionnaire asks persons to indicate (using a 6-point, 0-5 self-report scale) how each of the 21 facets preventing them from living as desired. The MLHF has demonstrated reliability and validity in several studies.34-35 Most recently, the internal reliability coefficient was estimated at 0.86. Other research findings provide evidence of the concurrent and construct validity of the scale.36

HF knowledge was measured with The Dutch HF knowledge scale,37 a 15-item, self-administered questionnaire that covers HF knowledge in general, knowledge on HF treatment (including diet and fluid restriction) and HF symptoms and symptom recognition. Content validity was estimated by a panel of 10 HF experts; all items achieved 100% validation. Factor analysis was completed using principal components extraction with oblimin rotation supporting a single factor structure. The scale is used as a total scale score and reliability analysis was performed only for the total scale. Psychometric testing of the instrument with 902 HF patients provided evidence of the reliability of the instrument. The scale was able to differentiate between HF patients with high and low level of HF knowledge. Cronbach’s alpha of the scale in this sample was .62.37

## Procedure

After receiving study approval from the university IRB, participants were recruited through the home care agency prior to discharge. The adults who expressed interest in participating were told they would be contacted by a member of the research team on home care discharge. A member of the research team then met with patients who expressed interest to explain the purpose of the study, procedures, and time commitment involved; answer any questions; and invite eligible participants to participate in the study.

Prior to being randomized, patients signed a consent form, filled out a baseline demographic questionnaire, and

completed the MLHF and Dutch HF Knowledge questionnaires. Randomization was carried out in advance by a statistician at the university. Participants were randomized at a 2:2 ratio to the intervention and control groups. The study nurses were provided with a box of sealed opaque envelopes commonly used in RCTs38 that they opened after each patient had completed the baseline questionnaires. Patients then were informed of their randomization assignment.

## Statistical Analysis

The sample size for analysis was 100 (50 in the intervention group and 50 in the control group), which provided 80% power to detect a moderate reduction in the rate of hospital readmission across 90 days of follow-up. This sample size allowed for a 10% attrition rate typically found in longitudinal studies with HF patients.39 Additionally, the sample size provided 80% power to detect a difference between the intervention and control conditions in mean posttest-versuspretest changes on the Dutch Heart Failure Knowledge Scale and 93% power to detect clinically significant difference in MLHF scores of 6-points.

Demographic and clinical variables were summarized as mean and standard deviation for continuous variables and as count and percentage for categorical variables. Before hypothesis testing descriptive statistics were computed for all study variables using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences for Windows (Version 19.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) and examined for random and systematic missing data. No systematic missing data were found; random missing data were minimal and handled using median substitution.

The primary outcome of 90-day hospitalization post– home care discharge and secondary outcome 30-day hospitalization post–home care discharge were evaluated using the pearson chi-square test. Other secondary outcomes (QOL, HF Knowledge) were evaluated using repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with intervention group and time as predictors of QOL and HF knowledge.

# Results
## Flow of Participants

Recruitment began in June 2011 and ended in August 2012. The intervention was conducted for 3 months for each participant, and data collection ended in September 2012. As can be seen in Figure 1, a total of 278 patients were assessed for eligibility, 92 were excluded based on the study criteria. An additional 86 declined to participate. A total of 100 HF patients were enrolled in the study on discharge from home care. Seven patients dropped out of the study over the 90 days resulting in a 7% attrition rate (I patient died, 3 refused TM installation, 3 were unable to be contacted for followup data)

## Sample Description

Ninety-three patients completed the study (46 in the intervention group and 47 in the control group). The demographic characteristics of the two groups are shown in Table 1. The typical participant in the TM and Self-care education group was a White married woman 78 years of age with a high school education who was retired. The typical participant in the attention control group was a married woman approximately 82 years of age, with a high school degree and retired. The numbers of comorbidities and medications were similar between groups

Study patients had between 2 and 4 comorbid diagnoses, with the most common diagnoses being hypertension (52% intervention, (46% control), diabetes (36% intervention, 22% control), and arthritis (28% intervention, 25% control). The number and type of HF medications were also similar

across groups. All patients were on a combination of medications representative of current HF management guidelines. The vast majority of study patients (95%) represented Class III on the NYHA HF classification categories.

The principal difference between the groups was age and living arrangements. Patients in the control group tended to be slightly older than their intervention group counterparts (p = .021) and more patients lived alone (p = .011). There were no other statistically significant differences on other demographic characteristics or baseline QOL and HF knowledge scores.

# Primary Outcome 90-day Post–Home Care Discharge Hospitalization
For the primary outcome, 90-day all cause hospitalization post–home care discharge, there was a significantly lower readmission rate in the intervention group than in the control

group (p < .046). During the first 90 days following home care discharge, 9 patients (19%) in the intervention group and 18 patients (38%) in the control group were hospitalized. Since there were significant differences in age and living arrangements between the two groups, we conducted logistic regression analysis to test the intervention effect controlling for these variables. This significant effect remained after controlling for age and living arrangements (p = .011). Figure 2 displays all cause 90-day post–home care hospitalization rates.

Further analyses were conducted to examine hospitalization rates by comparing HF-related admissions and all other causes of readmissions. Of the 9 readmissions in the intervention group, 3 were HF-related and 7 out of 18 admissions in the control group were HF related. No significant differences were seen between groups in HF-related admissions.

# Secondary Outcomes
30-day post–home care discharge hospitalization. There was a trend toward decreased hospitalization in intervention group patients at 30 days following home care discharge. However this was not statistically significant. A total of 11 study patients were readmitted to a hospital during the first month following home care discharge (4 intervention, 7 control).

No significant differences between the intervention and control groups were observed in hospital readmission due to HF. There were 2 patients in the intervention group and 4 in the control group hospitalized due to HF within 30 days of home care discharge.

An examination of the means for the Group X Time interaction revealed that QOL scores significantly changed from baseline at home care discharge to 90 days following home care discharge in the intervention group. Preintervention MLHF scores for patients in the intervention group (mean = 45.5; SD = 18.3) were significantly lower following the intervention (mean = 32.1; SD = 15.3, p = .004). In comparison, mean QOL scores in the control group remained similar to baseline scores. No change was observed in the before (mean = 42.9; SD = 16.2) and after MLHF scores for those in the control group (mean = 42.4; SD 16.7). ANOVA was followed by linear regression analysis to test the intervention effect controlling for age and living arrangements. This significant effect remained after controlling for age and living arrangements (p = .008).

## HF Knowledge

Both groups increased their knowledge of HF over time. The increase in HF knowledge the intervention group was significantly higher (p = .019) than that in the control group (increase in control group was statistically nonsignificant). Mean baseline HF knowledge scores were 11.7 (SD = 1.8) in

the intervention group and 11.0 (SD = 2.4) for control group patients. The mean overall improvement in the HF knowledge scores in the intervention group was 1.4 points, F(1, 91) = 6.401, p =.013, compared to 0.5 points in the control group which proved to be significant. This effect also continued after controlling for age and living arrangements (p = .017). Table 2 displays the pre- and postintervention scores for QOL and depression in the intervention and control groups.

# Other Outcomes
Telemonitoring outcomes of intervention patients were assessed by recording the number of phone calls placed to patients by TM nurses and abnormal findings on daily data. Of the 46 patients in the intervention group, 15 patients were contacted by the TM nurse. The most frequent reasons for contacting patients in the intervention group were weight gain, decreased blood pressure, and increased shortness of breath. The vast majority of calls were managed on the phone and no additional skilled nursing visits were required. TM nurses instructed patients to contact their physician for any finding over the established parameters, which resulted in 4 patients having their medications adjusted. In addition, the TM nurses called 911 for two patients who reported severe dyspnea in response to the daily question related to shortness of breath on the TM transmission. Those two patients were subsequently hospitalized earlier than they may have been had the TM nurse not intervened, possibly preventing further HF decompensation.

Field notes were kept during the postintervention followup interview at 90 days to assess patients’ experiences with the intervention. Patients reported a high level of satisfaction with the continued TM and self-care education session following home care discharge. One patient stated “The TM and the education booklet helped me gain independence in caring for my HF once the nurse stopped visiting and I was on my own.” Another patient stated, “I was glad to be part of this program and hope the agency continues to offer this service after patients are discharged. I think many patients will benefit”
