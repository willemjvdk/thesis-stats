# Methods
Twenty PAD patients were recruited from Duke University Medical Center cardiology clinics over 22 months. All PAD patients were exercise limited by IC and had a resting ankle-brachial index < 0.90. Exclusion criteria included critical limb ischemia, severe peripheral neuropathy, any open nonhealing wounds, revascularization for PAD within the prior 3 months, unstable angina or severe coronary artery disease, or other conditions prohibiting safe cardiopulmonary exercise testing or training. Under research protocols approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Duke University Medical Center, all subjects provided written informed consent before participation. All subjects were sedentary at time of enrollment. The study design was listed on Clinicaltrials.gov (identification number: NCT02472561) before initiation of the study.

Prior to randomization and at 12 weeks, subjects underwent a PAD-specific maximal treadmill cardiopulmonary exercise 18 with continuous 12-lead electrocardiogram and

expired gas analysis. Expired gases were analyzed continuously using a TrueMax 2400 Metabolic Cart (Parvo Medics, Sandy, UT). Each subject was tested on the same protocol and same metabolic cart before randomization and at 12 weeks. The last 30 seconds were averaged to determine peak VO2. In addition, COT and peak walking time (PWT) was recorded.

Before randomization, physical activity patterns were captured by providing all patients a Fitbit Charge device (Fitbit, Inc., San Francisco, CA). Patients were assisted in downloading the application, creating an account and shown how to use the device. Patients wore the device for 2 weeks, and were told to continue normal activities without purposely increasing or decreasing exercise. If a patient did not own a smartphone for Fitbit use, the study provided them with a Verizon mobile smartphone. Patients were asked to synchronize their device daily to their smartphone. Patients wore the activity tracker continuously during waking hours.

After this baseline testing, patients were randomized 1:1 into either usual care or mHealth. The usual care group returned the Fitbit activity tracker, did not receive any specific lifestyle recommendations from study personnel, and was encouraged to follow the guidance provided to them by their physician. The activity trackers were returned to them to wear during weeks 11 and 12. Each patient randomized to the mHealth intervention kept their Fitbit activity tracker, was provided a personalized exercise prescription based on steps per day. Study staff had access to patient on-line accounts so that they could better support technical problems, monitor physical activity, and provide motivation and feedback during the study. The following was used to determine step prescription:

Weeks 1 to 4: Increase physical activity by 2,500 steps over baseline activity level.

Weeks 5 to 8: Increase physical activity by 3,750 steps over baseline activity level.

Weeks 9 to 12: Increase physical activity by 5,000 steps over baseline activity level.

For example, if a patient averaged 3,200 steps per day during the initial 2 week assessment, then their exercise prescription for month 1 would have been 3,200 + 2,500 = 5,700 steps/day. If achieved, Month 2 would be 3,200 + 3,750 = 6,950 steps/day. If the patient’s prescribed step count was not attained at the end of any monthly period, the subject was instructed not to increase their steps until the step goal was attained. If any patient reached 10,000 steps per day during the study, then they would be instructed to maintain this amount without further increase.

Physical activity data were downloaded weekly by investigators and summarized into reports. If patients failed to synchronize the device to their smartphone, or data looked suspicious (e.g., unusual long gaps of no data or questionable values), a call was initiated with the patient for resolution. A call was initiated if a patient’s step count fell below the prescription for 2 consecutive weeks. Data

were captured on the following in the two groups: steps per day, minutes per day of exercise, distance per day in miles, and floors navigated per day. Data were also captured on minutes performed across various intensities: Fitbit reports minutes of activity in 3 categories: light, fairly active, and very active. In an effort to describe these activities relative to government guidelines, we have reported them as low, moderate-low, and moderate-high. The rationale for this was that none of our patients jogged or ran; therefore, by government guidelines, no patient will have exercised at a “vigorous” intensity. Fitbit “light” activity levels equate to activities of daily living; therefore, they should not be considered to be meeting government-sponsored moderateintensity exercise guidelines. Based on heart rate data and observations made on cardiac rehab patients, the Fitbit fairly active and very active represents walking briskly; this equates to moderate-low and moderate-high activity. Therefore, for this study, we considered the Fitbit rating of light to low, fairly active to moderate-low, and very active to moderate-high, respectively for this study population.

Patients randomized into the mHealth group received an electronic copy of a patient appropriate PAD book.19 In addition; patients received weekly emails containing a PAD tip of the week. These weekly tips consisted of information relevant to helping a PAD patient better understand their disease and independent self-care. Each tip coincided with a chapter of the book they received. Patients were encouraged to read 1 chapter per week. In addition, patients received feedback, consultation and were given an opportunity to ask any questions monthly when staff called to adjust patient step prescriptions based on patient physical activity levels. Patients in the usual care group received the identical book in a hard copy version. No other patient education was provided to them as part of the study.

Tabular data represent mean standard deviation for continuous variables and percentages for categorical variables. Chi-square analysis was used to compare categorical variables in patient groups at baseline. A paired t test was used to determine the statistical significance of 12-week pre-to-post change differences within groups. An independent sample Student’s t test was performed to determine if change scores were different between groups. A p value of 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 24 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, Illinois).

# Results

Groups were equivalent for the characteristics of medication, risk factors, gender, ankle-brachial index, and race. Of the 20 recruited patients, 10 subjects completed the mHealth arm, and 9 completed the usual care arm. During the course of the study, 1 patient randomized to usual care dropped out due to the development and complications of a new diabetic foot ulcer.
