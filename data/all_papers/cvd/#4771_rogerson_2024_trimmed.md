# Methods and results
Participants with cardiac conditions (n = 122) were randomly assigned to self-directed or supported groups and given access to the online programme for 2 months. The programme addressed depression, anxiety, physical activity, and healthy eating. Supported group participants also received two telephone sessions facilitated by a trained counsellor to further enhance their self-management skills and engagement with the online modules. The Patient Health Questionnaire-9, Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7, and Active Australia Survey and Diet Quality Tool were administered at baseline, 2, and 6 months. \chi ^ { 2 }tests were used to compare self-directed and supported groups. Cochrane’s Q tests assessed changes over time in depression, anxiety, and physical activity (PA) and healthy diet guideline achievement. Participants in both groups showed reduced depression rates (self-directed, P < 0.05) and increased PA after programme completion (both groups, P < 0.05). Amongst those classified as depressed at baseline, significantly fewer were classified as depressed over time (P < 0.001) and significantly more were achieving the PA guidelines ( P < 0 . 0 1 )compared to those who were not depressed at baseline.

## Conclusions

The Back on Track telehealth programme was effective in assisting with behavioural and emotional recovery after a cardiac event. The programme may be particularly beneficial for those who are depressed early in their recovery period.

# Methods
## Study design

Participants were randomly allocated to one of two arms: either ‘selfdirected’ or ‘supported’ completion of the Back on Track programme. The self-directed arm involved participants completing the online modules

at their own pace without assistance. The supported arm involved a hybrid model with participants completing the online modules at their own pace plus receiving two telephone sessions with a trained lifestyle counsellor. A full description of the protocol is published elsewhere.45 As the effectiveness of the group-based version of the programme had been demonstrated previously using an RCT design,40 the present study did not include a usual care control condition.

Data were collected at three timepoints: prior to participants accessing the programme (baseline); at the conclusion of programme access (postprogramme, 2 months after baseline); and at follow-up 4 months later (6 months after baseline). Figure 1 shows the study design and participant numbers.

## Sample size and power

The sample size was based on a systematic review of trials of telehealthbased CR where effect sizes on promoting healthy lifestyle ranged from 0.42 to 1.29.33 Conservatively, we anticipated a medium effect size of 0.55. Allowing for 80% power and a significance threshold of.05, the estimated sample size was 120 (60 per group), taking into consideration an expected attrition of 12% based on prior studies with similar post-programme time points.30,46

## Participants and recruitment

Participants were adults (over 18 years of age) who had experienced a recent cardiac event, namely acute myocardial infarction (AMI), coronary artery bypass graft surgery (CABGS), or percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). Eligibility required understanding written and spoken English, and internet and computer access.

Participants were recruited through cardiac health professionals, community-based cardiac support organizations, namely Heart Support Australia and Heartbeat Victoria, and social media, including announcements about the trial on Facebook, Twitter, and LinkedIn accounts of the ACHH. A promotional flyer was used for all promotion and recruitment.

## Data collection

Eligibility screening occurred at the time of participant registration via the ACHH website using a set of brief screening questions. All eligible participants were emailed the participant information and consent form via the secure web-based data management and survey programme, Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap). Consented participants then completed the baseline questionnaire. Participants were randomly allocated to the self-directed or supported groups using an independent and automatic random allocation numbering system.

Participants re-completed the main outcome measures using the REDCap survey link at 2 and 6 months. The initial participant was enrolled in May 2020 and the final data collection occurred in February 2022.

## The ‘back on track’ intervention Online modules

The online modules address goal-setting, healthy eating, PA and reduced sit ting, smoking cessation, and emotional well-being. The programme is based on the self-regulation theory4 and uses strategies from CBT and MI. Self-regulation theory underpins several behavioural interventions to support change, such as self-monitoring, feedback, reward, and goal setting.48,49 Likewise, CBT has been used successfully to assist people with heart disease in self-management of both behavioural50 and psychosocial51 risk factors. MI has also been shown to improve engagement and decrease resistance52 and can enhance CR outcomes.53 Research suggests that outcomes are optimal when these theories, interventions, and strategies are integrated.54,55

Participants in both groups completed modules relevant to them, in their own time and in any order over the course of 2 months. Throughout the programme, participants review situations in their lives and identify, challenge, and change unhelpful thoughts and beliefs associated with their recovery. They develop action plans and coping plans for implementing practical health behaviours, including goal-setting, and identifying motivators for change, resources, barriers, rewards, and relapse prevention strategies. The programme uses strategies to increase self-efficacy, including testimonials, and exploring ratings of importance of and confidence to undertake lifestyle changes. Sample screenshots from the online modules are shown in the supplementary material online (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1).

The behavioural recommendations included in the modules (e.g. healthy eating, PA, and smoking cessation) are based on the current National Heart Foundation of Australia (NHFA) guidelines.56 While not the central focus of the programme, this information was provided to assist participants in selfmanagement, including goal-setting.

## Telephone sessions

Supported group participants were offered two telephone sessions with a trained lifestyle counsellor to assist them in goal-setting and overall selfmanagement while undertaking online modules. Session duration was 45– 60 min for session 1 and ≤30 min for session 2. Session 1 occurred 1–2

weeks post-enrollment. The counsellor began the session with rapport development and then screened for anxiety and depression using the Patient Health Questionnaire-4 (PHQ-4)57 and assessed self-efficacy regarding lifestyle change and ambivalence about behaviour change.

Telephone sessions followed a written manual developed by a health psychologist. MI strategies were used to support participants to identify areas they wished to address, to articulate clear and achievable goals, and to develop action plans and coping plans. At completion of the first session, the second telephone session was scheduled for a mutually agreed time (approximately 4 weeks later). The second telephone session included a reflection on self-efficacy, action and coping plans, anxiety or depression symptoms, maintenance of behaviours, and relapse prevention. If the counsellor considered that the participant required more intensive mental health support, referral options for counselling were discussed.

To ensure treatment fidelity, lifestyle counsellors received initial training and fortnightly supervision with an experienced registered health psychologist.

## Measures

Sociodemographic and medical information was collected at baseline only. All participants completed the outcome measures at baseline, 2, and 6 months. Smoking status was used as a descriptive variable only due to so few smokers at baseline. Programme acceptability was assessed postprogramme using a six-item scale developed for this study.

# Outcome measures
## Depression

Depression was assessed using the nine-item { \mathsf { P H Q } } { \cdot } { \mathsf { P } } { \mathsf { I } } ,a brief depression screener. Scores range from 0 to 27. In patients with cardiac conditions, research has demonstrated an optimal PHQ-9 threshold of ≥559,60 (sensitivity = 83% and specificity = 76%60) as compared to the usual threshold of ≥10. Thus, participants scoring ≥5 were classified as depressed. The PHQ-9 has been validated with cardiac patients59 and has been endorsed by the NHFA as the recommended tool for depression screening.61

## Anxiety

Anxiety was assessed using the seven-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder instrument (GAD-7).62 Scores range from 0 to 21. Participants scoring ≥5 were classified as anxious.62 The GAD-7 has good reliability and validity for detecting generalized anxiety63 and has been validated in both primary care62 and cardiac populations.64

## Physical activity

PA was measured using the eight-item Active Australia Survey ( A A S ) ^ { 6 5 }which assesses frequency of walking, moderate and vigorous activity in the last 2 weeks. The AAS has acceptable reliability and validity in the Australian community66 and CR populations.67 Participants who engaged in ≥150 min per week across the three domains were classified as achieving the recommended target for adequate PA for secondary prevention.56

## Dietary intake

Diet quality was assessed using the 13-item Diet Quality Tool (DQT).68 All questions relate to nutrients of concern in CHD prevention (e.g. fruit and vegetable, saturated fat, total fat, ω-3 fatty acids, fibre, and salt intake). Each item is scored from 0 to 10, where 10 indicates that the participant is meeting the NHFA’s secondary prevention nutrition guidelines.56 Participants were classified as achieving a healthy diet for CHD secondary prevention if they received a total DQT score >60%.68 The DQT is a reliable and valid dietary assessment tool for secondary CVD prevention.68

## Additional measures

Basic demographic (namely age, sex, country of birth, employment status, educational level), psychosocial (partner status, living arrangements, financial strain, close confidante, recent bereavement, private health insurance), medical (other physical and mental health conditions, smoking status), and event-related information (event type, time since event, and attendance at

CR) were collected via a self-report questionnaire, using standard questions used in previous ACHH studies.69

## Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was granted by the Deakin University Human Research Ethics Committee (2019–438). This trial was registered with the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ACTRN12620000102976) and conducted and reported in accordance with the CONSORT-EHEALTH guidelines.70 This project was funded by the HCF Research Foundation.

## Statistical analysis

t-tests (for continuous variables) and \chi ^ { 2 }tests (for categorical variables) were used to compare self-directed and supported groups on baseline characteristics, number and type of modules completed, and acceptability ratings. To check the representativeness of the final study sample, t-tests and χ2 tests were also used, to compare baseline characteristics for completers vs. non-completers at both 2 and 6 months.

Dichotomous variables were created for the four primary outcome measures to determine the proportion of participants who were depressed, anxious, and achieving the recommended PA and dietary guidelines at each timepoint. For comparisons between the self-directed and support groups, and between the depressed and non-depressed groups, Cochrane’s Q-tests were used to determine change over time in the proportion of respondents classified as depressed (PHQ ≥5), anxious (GAD7 ≥ 5), achieving the PA guidelines (minutes ≥150 per week), and having a healthy diet (DQT scores >60). These analyses were restricted to participants who had completed questionnaires at all three timepoints. If a significant Cochrane’s Q-test was found, a pairwise comparison was undertaken using McNemar test to determine differences between baseline and post-programme, baseline and 6 months, and post-programme and 6 months. To address our primary aim, we also analysed differences between the self-directed and supported groups at each time point using post-hoc χ2 tests. Analyses were undertaken using intention to treat. The significance level was set at P < 0.05 for all analyses. Differences between P > 0.05 and P < 0.10 are reported as trends towards significance.

# Results
## Characteristics of the study sample

In total, 122 participants were recruited into the trial between May 2020 and August 2021 and randomized to the self-directed (n = 58) or supported (n = 64) groups. Participants ranged in age from 31 to 91 years (mean 60.94, SD 12.23). Most had their cardiac event in the preceding 12 months (94%) with two-thirds being within 3 months post-event (66%). Table 1 shows participant characteristics according to group allocation. Apart from self-directed group participants being more likely to be experiencing considerable or extreme financial strain (χ2 = 5.57, P = 0.018), there were no other significant group differences at baseline.

## Participation in follow-up assessments

At 2 months, 37 participants (64%) from the self-directed and 43 (67%) from the supported group completed the questionnaire. At 6 months, 40 participants (69%) from the self-directed and 44 (69%) from the supported group completed the questionnaire. The completion rate did not differ significantly between groups at either timepoint ( \chi _ { 1 } ^ { 2 } =0.16, P = 0.69; χ2 = 0.00, P = 0.98, respectively). Non-completers at 2 months were more likely than completers to be non-university educated (χ 21= 6.50, P = 0.01), while non-completers at 6 months were significantly younger than completers (mean age 57.52, SD 12.53 vs. 62.48, SD 11.85; F = 4.41, P = 0.04). There were no other differences between completers and non-completers.

## Completion of online modules

The number of online modules completed ranged from 0 to 5, with a mean (SD) of 2.65 (1.86) modules per participant. Supported group

participants completed a significantly higher number of modules than did self-directed group participants (mean 3.09 vs. 2.17 modules, respectively; F _ { 1 , ~ 8 3 } = 6 . 6 3 , ~ P = 0 . 0 1 ). Most participants in both groups completed at least one module [n = 30 (80%) self-directed; n = 39 (89%) supported]. Of these, a majority completed three or more modules [n = 17 (43%) self-directed; n = 29 (66%) supported], while the remainder completed one to two modules [n = 15 (38%) self-directed; n = 10 (23%) supported]. A minority did not complete any modules [n = 8 (20%) self-directed; n = 5 \ ( 1 1 \% )supported]. The proportion of participants completing each module is shown in Figure 2.

## Participation in telephone sessions

Amongst supported group participants (n = 64), 44 (69%) completed both telephone sessions, 14 (22%) completed one, and 6 (9%) completed neither telephone session. Topics covered were commonly

PA (n = 37; 58%), healthy eating ( n = 2 2 ; 3 4 \% ), and emotional wellbeing (n = 17; 27%). Fewer participants discussed goal setting (n = 9; 14%) and smoking cessation ( n = 1 ; 2 \% ). Total telephone session duration ranged from 30–127 min (mean 72.61, SD 32.58 min).

## Back on track programme acceptability

Almost all participants rated the programme as highly acceptable (Figure 3). Of those who completed the post-programme questionnaire (n = 78), most agreed (somewhat or strongly) that the programme had helped them make lifestyle changes ( n = 7 4 ; 9 5 \% )), gave them strategies to help them manage their behaviours (n = 72; 92%) and emotions (n = 67; 86%), and had increased their confidence to manage their behaviours (n = 73; 95%) and emotions (n = 67; 86%). Almost all agreed that they would recommend the programme to others with heart

disease ( n = 7 7 ; 9 9 \% ). There were no differences in acceptability ratings between the self-directed and supported groups.
