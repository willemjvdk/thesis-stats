# Improving Outcomes for African Americans with Chronic Heart Failure: A Comparison of Two Home Care Management Delivery Methods
Mary D. Bondmass, RN, PhD

Available data indicate disparity in heart failure (HF) morbidity and mortality among African Americans. African Americans are diagnosed with HF at a younger age, have a more rapid clinical progression, and have higher hospitalization rates related to HF than Whites. Lack of inclusion of African Americans in research studies has been suggested as a possible contributing factor to this disparity. This study presents a secondary analysis from a randomized trial comparing biopsychosocial outcomes for only the African Americans whose HF home management was provided by one of two home care delivery methods—nurse telemanagement (NTM) or nurse home visits. Results indicate significantly higher self-efficacy, home care satisfaction, and quality of life, with significantly lower symptom distress, HF rehospitalization, and intervention charges for African Americans whose HF home management was delivered by the NTM method. These data suggest, independent of race, that NTM may be more efficient and effective as a delivery method for HF home management.

## THE PROBLEM

Independent of race or ethnicity, chronic HF is a complex clinical syndrome characterized by frequent occurrences of physiologic decompensation resulting in the need for inpatient hospitalization. Although the problem of decompensated HF may be related to multiple factors, it remains a major obstacle to effective outpatient management of chronic HF. A decompensation of chronic HF is often manifested with severe signs and symptoms of intravascular and interstitial volume overload, including shortness of breath, pulmonary and peripheral edema, and/or acute manifestations of inadequate tissue perfusion to the brain, heart, and kidneys (Konstam et al., 1994). Moreover, decompensated chronic HF requires clinical management to be moved from the outpatient to the more costly inpatient hospital setting. The magnitude of the problem is evidenced by data indicating increasing numbers of HF-related hospital readmissions costing billions of dollars annually (AHA, 2006; NHLB, 2004).

Most HF experts believe that the problem of decompensated chronic HF could be prevented and outcomes improved by implementing evidence-based care guidelines for the multifaceted management of this disorder

(Advisory Council to Improve Outcomes in Heart Failure [ACTION HF], 1999; American College of Cardiology/ American Heart Association [AHA/ACC], 2001, 2005a, 2005b; Heart Failure Society of America, 2006; Konstam et al., 1994). However, little evidence exists that suggests the most effective and/or efficient methods to deliver the outpatient home care described in the past and present HF guidelines.

Proceeding from the available evidence that African Americans with HF have greater morbidity and mortality and have historically been underrepresented in research studies, an in-depth secondary analysis is presented here of only the African Americans who participated in a previously reported study comparing outcomes of two home care delivery methods (Benatar, Bondmass, Ghitelman, & Avitall, 2003; Bondmass, 2002). For clarity, this secondary analysis is hereafter simply referred to as the current study. African Americans were separated out for this analysis so as not to decrease the power of finding any differences, which may have been confounded by racial and/or ethnic characteristics, and to add instrumental subscale analyses not presented in the original study. The current study was approved by the University’s Institutional Review Board (IRB) and supported, in part, by a grant from the National Institutes of Health (NIH).

The purpose of the current study is an in-depth comparison of physical, psychological, and socioeconomic outcomes for African Americans with chronic HF, whose home care was delivered by either of two home care management delivery methods. Nurse telemanagement (NTM) and nurse home visits (NHV) are the two home care management delivery methods compared. The physical, psychological, and socioeconomic outcomes include HF self-efficacy, symptom distress, quality of life, HF readmission data (hospitalizations and charges), intervention charges, and satisfaction with home care management.

The overall research question asked here is, Are there differences in physical, psychological, and socioeconomic outcomes for African Americans with chronic HF whose home care management is delivered by either the NTM or NHV method?

# METHOD
## Design and Setting

As noted above, the current study is a secondary analysis of a previously reported study that utilized a prospective randomized design (Benatar et al., 2003). The study setting was participants’ homes, beginning the day of discharge from a HF hospitalization (enrollment/baseline data collected) and concluding 90 days later. Participant recruitment was conducted on the medical–surgical units of a large, Midwest, urban University Medical Center and an associated Veterans Administration Hospital. Potential participants were identified though daily searches of the Medical Center’s information-system computers for HF admissions and from physician/nurse referrals. All identified eligible participants were asked to participate.

## Procedure

Eligible patients were randomized to receive either NTM or NHV following their respective hospital discharge. Preintervention (baseline) data collection was accomplished using the Heart Failure Self-Efficacy Scale–30 (HFSE-30), Heart Failure Symptom Distress Scale (HFSD), Minnesota Living with Heart Failure Questionnaire (MLHF), and the Demographic and Other Information Form (DOIF). Following 90 days of either intervention delivery method (described below), the participants were contacted by phone, and postintervention data were obtained. Postintervention data were collected with the same preintervention questionnaires plus a Home Care Satisfaction Survey (HCSS). Health care utilization data (readmissions, length of stay, hospital charges, and the intervention delivery costs) were obtained from the participant and from the participant’s medical record at the study conclusion. Both intervention groups continued scheduled clinic follow-up with their regular health care providers throughout the duration of the study.

NTM delivery method. Telemanagement was defined for the current study as the use of telecommunications to determine and implement management interventions in the absence of face-to-face contact. At the time of the current study, the NTM method was considered a new home care management delivery method that incorporated an APN working collaboratively with a physician to telemanage chronically ill patients.

The materials needed for the NTM method include a home monitor attached to an integral scale stand, a data-receiving computer, and a network server connection. Other than the University’s network server, all hardware and software were developed by and purchased from AvidCare, formerly Advanced Medical Devices (Milwaukee, WI). All devices used were extensively validated prior to the current study and subsequently approved for use by the Federal Drug Administration (FDA) in 1996.

Home monitor and scale. The home monitor (HM) is a small, lightweight device (64mm x 152mm x 89mm, 680g) that automatically notifies patients to obtain their vital signs. The display on the device has a

readability equivalent to the fifth-grade reading level. The device shares patients’ existing telephone lines and does not cause interruption of existing phone service, except during data transmissions. The HM was programmed for the current study to measure blood pressure (BP), heart rate (HR), arterial oxygen saturation (02 sat), and weight. Greater details on the HM and its development and utility are reported elsewhere (Schiller, Bondmass, & Avitall, 1997).

For the NTM delivery method, participants measured their own vital signs daily with the HM. The device automatically transmitted these data, via the participant’s existing telephone line, to an electronic network server connected to the University’s Medical Center. The APN accessed the data daily via any personal computer with an electronic connection to the University’s network server. If any participant’s data were aberrant or outside their preset physiologic parameters, an alarm was generated, and the APN received an automatic alphanumeric page providing the participant’s name, phone number, and the specific aberrant data. The APN then telephoned the participant, conducted an assessment, and directed interventions over the phone if needed. Interventions might have included, but were not limited to, patient education and medication titration and/or changes. The APN also called each participant weekly, independent of aberrant data alarms, for a general assessment and to address participant and/or family disease-management education issues. The APN coupled the transtelephonically transmitted objective data with information solicited from the participant during phone conversations to evaluate physiologic status and general compliance with the treatment plan. HF management for these participants was based on existing clinical guidelines (ACC/AHA, 2001; Konstam et al., 1994) and, other than care received in their respective outpatient clinics, was conducted exclusively over the phone.

NHV delivery method. Participants randomized to the NHV intervention received HF-specific home care provided by one of three participating home care agencies, all utilizing HF clinical pathways, and all with self-described cardiac nurse specials. Participants in the NHV group were managed with their agency’s respective clinical pathways for a period of 90 days. The agency’s clinical pathways were all based on existing clinical guidelines (ACC/AHA, 2001; Konstam et al., 1994) and, therefore, contained the same content regarding the standards of care for HF as in the NTM method. This included the performance of physical,

psychosocial, environmental and compliance assessments, medication administration, patient/family education, and disease management counseling. Although the agencies had similar number of visits in their respective pathways, the number of visits could vary for each patient depending on case severity; however, generally the number of visits ranged from 3 to 12.

Heart Failure Self-Efficacy Scale–30 (HFSE-30). Overall HF self-efficacy and self-efficacy with medications, diet, symptoms, activity, and HF readmission were measured with the overall and respective subscale scores of the HFSE-30. The HFSE-30 was developed by Perry (2000) and consists of questions specifically designed to assess perceived self-efficacy of HF patients.

The HFSE-30 queries participants on how sure they are about taking care of their HF-related self-care activities in five categories that make up the instrument’s subscales: taking medicine, following a low-salt diet, observing and reporting their symptoms, physical activity, and avoiding HF hospitalizations. Questions are answered on a 0 to 10 rating scale, proceeding from 0 (doesn’t apply to me) to 1 (never can do), and 5 or 6 (maybe can do), through 10 (always can do). Scoring consists of summing the responses for items within each subscale and calculating a mean for the number of items rated from 1 to 10 (items rated as not applicable are eliminated); the total score is the sum of all the subscale scores. Initial Cronbach’s alphas were obtained for the HFSE-30 and subscales at two measurement times and ranged from .64 to .93. Test–retest reliability coefficients ranged from .51 to .82, all p < .01 (Perry, 2000).

Heart Failure Symptom Distress Scale (HFSD). Symptom distress was measured with the HFSD (Bondmass, 2002). The HFSD was developed from previous instruments (Cohen & Hoberman, 1983; Friedman & King, 1995) and modified to include 17 physical problems/symptoms most frequently attributed to HF patients (Dracup, Walden, Stevenson, & Brecht, 1992; Friedman, 1997; Friedman & King, 1995; Mayou, Blackwood, Bryant, & Garnham, 1991; Rector & Cohn, 1992; Romm, Hulka, & Mayo, 1976).

Participants are asked to rate how much each problem had bothered or distressed them during the past month. Responses are recorded on a Likert-type scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 4 (extremely). The HFSD is scored as the sum of all the responses, with higher scores indicative of more distress from physical symptoms. Possible scores can range from 0 to 68. Initial Cronbach’s alphas

for the HFSD were reported as .86 and .96 pre- and postintervention, respectively (Bondmass, 2002).

Minnesota Living with Heart Failure Questionnaire (MLHF). The MLHF, a 21-item questionnaire developed by Rector, Kubo, and Cohn (1987), provides an overall total score and physical and emotional subscale scores relating how persons with HF perceive the effect that HF has had on their lives. The MLHF asks the question, “Did your HF prevent you from living as you wanted during the last month by . . .” before each item. The response scale of each item ranges from 0 (no) to 5 (very much). The total score is the sum of the responses to the 21 items, with the subscale scores equal to the sum of each of the respective subscale items. Total scores can range from 0 to 105, with higher scores indicative of a less favorable experience of living with HF. Physical dimension scores can range from 0 to 60, and emotional dimension scores from 0 to 45.

Test–retest assessment and weighted kappa indicated initial reliability for the MLHF (r = .80, p < .01, kappa = .84). Criterion validity was also reported with participants’ NYHA functional classifications significantly correlated with MLHF scores (r = .60, p < .01) (Rector et al., 1987). More recently, reliability testing indicated Cronbach’s alphas of .94, .94, and .90 for the total, physical, and emotional dimension scores, respectively (Rector & Cohn, 1992).

Home Care Satisfaction Survey. Participants’ satisfaction with their respective home care and delivery method was measured by six items that make up the HCSS designed for previous studies (Benatar et al., 2003; Bondmass, 2002). Responses are recorded on a Likert-type scale ranging from 0 to 5 (1 = poor to 5 = excellent, 0 = does not apply). Scoring consists of summing the item responses; higher scores indicate higher satisfaction.

Demographic and other information form (DOIF). HF readmissions and hospital charges were tabulated by the researcher after submitting search request forms to area hospitals for these data (all participants signed a medical records release of information for the current study). Participants were also queried as to whether they were hospitalized (where and when if applicable) to capture any out-of-area hospitalizations. The DOIF was used to organize hospital readmission data and to record intervention charges made available from administrative records of the respective home care providers. The DOIF also included information provided by the participants

and obtained from hospital records regarding age, gender, marital status, smoking history, alcohol intake, income, education, health insurance, social-living arrangements, medications, comorbid conditions, other physical impairments (mobility, hearing, vision), and primary medical service provider.

## Data Analysis

Descriptive, chi-square, independent t test, and repeated measure ANOVA were used in the current study dependent on the measurement level of the data. Univariate analysis is reported here unless indicated otherwise. Either the statistic or the name of the statistical test is noted on the tables and figures used to present the current study’s results.
