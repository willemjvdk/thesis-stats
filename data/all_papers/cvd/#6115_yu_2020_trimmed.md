# Methods
The MISSION-2 study was supported by the Ministry of Science and Technology of China and the Prevention and

Control Project of Major Chronic Non-infection Disease during the 13th 5-year plan period (No: 2016YFC1302000), the capital's funds for health improvement and research (No: CFH; Shoufa 2016–1-4031), and the Beijing Municipal Commission of Science and Technology (No: D171100002917001). The authors are solely responsible for the design and conduct of this study, all study analyses, the drafting and editing of the manuscript, and its final contents.

## Study design

The trial design has been described previously.30 In brief, the study was a large scale, multicenter, open-label, randomized controlled trial. The trial was conducted at five teaching hospitals (one of the hospitals dropped out at the beginning of the study) with annual CABG number N100 cases.

All study participants provided written informed consent. The National Coordinating Center (NCC) and Fuwai Hospital led the study design, management, data auditing and analysis for this study. The Ethics Committee of Fuwai Hospital approved the study design, and all participating sites accepted the central ethics approval or obtained local approval by internal ethics committees.

## Participants

Patients were eligible if they underwent an isolated CABG (no other cardiac surgery, such as valve replacement surgery or surgical ablation for atrial fibrillation, was performed except for CABG) and were 18 years of age or older, had been prescribed at least one secondary preventive oral medication within 2 weeks after surgery, had access to a smartphone and were able to operate at least three smartphone applications. Patients were excluded if they were unable to understand Chinese text, could not attend the planned 6-month follow-up or were unable to provide informed consent. The four hospitals included in this study are located in different regions across China, and they serve culturally and socioeconomically diverse populations. Patients undergoing CABG at these hospitals received information on the study protocol and provided written informed consent for their participation.

## Randomization

Participants were randomly assigned to the smartphone application intervention group or the usual care control group in a 1:1 ratio using the password-protected computerized randomization program on the Chinese Cardiac Surgery Registry (CCSR) website (http://ccsr.cvschina.com). The randomization process was stratified by the study sites and overseen by the NCC.

## Intervention

In developing the smartphone application (Heart Health Application), we first reviewed the adherence literature and adopted a user-centered design process to

thoroughly investigate patient needs.31 We then translated these needs into a set of functional requirements and design guidelines, creating four modules focused on secondary prevention: medication reminders, cardiac health education, health questionnaire and feedback, and a personal data center.30,31 We conducted iterative cycles of prototyping and user testing to maximize the user experience and promote adoption by patients.

In the smartphone application intervention group, researchers helped participants download and install the application, and they demonstrated the operation of using the four modules after randomization. The participants' discharge medications and other related information were input by our well-trained nurses before the patients were discharged. During the follow-up period, the application automatically reminded the participants when it was time to take each medication, and participants clicked the application bar of the reminder to confirm that the medicine had been taken. Second, there were many educational readings on secondary preventive cardiac care that originated from scientific guidelines, and participants could read them at any time during the follow-up period. Third, participants could receive an 8-item questionnaire about medication adherence and secondary prevention goals, such as blood pressure (BP) and body mass index (BMI), each week through the application messaging service. After finishing all the questions, participants would receive feedback, encouragement, and advice about their secondary prevention status and performance. Participants received these messages from the application directly. Participants could conveniently view their individual cardiovascular risk factor control levels, recent blood biochemistry test results, and secondary prevention targets.30

Participants randomized to the control group received standard post-CABG care. They received cardiology education, instruction on CABG secondary prevention, and promotion of self-care management from our research nurses and physicians during the inpatient stay after randomization.

## Data collection

The dataset for this study included the following four modules, which we have been described previously: baseline in-hospital information, 3- and 6-month follow-up data, and interactive information from the application. 30

For baseline participant information, participating sites imported their in-hospital data into the CCSR database. Researchers conducted face-to-face interviews with participants at the 3-month and 6-month follow-up visits. The researchers who were not blinded to group allocation measured outcomes. Prior to the start of the study, all researchers in this trial received uniform training. Data collected at reviews included the translated Chinese version of the 8-item Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-8),32-34 self-reported secondary

preventive medication information (including names, dosage, and frequency of the drugs taken), BP, BMI, smoking status, recent cardiovascular events and rehospitalizations. At the end of the interview, after the blinded follow-up assessment, participants in the intervention group then completed the application user feedback questionnaire, which included three modules: frequency of utilization, whether the app would be used after the trial or not and usefulness of the application. Participants who were unable to attend the face-to-face interview were asked to complete the questionnaires by telephone.

As to MMAS-8, we have been trained by Morisky team, obtained the certification and the full use right. Respondents indicated a “yes” or “no” answer to each item, except for “How often do you have difficulty remembering to take your binders?”, which was rated using a 5- point Likert-type scale. Overall scores were computed by summing those responses for a total possible score of 8. The MMAS-8 can additionally be broken down into 2 subscales—intentional nonadherence, characterized primarily by failing to take medications when asymptomatic or when experiencing side effects, and unintentional nonadherence, characterized primarily by forgetting.32-34

In addition, we recorded the application transaction log files on the application server station for each participant. We acquired information from the application server station directly about the frequency of application usage and whether the weekly questionnaire and the daily medication reminder feedback were completed in time.

# Outcome measures
The primary outcome was CABG secondary preventive medication adherence as measured by the MMAS-8 at the 6- month visit after randomization. We defined low medication adherence as MMAS-8 score b6 which has been applied by other trials.35-37 Secondary outcomes were mortality, major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCE), cardiovascular rehospitalizations, self-reported secondary preventive medication use after 6-month followup, BP, BMI and self-reported smoking status.30 Secondary outcomes were assessed at the 6-month follow-up visit. MACCE was defined as a composite of death, myocardial infarction, stroke, and unplanned revascularization. Smoking status and secondary preventive medication use were assessed via self-report.

## Statistical analysis

We previously estimated that a sample size of 1000 participants would provide 80% power and a 5% level of significance to detect a 10% to 15% decrease in the percentage of participants with low adherence according to the MMAS-8 at 6 months. This sample size allowed for a 20% loss to follow-up during the study period.

All analyses were conducted using the intention-to-treat principle. We used frequencies with percentages to describe categorical variables, and we used means with

standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges to describe continuous variables. We compared baseline participant characteristics between the control and intervention groups using chi-square tests for categorical variables and Student's t-tests for continuous variables. We also compared patient outcomes using these methods.

We conducted sensitivity analyses that considered participants who withdrew from the study, or were lost to follow-up as nonadherent. In addition, we assessed the effect of the intervention on adherence in prespecified subgroup analyses based on age, sex, level of education, and risk factors for cardiovascular disease. We exported log files to analyze the participants' engagement with the smartphone applications. All tests of significance were 2- tailed, with an α of .05. The statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). Investigators who were blinded to group allocation conducted statistical analyses.

# Results
Between September 2015 and September 2016, 2653 patients were screened for eligibility. A total of 1000 patients were enrolled and randomly assigned to the intervention (n = 501) or control group (n = 499); 1653 individuals did not meet all eligibility criteria or declined to participate (Figure 1). At the 3-month follow-up, 3 participants were unable to be contacted, and 3 participants had died; at the 6-month follow-up, 5 participants were unable to be contacted, and 2 participants had died. Therefore, the primary outcome was available for 987 participants (98.7% of eligible randomized participants). The median duration of follow-up was 6.2 months. The mean age was 57.28 (SD, 9.09) years, and 85.5% of the participants were male. The baseline characteristics were similar between the intervention and control groups (Table I).

# Primary outcome analyses
At the 3-month follow-up, the proportion of lowadherence participants categorized by MMAS-8 scores was 6.3% in the intervention group and 5.8% in the control group (RR = 1.080, 95% CI 0.641-1.822, P = .791); at the 6-month follow-up, the proportion in the intervention group was 11.8%, and the proportion in the control group was 11.7% (RR = 1.005, 95% CI 0.682-1.480, P = 1.000) (Figure 2). Similar results were found in the sensitivity analysis, in which participants with missing data for medication adherence were considered as having low adherence (Supplemental Figure 1). In the subgroup analyses, there were no significant differences in the primary outcome across the subgroups tested (Figure 3).

At 6-month follow-up, there were no significant differences in the mean MMAS-8 score between the intervention group and the control group (mean difference 0.052, 95CI -0.087 to 0.191, P = .460). Also, there were no significant differences in intentional nonadherence (mean difference

0.038, 95% CI -0.037 to 0.112, P = .318) or unintentional nonadherence (mean difference 0.014, 95% CI -0.074 to 0.103, P = .749) between the intervention group and control group.

## Secondary analyses

There were no significant differences in death, rehospitalization, or MACCE between intervention and control groups. There were no differences in BP, BMI, number of participants identifying as non-smokers or selfreported secondary preventive medications between the two groups after 6 months of follow-up (Table II).

## Feedback from patients in the intervention group

Among the 501 participants in the intervention group, 379 (75.6%) submitted feedback via the research questionnaire about the use of the smartphone application. According to the feedback, only 17.4% of participants used the application every day, and 44.6% of participants often forgot to use the application; only 15.0% of participants thought the application was very useful, and more than half of the participants thought the application was of little use or useless. Approximately 81.3% of participants said they might continue to use the application (Supplemental Table I).

## Use rate and response rate of the smartphone application

The proportion of participants who used and operated the application during the first month of the study was 88.1%. However, the use rate decreased sharply to 42.5% during the second month. The rate of participants who responded to medication reminders and health questionnaires also decreased over time. By the end of the study, the use rate and response rate were both under 10% (Figure 4).
