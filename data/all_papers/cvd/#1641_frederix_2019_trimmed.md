# Methods
## Study design and study population

This paper presents the results of the TEMA-HF 1 longterm follow-up study (NCT03171038). TEMA-HF 1 was a

multi-centre, prospective, randomised controlled trial conducted in Belgium. The study protocol and short-term results were described previously in detail.8 Briefly, 160 patients with chronic heart failure and reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) were included after eligibility screening and randomisation (time point: t _ { 0 } ). All patients had to be optimally treated for heart failure according to guidelines at the time.2 The inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in Supplementary File I. Patients were block randomised by sealed envelopes (1:1) to six months of intense follow-up facilitated by telemonitoring (telemonitoring group, TM group) or usual care (usual care group, UC group). Patients were included after an initial hospitalisation for heart failure. After TEMA-HF 1 (time point: t _ { 1 } ), 142 of them were still alive and entered the long-term follow-up study (Figure 1). The follow-up study was conducted according to the principles stated in the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki, local and national regulations. Its study protocol (B.U.N. B14320072881) was approved by the coordinating Ethics Committee. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to enrollment. The TEMA-HF 1 longterm follow-up study adheres to the relevant CONSORT standards of reporting (See Supplementary File II).

## Study intervention

Study intervention: The telemonitoring group. The TM group patients were randomised and enrolled at time point t _ { 0 }(Figure 1). They initially received six months of intensive follow-up, facilitated by telemonitoring (between time points: t _ { 0 }and t _ { 1 } ). During these months, patients were instructed to measure their body weight, blood pressure and heart rate daily by a standardised protocol. The recorded data were forwarded to a central computer. In case the recordings were outside the predefined alert limits, both the general practitioner and heart failure clinic were alerted by email. At that moment, per protocol, the general practitioner (or cardiologist) was asked to visit or contact the patient and to adapt the treatment if he/she felt it was necessary. The heart failure nurse contacted the patient by telephone 1–3 days after the alert to verify whether the intervention had been effective. The telemonitoring phase was followed by the long-term follow-up phase (between time points: t _ { 1 }and t _ { 2 } ) ;during which no telemonitoring was provided and patients received standard heart failure care. The follow-up phase ended after the last long-term followup study chart review (at time point: t ).

Study intervention: The UC group. The UC group patients were randomised and enrolled at time point t _ { 0 }(Figure 1). They initially received only standard heart failure care (no telemonitoring phase) between time points t _ { 0 }and t _ { 1 } .. The ‘no telemonitoring’ phase was followed by the follow-up phase (between time points: t _ { 1 }and t _ { 2 } ) ;during which patients again received standard heart failure care. The follow-up phase ended after the last long-term follow-up study chart review (at time point: t2).

# Study endpoints/outcomes
The primary endpoint of the TEMA-HF 1 long-term follow-up study was all-cause mortality.

The secondary outcomes included:

Both day procedures (<24 h) and hospital admissions (>24 h) were defined as readmissions. The TEMA-HF 1 long-term trial investigators retrieved all readmission data from the electronic health records in the recruiting hospitals. All identified readmissions were cross-checked with those on file in the patients’ medical insurance records as confirmation. The reasons for readmission were determined by the trial investigators based on the discharge reports. A resident in cardiology subsequently adjudicated the reasons for readmission as an independent assessor, blinded to treatment allocation. Only the primary reason

for readmission (i.e. first diagnosis) was adjudicated, one diagnosis per readmission was identified.

## Healthcare-related costs

The cost analysis was performed from a patient and healthcare perspective. The combination of readmission costs, the costs due to cardiologist and nephrologist follow-up visits and diagnostic tests performed, constituted the healthcare costs. Productivity losses were not taken into account, since the vast majority of study participants were retired. The readmission costs (both those due to: 1. heart failure, 2. cardiovascular reasons other than heart failure, and 3. non-cardiovascular reasons) were derived from their invoices, which were retrieved from the hospitals’ financial departments. The nomenclature-based tariffs quantified the cardiologist follow-up visit, nephrologist follow-up visit and diagnostic test denominations.

## Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS v.22 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA), according to the intention-to-treat principle by the assigned treatment group. All the available data were used. No data imputation was performed for missing values; assuming that the reason for the

missing values was not related to the condition of the patient, nor to the treatment received. The sample size for TEMA-HF 1 (N ¼ 160) was calculated based on the a priori sample size calculation to detect an effect size of 0.30 in the primary endpoint between the TM group and UC group at the end of the (no)telemonitoring phase (with a statistical power of 80%, working with type I error level 0.05 and a dropout rate of 20%).12 The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess whether normal distribution was present. Nonparametric tests were used in case that the normality assumption was violated. Independent t tests (parametric) or Mann–Whitney U tests (nonparametric) were used for between-group analyses of continuous variables. Chi-square tests were used in the case of categorical variables. Fisher’s exact tests were used when expected frequencies were small. Data were presented as N (%) for categorical variables and both mean - SD and median [Q25–Q75] for continuous variables for reasons of completeness. P-values <0.05 (two-sided) were considered to be statistically significant. Cumulative survival curves for the time-to-event (i.e. death) were constructed according to the Kaplan–Meier method. The difference between the two curves was assessed with the log-rank test statistic. The log–log plot and the comparison of curves for the different strata was used to confirm that the proportional hazards assumption was valid. The Cox regression model was applied to calculate the hazard ratio (HR), with treatment (i.e. telemonitoring) as the only covariate. Censoring was applied when the follow-up period terminated before the event occurred.

For the analysis of days lost due to death or heart failure readmission, the percentage of follow-up time lost because of death or heart failure readmission was defined as the number of days lost by the intended follow-up. For patients who survived during the followup period, the percentage of follow-up was defined as the number of days lost (due to readmission) divided by the follow-up time (i.e. up until the censoring date). For patients who did die during the follow-up period, the number of days lost between the date of death and the date of intended follow-up plus the number of days spent in hospital was counted. Additional sub-group analyses were performed. They assessed whether the CHF patient’s age (5 75 years versus < 75 years) or his/her disease severity (EF 5 35% versus EF < 35%; NYHA Class I/II/III versus NYHA Class IV) would make a difference in terms of the impact of telemonitoring.

# Results
Of the 160 CHF patients initially enrolled in TEMA-HF 1, 142 patients were alive at the end of the (no)telemonitoring phase (t1) and entered the long-term follow-up study. The average age was 76 - 10 years in both the TM and UC group (U ¼ 2587, z ¼ 0.32, p ¼ 0.75); 49 (64%) and 44 (67%) were male in these respective study groups (-2 (1): 0.08, p ¼ 0.78). There were no

differences in the baseline patient characteristics between the study groups (see Table 1). The median patient followup time was 79 months (range of 68–89 months). The final analysis date was 1 August 2015.

At the end of the TEMA-HF 1 long-term follow-up period (t ), all-cause mortality was not significantly different between the study groups: 57 (71%) subjects died in the TM group versus 54 (68%) in the UC group ( \chi ^ { 2 } ( 1 ) ;: 0 . 2 6 , p = 0 . 6 1 ). The average (95% confidence interval [CI]) time to event (i.e. death) was 1626 [1432–1821] days for the TM group and 1356 [1130–1582] days for the UC group (p ¼ 0.32; HR 0.83 (0.57–1.20) (Figure 2).
