## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# Outcomes variables
Access to expert clinicians may be an issue for some HF patients; however, it is imperative for patients to receive accurate and consistent information, to feel confident in their abilities, and to have the mental energy to appropriately manage their condition, maintain functional status, and experience an acceptable level of quality of life. Data from clinical trials suggest that disease management programs that promote self-care maintenance (adherence to positive health practices such as appropriate diet, exercise, and medications) and management (attending to subtle changes in health status and taking action to treat those changes) may improve outcomes for patients with HF.20,22,23 Key outcomes are self-efficacy, functional status, depression, and health-related quality of life.

## Self-efficacy

Having confidence in one’s abilities may promote positive self-care practices. Selfefficacy refers to the self-confidence of an individual to perform a specific activity. The stronger the efficacy beliefs generated by an intervention, the more likely a person is to use personal resources and foster the effort necessary to engage in healthy behaviors.24 Providing intervention in the form of education about disease management, recommendations for increasing activity, and peer support have demonstrated positive changes in self-efficacy in HF and in cardiac surgery patients.25,26

## Functional status

Maximizing physical functioning as a way to achieve a high-level of well-being is a primary goal for individuals with HF. Functional status has been found to be a strong predictor of length of hospital stay, early readmission, nursing home placement, and mortality.27,28 Also, re-hospitalization can lead to further decreases in functional ability. Wolinsky and colleagues,29 studied 7,527 noninstitutionalized older adults, 428 of whom had HF. Having been hospitalized for HF was related significantly to greater increases in the mean number of functional limitations at followup. Home visit interventions that include assessment and education for HF are expected to improve functional status, leading to decreased HF-related morbidity and mortality.30 However, most studies have considered the effect of home visits on readmission rates rather than functional status.

## Depression

Depression occurs in 19% to 30% of the elderly31,32 and may contribute to the development or exacerbation of chronic cardiac diseases or conditions.33–35 Among participants with the highest cumulative mean depression scores in the Cardiovascular Health Study (n  4,493), the risk of cardiovascular disease increased by 40% and the risk of death by 60% when compared with those who had

the lowest mean depression scores.36 In HF patients, mood disturbances have been found to be higher than normative values37 and to impair activities of daily living over time.33 Few studies have considered the impact of telephonic management, home visits, or inhome telehealth communication on depression in individuals with HF.

## Health-related quality of life

Health-related quality of life refers to the impact an individual’s health has on level of productivity, ability to function in daily life, performance of social roles, intellectual capabilities, and emotional status.38,39 Previous studies have indicated health-related quality of life in individuals with HF may be diminished because of physical symptoms, psychological problems, adverse treatment effects, and social limitations.40

In a study of HF patients being evaluated for heart transplant, Dracup et al.38 found that self-reported functional status, depression, and hostility accounted for 43% of the variance in total psychosocial adjustment, one component of health-related quality of life used in this study. Baas and colleagues41 conducted a study in which 38 HF patients were placed in one of two groups: modeling, in which a model of the client’s world is built but no therapeutic interventions beyond the usual are provided; or role-modeling, in which therapeutic interventions such as unconditional acceptance, nurturance and facilitation, goal setting with promotion of client advocacy, strengths, positive orientation, and trust building were provided to achieve or maintain optimal health.

Results indicated the mental composite score from the health-related quality of life measure and physical symptoms predicted activity level. Although many studies have considered quality of life in HF patients, few have considered the effect of a posthospital follow-up intervention on quality of life. In those studies where home management was addressed, intensive education was provided in the hospital before discharge with little or no in-home follow-up.37,42

Telephone nursing or telephone triage has long been recognized as a strategy for economical management of chronic health conditions.

## Home management interventions

Interventions provided to HF clients have taken many forms and have demonstrated various levels of effectiveness. Telephone nursing or telephone triage has long been recognized as a strategy for economical management of chronic health conditions43,44 and has been effective in producing favorable outcomes for clients within organizations,45 but the effects for those with HF are not commonly singled out. Telephonic case management may vary widely depending on the education and expertise of the nurse telephonic case manager, the caseload any one nurse is expected to manage, and the relationship with the physician provider.46–48

Home health care has also demonstrated effectiveness as an intervention for helping clients maintain functional status and prevent exacerbation of HF, but the effectiveness may vary depending on the specific services provided. Despite the effectiveness of home health, one study found that 12% (n = 13) of HF participants were readmitted before completion of the home care plans.49 Innovative, cost-effective options for maintaining or improving outcomes for HF patients are needed.

In recent years, technology has become increasingly interactive, less expensive, standardized, and available. However, the uptake of technology in health care has been slow. One option for improving HF outcomes may be the use of in-home telehealth communication devices such as the Health Buddy®. A follow-up intervention that provided comprehensive, daily interaction post hospitalization was evaluated to determine its effectiveness in producing individuals who are more knowledgeable and confident of their ability to participate in the maintenance and management

of their HF, who have improved functioning, diminished depression, and improved healthrelated quality of life.

# METHODS
The design of this pilot study was a randomized clinical trial in which participants were randomized to four groups. All groups received the same educational content of the HF disease management program (Table 1). However, method of delivery and number of inhome contacts differed between groups. The first group received telephonic case management similar to that provided by nurses who telephonically monitor large client caseloads. The second group received five home visits by a research nurse for assessment and education related to HF, an intervention designed to be similar to visits provided by home health nurses. The third group received assessment and education through a telehealth communication device called the Health Buddy®. The fourth group received assessment and education through both the five home visits and the Health Buddy®. Table 2 provides a detailed outline of the research groups and their respective activities.

## Setting

Data were collected in the homes of participants who were capable of living independently and who had been discharged from the hospital in the previous 6 months with a primary diagnosis of HF.

## Sample

After Institutional Review Board approval, the sample was selected from 140 persons who had been recently dismissed from the hospital with a diagnosis of HF and referred to the HF disease management program by their health maintenance organization. Inclusion criteria were oriented to person, place, and time, no visual impairment, ablity to speak and read English, and having an active phone service. After talking with a research nurse, 103 participants were eligible, agreed

to participate, and enrolled in this study. Because of other debilitating chronic illnesses (eg, renal failure or cancer), death, and moves out of the area or into nursing homes for 13 subjects, 90 participants completed the study through 2 months. Eighty-nine percent of participants lived in a large metropolitan area. All

participants were on medication to manage their HF, with 87% of participants on diuretics, 51% on angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, 50% on digoxin, and 37% on betablockers. Table 3 provides a description of the demographic characteristics of participants in the four research groups and in the total

sample. There were no significant differences between groups based on demographics.

## Telehealth communication device

The Health Buddy® (Figure 1), a simple, user-friendly, readily visible, telehealth communication device, allows interaction between a participant and the health care provider in the individual’s home. Participants received a set of questions and health-related comments daily. Questions are displayed on a liquid crystal display (LCD) screen of the Health Buddy® and the individual responds by pushing one of four buttons on the front of the box. The Health Buddy® allows the health care provider to script specific information for a particular person or can provide a generic script based on specific disease conditions or behaviors to be changed. Programming provided by the investigator for this study included questions about symp-

tom occurrence and behavior modification, provided educational tips, and conducted some outcome assessments. The programming was used to monitor and manage participant symptoms and the potential for problems or complications; monitor and manage participant compliance with and response to treatment; and reinforce health behaviors or changes in methods of disease management.

The device, which attaches to the phone line, automatically calls a toll-free number during the night. During this phone call, participant responses from that day are downloaded via the Internet and the next day’s content is uploaded to the Health Buddy®. Patient responses are available to the health care provider after the download is complete. Commonly, the health care provider reviews responses the next day. At this time, if symptoms or behaviors are not improving as expected, or if the participant’s health status is deteriorating, the health care provider may

call the client for further assessment and recommended intervention.

Content for the educational components and tips used in the scripting of Health Buddy® were based on the HF disease management program (Table 1), which was based on the literature and the Agency for Healthcare Policy and Research Guidelines.12 A sample question used in the Health Buddy® is included in Table 4. Patients were provided with directions for further care if their response indicated a need for medical intervention. If a patient provided a response that did not require intervention, a health tip was provided. In addition, if participants provided low risk or no risk responses, the Health Buddy® pro-

vided recognition and reinforcement for positive health behaviors.

## Measurement of variables

Measures included the Barnason Efficacy Expectation Scale-Heart Failure (BEES-HF), the Mood Scale, the 6-minute walk test, and the Medical Outcomes Study Short Form-36 (SF-36). Demographics assessed in this study included age, gender, race, educational level, income, marital status, living arrangements, and medication use.

## Self-efficacy

Self-efficacy regarding HF management was measured by the BEES-HF, a 16-item tool,

modified with the permission of the original authors from a 15-item tool developed for coronary bypass surgery patients and designed to reflect the specific disease management components of HF. The BEES-HF measures an individual’s level of confidence in making lifestyle and behavioral changes related to managing their HF. Responses for each item ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree. The higher scores reflect greater confidence. The original tool demonstrated construct validity and internal consistency reliabilities of 0.92–0.95.50 The reliability for the modified BEES-HF in the current study was α  0.83.

## Functional status

Functional status was measured using the 6- minute walk test, a simple test in which people are asked to walk as far as they can on a flat surface in 6 minutes. This test objectively assesses exercise capacity in individuals with HF, strongly predicts morbidity and mortality in patients with left ventricular dysfunction, and is considered to be a reliable measure of a patient’s functional capacity.51,52 Participants could walk inside their home, apartment, or an interior hall of their building, or walk outdoors provided a flat surface was available and weather permitted.

## Depression

Depression was measured by the Mood Scale, a 15-item tool with yes/no responses and a short form of the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS). The GDS was designed specifically for rating depression in the elderly.53 Possible scores for the Mood Scale range from 0 to 15, with scores greater than five suggestive of depression. Concurrent validity and reliability (α = 0.77) of the Mood Scale were established.54,55 The reliability for the Mood Scale in this study was α  0.77.

## Health-related quality of life

Quality of life was measured by the SF-36, a multidimensional scale measuring eight health dimensions: physical functioning (10 items), role limitations because of physical problems (4 items), social functioning

(2 items), bodily pain (2 items), general mental health (5 items), role limitations because of emotional problems (3 items), vitality (4 items), and general health perceptions (5 items).56 Each of the dimensions is scored on a scale of 0 to 100, with a higher score indicating better quality of life. Item-discriminant validity as well as reliability reported for each subscale in a HF population ranged from 0.78 to 0.92.57

## Data collection

After talking with a research nurse and agreeing to participate in the study, participants were randomly assigned to one of the four research groups. All participants were then given a baseline, in-home focused assessment and an educational notebook including printed materials covering content as outlined in Table 1.

Data collected during the initial visit included demographics, self-efficacy, functional status, depression, and health-related quality of life. Two months from the time of enrollment, all participants received another home visit during which a focused assessment was completed as well as measurement of selfefficacy, functional status, depression, and health-related quality of life.

## Data analysis

Frequencies and measures of central tendency and dispersion were calculated for description of the sample. Mixed model analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to determine if there were any differences in patient outcomes among the four methods of delivering the home management intervention. Data were relatively complete as the research nurses asked the questions on the instrument. However, if a subject did not provide complete data, they were dropped from analyses that included that measure.

# RESULTS
## Feasibility of using the Health Buddy®

To ensure that content for the Health Buddy® was consistent with the disease management program, two of the researchers

spent approximately 75 hours each transcribing 60 days of content. During the time an individual had a Health Buddy®, a researcher monitored daily the participant’s responses and made follow-up phone calls when responses suggested the individual was at risk for exacerbation of his or her HF.

Out of 66 patients originally assigned to the Health Buddy® (Group 3) or Health Buddy®/Home Visit (Group 4) groups, 20 (30%) were not able to use a Health Buddy® and not eligible for inclusion, or in some cases, continuation if they had already begun the study. Of those, four potential participants were not interested in being inconvenienced by the technology and two were too weak or ill because of other health conditions. Another six participants lived in retirement apartments and had no available outlets for the Health Buddy’s® electrical cord. Additional impediments to use of the Health Buddy® included eyesight that was too poor to accommodate reading the Health Buddy’s® LCD screen; moving after agreeing to participate with no phone service in the new location; a physician request that the patient be removed from the study because the Health Buddy®, as used in this study, provided only HF assessment and educational content; and living in multiple locations without a consistent place in which to access the Health Buddy®.

# Outcomes of disease management intervention
A mixed model (method by time) ANOVA compared outcome variables. For selfefficacy, a significant group by time effect (p = 0.027) was found. Post hoc analysis using Scheffe’s test found a significant difference (p < 0.05) for the telephonic group, with the telephonic group demonstrating decreased confidence in their ability to manage their HF and all other groups demonstrating increased confidence. There were no other significant group-by-time interactions, nor were any group effects significant. Effect sizes were estimated to provide information about the

strength and integrity of the intervention. Using baseline data, effect sizes for all variables ranged from 0.06 to 0.32. The remainder of the discussion focuses on changes over time regardless of group.
