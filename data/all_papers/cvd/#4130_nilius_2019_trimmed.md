# Methods
## Study Design

This study was designed to examine the effect of telemedicine on PAP usage time (primary endpoint), quality of life (QoL), and blood pressure (BP; secondary endpoints) in a group of poststroke patients diagnosed with moderate to severe OSA. The telemedicine concept consisted of remote monitoring of the PAP devices in the randomized intervention group and included telephone advice and support in times of declining usage (<4 h/night over a week).

The patients were informed about the study during the first anamnesis upon being admitted to hospital. The same patients had already participated in a pre-investigation designed to test the efficiency of an OSA screening during neurorehabilitation after stroke. In case of a positive diagnosis of moderate to severe sleep apnea (AHI >15/h), the patients received a positive pressure device (ICON, Fisher and Paykel Healthcare, New Zealand). In addition, all devices were equipped with the telemonitoring transmission module during the rehabilitation phase. The patients participating in the telemedicine trial were randomized into the telemedicine care (TC) or standard care (SC) group shortly before they were discharged from hospital.

## Inclusion/Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows: patients who had suffered an ischemic stroke within the last 3 months; a moderate to severe baseline OSA with an AHI >15, that had been confirmed in the sleep laboratory; physical capability to operate a PAP device and mask (Barthel Index question 5 [ability to dress unassisted] >5 points); age <75; CPAP naive; no COPD; and regular PAP usage (>3 h/night) during the inpatient phase.

## Therapy Initiation

Therapy was uniformly initiated in all eligible patients, that is, after a positive diagnostic polysomnography (PSG; AHI >15) with Alice 4 (Philips Respironics, Murrysville, PA, USA), patients were visited by sleep laboratory staff, and a training session and mask adjustment followed before the initial therapy PSG. The device used was usually an APAP device, set to a pressure of 4–18 cm H O for the sake of uniformity. The clinical routine of the sleep laboratory included a second PSG with predefined CPAP pressures overnight (titration) if the results of the first night of therapy were insufficient (AHI exceeds 10/h, central apnea index >5/h, unnecessarily high APAP pressure). All PSG measurements were especially evaluated by experienced sleep specialists in accordance with the AASM 2012 standards [26]. The therapy devices were linked to a Global System for Mobile Communications (GSM)

module from the first day onward and connected to the webserver (Infomart Web, Fisher and Paykel Healthcare, located.eu) during the rehabilitation phase in all potential study subjects. This allowed for controlled supervision and visits by sleep specialists to ensure good adjustment during the initial phase. All patients received this intensive inpatient care during the initial phase of PAP therapy. The patients were randomized into the telemedicine care group (TC) or the SC group on the last day before being discharged from the rehabilitation clinic, and only the TC group kept the GSM module for home use. Because of the additional module and the nature of intervention, blinding of the investigator or the patients was not feasible. All study participants received standard instructions on how to use the devices at home and had the opportunity to contact the clinic or the service provider if they experienced any difficulties or problems when using the devices.

## Baseline Measures

Baseline patient data included age, smoking status, BP measured after 5 minutes of resting in a sitting position between 9 and 11 o’clock, neck circumference, comorbidities, grading of neurologic deficits by the National Institute of Health Stroke Scale, Barthel Index, the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) [27], STOP-BANG scale (snoring, tiredness, observed apnea, BP, body mass index, gender) [28] (Short Form Health Survey [SF-12], which assesses the global health status of patients with chronic conditions on a physical and mental scale) [29], and the nasopharyngeal complaints (NPC) questionnaire [30, 31].

## Standard Care

In accordance with standard practice, all patients went home with a PAP device and the sleep laboratory informed the homecare provider about the therapy settings and equipment. The patients were advised to visit their primary care physician or lung specialist after being discharged from the hospital or the homecare provider if they experienced any difficulties or problems with the mask or the device itself. In addition, all patients had the opportunity to contact the study site.

## Telemedicine Care

The telemedicine concept was based on telemonitoring, telephone calls, and remote interventions. The online data of the TC group (i.e., adherence, leakage, pressure, AHI) were anonymously transferred to the password-protected web server each morning. The usage data were sent automatically, without requiring any user input. We evaluated the data for relevant therapy details each week, starting 7 days after the individual discharge date of each patient.

A color code provided an overview of the main treatment details of the study participants at a glance. In cases of suspected therapy problems, the data were evaluated in more detail, and the patient was contacted via telephone if the weekly mean usage time had dropped below 4 h/night. During the telephone call, which was limited to 5 min, we endeavored to solve the usage problem or motivate patients with a negatively framed educational message and reinforced their knowledge about their sleep apnea treatment by conducting a standardized semi-structured clinical interview questionnaire. The platform also enabled online access to therapy options of the device (e.g., humidification). A personal visit by the service provider was offered, if the problem could not be solved online or by means of telephone advice.

All interventions were categorized to define the main reason for contacting the patient (motivation, mask problem, problems operating the device, nose/throat dryness).

## Follow-Up Measures

A visit to the sleep laboratory was offered to the patients of both arms of the study after 6 months, although this was not mandatory. Patients that were not able or did not wish to visit the sleep laboratory were visited at home in order to download the therapy data from the device and to conduct the follow-up questionnaires (ESS, SF-12, STOP-BANG, NPC). BP was measured under the same circumstances as the baseline examination, but only in those patients who visited the clinic for follow-up care and check-up.

The closing date had previously been arranged at 6 months ± 2 weeks. Usage hours and runtime of the device were objectively measured via the device’s built-in counter, and all device protocols were downloaded on the closing date.

## Statistics

The primary measure in this study was the mean therapy adherence, which was evaluated on the basis of the total number of hours the device was used, divided by duration of the study in days. The sample size evaluation was based on the studies of Hoy et al. [32] and Bravata et al. [19]. Hoy et al. [32] found an adherence difference of 1.5 h/night after 1 month in favor of the intensive care group. An important distinction is that that group only included OSA patients who had not suffered a stroke. Bravata et al. [19] found a usage time of 5.1 ± 2.8 h/night in OSA patients after stroke. Based on their findings, the sample size calculation at α = 0.05 and a power of 90% estimated that 37 patients would be required to complete the study in each arm. With a dropout rate of approximately 10%, the recruitment target was 40 participants in each arm. Randomization was achieved using a simple randomization technique with a predefined list. Since only 5 patients dropped out of the study, we evaluated the data per protocol. The outcomes compared between the groups are expressed as mean and SDs and analyzed using the independent Student t test. The effect size for the primary outcome (PAP adherence after 6 months in minutes used per day) was evaluated and presented according to Cohen’s classification of effect sizes [33].

# Results
According to the concept of the pre-investigation that tested the efficiency of an OSA screening during neurorehabilitation after stroke, 253 consecutive patients were asked to accept a baseline polysomnography. Two hundred and sixteen consented to the PSG. One hundred and seventeen participants were diagnosed with an AHI >15 (54.2%), 12 refused treatment outright, 5 dropped out of the study due to scheduling issues (unexpected early discharge date), and 100 patients agreed to the initiation of positive pressure therapy and were supplied with a PAP device. Nine refused the overnight therapy PSG offered after daytime PAP training and 11 used the device very

irregularly (<3 h/night) and thus met an exclusion criterion for the telemedicine trial.

Eighty subjects fulfilled the inclusion criteria for randomization into the telemedicine study. The average rehabilitation phase was 3 2 . 8 \pm 1 0 . 9days. The patients suffered a stroke on average 3 6 . 7 \pm 4 3 . 8days before admission to rehabilitation, and the PSG diagnostic was conducted 2 1 . 0 \pm 1 0 . 8days before discharge and 48.4 ± 43.5 days after their most recent stroke.

During the study, 3 patients of the telemedicine care intervention group (TC) and 2 of the SC group dropped out. Reasons included 1 recurrent stroke (TC) and 4 patients had been lost to follow-up (2 TC, 2 SC). No contact was possible in the case of all 4 patients who had been lost to follow-up, and no telemetric data arrived from the 2 lost TC patients.

Seventy-five participants (20 females) with a baseline AHI of 39.4 were included in the final analysis group (Fig. 1). The average questionnaire scores and other baseline measures of the evaluated study group are presented in Table 1. The patients showed the following additional baseline characteristics, compared by chi-square test: known arterial hypertension (TC: 78.4%, SC 81.6%, p =

0.25968), previous stroke prior to the recent stroke (TC: 32.4%, SC: 23.7%, p = 0 . 5 7 4 5 6 6 ), chronic heart failure (TC: 29.7%, SC: 31.6%, p = 0.86215), and current or exsmoker (TC: 70.2%, SC: 68.4%, p = 0 . 7 4 3 9 5 ). The randomized groups were well matched in the measured baseline values, in particular the first inpatient compliance during the rehabilitation phase (TC: 5.0 ± 1.7 h/n, SC: 4.8  ± 1.4 h/n, p = 0.652). The control group was slightly older and had a slightly lower body mass index.

# Clinical Outcome and BP Checks
Not all patients accepted the invitation to attend the follow-up PSG at the sleep laboratory to deliver clinical data such as BP and AHI. The main reason for this was the large geographical area served by the rehabilitation clinic, and some patients refused to travel that far to spend a night in the sleep lab. The follow-up BP results

of the subgroup of 55 patients differed substantially. During the baseline examination, BP was 136/82 ± 21.1/8.9 mm Hg in the TC group and 134/80.3 ± 17.2/10.6 mm Hg in the SC group. Eighty percent of the patients were diagnosed with arterial hypertension and were prescribed at least one antihypertensive medication (78.4% in the intervention group, 81.6% in the SC group). We were able to collect the follow-up clinical outcome with BP readings of 55 patients. BP was measured under the same conditions as the baseline examination. An analysis of the compliance data in this subgroup showed a difference of 5.0 ± 2.4 in TC versus 2.4 ± 2.4 h of usage per night in the SC group and the systolic values in the TC group improved to 129.9 ± 15.2 mm Hg, whereas the systolic BP of the SC patients was 138.8 ± 16.1 (p = 0.034). Diastolic pressure was 78.4 ± 11.1 mm Hg in the TC group and 82.8 ± 9.2 mm Hg in the SC group (p = 0.120).
