# Methods
## Design

The study was a randomized, controlled, unblinded trial of 6 months’ duration with comparison of the effectiveness of antihypertensive treatment based on telemonitoring of home BP (intervention group) and conventional office BP monitoring (control group).

## Ethics

The study was approved by the local ethics committee (ClinicalTrials.gov identification number NCT 00282334). All participants provided written informed consent before entering the study.

## Enrolment

Between April 2004 and April 2006, 10 general practitioners consecutively recruited the study participants among their patient populations.

## Participants

Inclusion criteria were: age 20–80 years, both men and women, newly diagnosed or treated but not controlled hypertension with elevated office BP (w150/95 mmHg or systolic BP w150 mmHg and diastolic BPv90 mmHg). Exclusion criteria were: 24-h ambulatory BP monitoring (ABPM) (125/ 80 mmHg, atrial fibrillation (ECG at randomization) and lack of mental or physical capacity to perform HBPM. Withdrawal criterion was: withdrawal of consent to participate.

## Randomization

A random number generator produced a random allocation sequence. Allocations were transferred to numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes and opened sequentially by the participants after enrolment at the Department of Medical Research.

## Number of participants

A power calculation indicated that a sample of 204 patients was necessary to detect a 5-mmHg difference in systolic daytime ABPM with a power of 90%

at a significance level of 5%, assuming a standard deviation of 11 mmHg.

## Effect variables

Primary outcome was defined as difference in systolic daytime ABPM change from baseline to follow-up between the intervention and control group. Secondary outcomes were: difference in diastolic daytime ABPM change from baseline to follow-up between the intervention and control group, differences in systolic and diastolic nighttime ABPM change from baseline to follow-up between the intervention and control group, differences in ABPM changes in relation to age, gender and diabetes, and finally differences in number of patients who achieved normal daytime ABPM and target BP (home BP in the intervention group, office BP in the control group) after 6 months. Normal daytime ABPM was v130/85 mmHg or lower if diabetic. Target home BP was v135/85 mmHg and 125/75 mmHg for non-diabetics and diabetics, respectively. Target office BP was v140/90 mmHg and 130/80 mmHg for non-diabetics and diabetics, respectively (2,20).

## Procedures

Intervention. Patients in the intervention group were provided HBPM equipment. This equipment included a validated (21), semiautomatic oscillometric BP measuring device (Omron 705 IT, Omron Matsusaka Co., Ltd., Japan) connected to a personal digital assistant (Hewlett Packard iPAQ Pocket PC, Hewlett-Packard Company, Houston, TX, USA) with a software interface developed for BP measurement (Bang & Olufsen Medicom, Struer, Denmark and CIM Electronics, Hasselager, Denmark). BP measurements were transferred to a central server by a PDA-embedded mobile phone unit. Doctors accessed their patients’ BP measurements on a secure home page. Each patient could access their own BP measurements on this web site, where they could also communicate with their general practitioner by e-mail. For patients with no Internet access, the PDA could record and send spoken messages to the general practitioner, who could respond by written messages to the PDA.

Patients measured their BP between 09:00 and 20:00 h, three times a week during the first 3 months and once a week during the last 3 months. Each BP measurement consisted of three readings with 1-min intervals.

During the study period, patients were instructed to perform home BP measurements according to the protocol, to follow the antihypertensive treatment instituted, and to contact their general practitioner by e-mail or recorded message if any adverse effects occurred. Other doctor–patient contacts not regarding BP, measurement of BP or antihypertensive treatment could be performed as usual. General practitioners were instructed to give their patients instruction in correct BP measuring technique (2,20), to check the web site on a weekly basis to monitor BP levels of their patients and contact patients if BP measurements were not performed, and to institute or change antihypertensive treatment at their own discretion with the goal to achieve target home BP for each patient.

Control group. During the study period, patients were instructed to visit their general practitioner as often as needed and to follow the antihypertensive treatment instituted. The frequency of office visits was decided by the patients’ usual doctor according to usual practice. Office BP was measured in the seated position by the general practitioner after at least 10 min of rest. Office BP was measured with the same type BP device as used for HBPM. General practitioners were instructed to institute or change antihypertensive treatment at their own discretion with the goal to achieve target office BP for each patient.

ABPM. At randomization and follow-up, participants underwent 24-h ABPM. Validated (22), oscillometric and fully automatic devices (TM 2430, Bosch+Sohn Gmbh, Jungingen, Germany) recorded BP at 15-min intervals between 07.00 and 23.00 h and 30-min intervals between 23.00 and 07.00 h. Daytime and night-time BP were calculated according to individual waking and sleeping times as noted by each patient. Fitting of ABPM devices and laboratory investigations were done at the Department of Medical Research.

General practitioners provided information on cardiovascular disease and risk factors, time since diagnosis of hypertension, and number of prescribed antihypertensive drugs at randomization and follow-up.

## Analysis

Randomized patients were included in the final analyses on an intention-to-treat basis. If patients did not participate in the follow-up visit and ABPM therefore lacked, the result of the baseline ABPM was carried forward to follow-up. In the intervention group, the last home BP (home BP after 6 months for patients, who completed the study or the last

transmitted BP for patients, who discontinued HBPM) was used in analysis of achievement of target home BP. Likewise, in the control group, the last office BP (after 6 months for patients, who completed the study or the last transmitted BP for patients, who discontinued the study) was used in analysis of achievement of target office BP.

In analysis of home BP measurements, the average of the last two BP readings of each measurement was used. Average BP for each of the 26 weeks of intervention was calculated for each patient. The average home BP was based on at least six BP readings/week during the first 13 weeks and at least two readings/week during the last 13 weeks (in total >104 BP readings).

In analysis of office BP measurements, the number of office visits during the study period was recorded. BP in the first and last month (or average of BP taken during the first and during the last month, if patients had more than one clinic visit) was used for each patient.

Nocturnal dipping was defined as a reduction in average systolic and diastolic BP at night greater than 10% compared with average daytime values. Accordingly, a BP night/day ratio v0.9 was used to characterize patients as dippers.

## Statistical analyses

SPSS statistical software (Version 13.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA) was used. Unpaired t-test was used for comparison between groups and paired t-test for comparisons within groups. Pearson x2 test was used for comparisons of proportions. Results are given as mean (SD) unless otherwise stated. Significance level was set at pv0.05.

# Results
## Demographics

In total, 244 patients were considered eligible by their general practitioners. Of these, seven had 24-h ABPM (125/80 mmHg and one declined after further explanation and therefore had to be excluded, leaving 236 patients for randomization. Of these, 13 patients did not attend follow-up. According to the intention-to-treat principle, the study is based on results from the 236 randomized cases. Figure 1 shows flow of participants through the study. Baseline characteristics and laboratory findings are shown in Table I. In both groups, about half was diagnosed with hypertension within 1 year prior, 60/113 (53%) in the intervention group and 56/113 (50%) in the control group (information not provided for 10 patients).

## Dropouts

As shown in Figure 1, eight patients from the intervention group and five patients from the control group were lost to follow-up. Characteristics of dropouts in the intervention group: three women and five men, mean age 62 years (range 40–73), and

in the control group: two women and three men, mean age 57 years (36–69).

Primary outcome (change in systolic daytime ABPM)

At baseline, systolic daytime ABPM was similar in the two groups as shown in Table II. In both

groups systolic daytime ABPM decreased significantly from baseline to follow-up, but not more so in the intervention group. Decrease in systolic daytime ABPM was 211.9 mmHg in the intervention group and 29.6 mmHg in the control group (mean difference 22.3 [95% CI 26.1 to 21.5], p50.225.

# Secondary outcomes
Change in diastolic daytime ABPM. At baseline, diastolic daytime ABPM was also similar in the two groups as Table II shows. After 6 months, diastolic daytime ABPM was decreased significantly in both groups. However, the BP decrease was not significantly different between the two groups.

Change in systolic and diastolic night-time ABPM. Table II shows that both systolic and diastolic night-time ABPM were similar in the intervention group and the control group at baseline. After 6 months, both systolic and diastolic night-time ABPM was significantly lowered in both groups, but no significant difference was found between the two groups.

Heart rate. Mean heart rate (beats/min) during daytime was similar in the two groups at baseline (intervention group: 73 (9) beats/min, control group: 73 (9) beats/min) and after 6 months (intervention group: 74 (10) beats/min, control group: 72 (9) beats/min). Mean heart rate during night-time was also similar in the two groups at baseline (intervention group: 64 (9) beats/min, control group: 65 (9) beats/min) and at follow-up (intervention group: 64 (9) beats/min, control group: 62 (7) beats/min).

## Subgroup analyses

Age. The study sample was divided into groups according to age (age v60 years or >60 years) in

order to analyse the influence of age on the effect of intervention.

No differences in daytime ABPM were found between age groups or between intervention and control groups at baseline. However, in the older age group, systolic daytime ABPM tended to be lower in the intervention group than in the control group. Table III shows that among older patients the decrease in systolic and diastolic daytime ABPM was almost twice as high in the intervention group as in the control group, although these differences were not significant.

Gender and diabetes. Analyses revealed that neither gender nor diabetes influenced the effect of intervention.

Dipping status. At baseline the number of dippers was similar in the two groups, as 72/113 (64%) in the intervention group and 75/123 (60%) in the control group had a BP night/day ratio (0.9, p50.550. After 6 months, the same numbers were 75/113 (66%) and 69/123 (56%), respectively, p50.095. Dipping status was improved in 14/113 (12%) in the intervention group and 13/123 (11%) in the control group and the number of patients with unchanged or worsened dipping status were also the same in the two groups, p50.434.

## Target BP

Normalization of daytime ABPM. After 6 months, fewer patients in the intervention group than in the control group had normalized daytime ABPM, although this difference was not statistically significant. Daytime ABPM v130/85 mmHg was reached by 32/113 (28%) in the intervention group and 46/123 (37%) in the control group, p50.139.

Home BP measurements. In the first week, mean home BP was 148 (16)/89 (8) mmHg and 12% (13/ 113) had home BP below target. In the last week,

mean home BP was 134 (14)/80 (8) mmHg. Mean difference in systolic home BP was 214 mmHg (95% CI 11–17, pv0.001). ## Antihypertensive treatment

After 6 months, the mean number of antihypertensive drugs had increased in both groups to 1.9 (1.2) in the intervention group and 2.0 (1.4) in the control group with no significant differences between groups. In both groups, a large number of patients had changed from monotherapy to treatment with multiple antihypertensive medications at follow-up. At baseline, 64% was on a single drug antihypertensive regimen. At the end of the study, only one third received monotherapy, and one third was treated with three or more antihypertensive medications. In addition, the number of antihypertensive medications was increased in 46/113 (41%) in the intervention group and 52/123 (42%) in the control group. In the intervention group, the number of drugs was unchanged in 65/113 (58%) and 70/123 (57%) in the control group, p50.793.
