## Abstract

Aim: The aim of this study was to assess the impact on health-related quality of life (HRQoL) of a telehealth care solution compared with usual practice of patients with heart failure (HF).

Methods: A randomized controlled trial with a telehealth care solution (Telekit) as the intervention (with a focus on self-empowerment achieved by engaging patients in their own illness through self-monitoring) combined with usual care and usual care as the control. The primary outcome was a change in HRQoL as measured by the Short Form-36 (SF-36) questionnaire Physical Component Summary (PCS) score. Secondary outcomes were changes in HRQoL as measured by the SF-36 questionnaire Mental Component Summary (MSC) score and the HF disease-specific questionnaire Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire 12 (KCCQ12) score, all of which were assessed from baseline to approximately 12 months’ follow-up between the two groups. Outcomes were assessed via unadjusted and adjusted analyses.

Results: At baseline, 299 (145 interventions, 154 controls) patients were enrolled. In the primary analysis (n ¼ 299), the adjusted intervention effects were PCS –0.81 (95% CI 2.7–1.1), MCS 4.66 (95% CI 1.8–7.5) and KCCQ12 3.67 (95% CI -0.7–8.1). Only the change in MCS was statistically significant. An unadjusted analysis replicated the primary analysis. Complete case analyses (n ¼ 193) generally resulted in a lower intervention effect on the PCS score, but the difference remained statistically insignificant.

Conclusions: Only the MCS score was significantly higher in the telehealth care group compared to the control group. ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02860013), July 28, 2016

# Methods
## Study design

This study was conducted in accordance with the study protocol for the TeleCare North heart failure trial,30 which is described briefly below.

The TeleCare North heart failure trial was a pragmatic, randomized trial with an approximately 12-months’ follow-up. The trial was based on the collaborative efforts of the North Denmark Region, all 11 municipalities in the region and Aalborg University.

## Participants

The trial targeted all patients with HF in the North Denmark Region who fulfilled a set of inclusion criteria: participants were qualified for inclusion if they had been diagnosed with HF according to national guidelines and were placed within the New York Heart Association (NYHA) class 2, 3, or 4.31 Patients should also have a permanent residence in Denmark and be motivated to use telehealth care. Motivation was assessed by the health care personnel by asking the patient prior to inclusion. Patients were excluded if they lacked a landline phone, a mobile phone or GSM (Global System for Mobile communications) coverage. Patients not able to understand Danish adequately enough to complete the questionnaires and patients without the energy to participate were also excluded.

Recruitment took place in three outpatient health care centres in the North Denmark Region. The recruitment included both newly diagnosed patients and people with an existing diagnosis of HF. All participants provided written informed consent before participation. The randomization used prepacked envelopes to assign patients to the control or intervention group.

Overall, the trial included 299 patients with 145 randomized to telehealth care and 154 to usual practice.

The study protocol was assessed by The North Denmark Region Committee on Health Research Ethics (http://www.rn.dk/sundhed/til-sundhedsfagligeog-samarbejdspartnere/forskning/den-videnskabsetiskekomite-for-region-nordjylland), and the project was assessed as not requiring ethical approval. The study was conducted in accordance with The Helsinki Declaration and guidelines for Good Clinical Practice.32

## Intervention

The intervention was based on the same concept as the TeleCare North COPD trial.29,33 The core concept was patient empowerment, achieved by engaging patients in

their own illness and increasing their coping capabilities through self-monitoring. The study featured extended monitoring of various physical and selfreported indicators. The self-monitoring helped health care providers detect disease worsening and, based on reported physical measurements, rapidly initiate actions to prevent more disease exacerbations. Patients in the intervention group received telehealth care in addition to usual practice. The telehealth care solution, the so-called Telekits, consisted of a standard tablet (Samsung Galaxy Tab2) that facilitated data collection from a disease-specific questionnaire and Bluetooth linkage with two external devices. The external devices included a digital blood pressure monitor (Model UA-767, plus BT-C, Nonin Medical, MN, USA) and a scale (Precision Health Scale, UC-321PBT-C, A & D Medical, Tokyo, Japan) that collected disease-specific data (blood pressure, pulse and weight) and wirelessly transmitted these data to a central clinical system. In addition, the patients’ responses to a disease-specific questionnaire, which was developed by clinical domain experts in the North Denmark Region, were set up in an application on the tablet and transmitted to the clinical system. The tablet actively reminded patients when it was time to take measurements and answer the questionnaire. Patients were asked to use the scale and blood pressure monitor daily during the first 2 weeks and one to two times weekly after the first 2 weeks.

Specially trained nurses assessed all the data in the clinical system. Data assessment comprised the following elements: that the data were seen, interpreted and evaluated to determine whether there was a need for specific action. The data assessments were conducted at fixed intervals (typically 1-2 times per week). After each assessment, an acknowledgement of the data having been seen was sent to each patient. Patients were contacted if data were not received as planned. If the clinician observed abnormal measurements, several options were available:

The intervention needed was always a clinical judgment based on the patient’s measurement and possibly

a subsequent contact with the patient. The patients, their relatives and general practitioners could all access the collected data via the public website www. sundhed.dk at any time.

After randomization, patients with HF in the intervention group were contacted by a contact at their residing municipality. The Telekit, including external devices, was ordered through Atea (Atea Danmark, Aalborg, Denmark), which delivered and installed the Telekit at the citizen’s home. A nurse from the HF patient’s municipality instructed the patient in the use of the Telekit.

## Control group: usual care

Patients assigned to the control group received usual practice only. This involved usual treatment, monitoring, and care throughout the study period. The patients’ general practitioners or the outpatient health care centres provided this treatment and monitoring. As part of usual care, HF patients in the North Denmark Region were offered rehabilitation involving dietary advice, training, closely monitored up-titration of medicine if necessary (ACE Inhibitors, beta-blocker, Spiron and more), screening for risk factors and conversation about lifestyle changes in relation to the disease. The rehabilitation took 3–6 months, based on the patient’s needs. The intervention groups received usual care as well.

# Outcome measures
The primary outcome was change in HRQoL measured by the SF-36 questionnaire PCS score. Secondary outcomes comprised change in HRQoL from the SF-36 questionnaire MSC score and KCCQ12 score, all from baseline to 12-months’ follow-up between the two groups. Upon inclusion and at the 12-month follow-up, all patients were administered the Short Form 36-Item Health Survey, Version 2 (SF-36v2),34 the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ)35 and questions concerning their baseline demographic characteristics. The SF-36v2 is a generic and validated questionnaire for measuring HRQoL, consisting of 36 questions. It measures perceptions of physical, social, mental and emotional health and enables the calculation of overall scores, including a physical component summary (PCS) and mental component summary (MCS).36 All scores are standardized to a 0 and 100 interval, where higher scores reflect a better health status.34

The KCCQ12 is a 12-item disease-specific questionnaire that quantifies physical function, symptoms, social function, self-efficacy and knowledge, and quality of life.35 An overall summary score can be derived

from the physical function, symptom, social function and quality of life scores. For each score, the validity, reproducibility, responsiveness and interpretability have been independently established.35 Scores are standardized to a range of 0–100, in which higher scores reflect better health status. The purpose of including the KCCQ12 was to investigate if a disease-specific questionnaire was better able to capture impact changes than the generic SF-36 questionnaire.

# Statistical methods
Statistical analyses followed the intention-to-treat principle. To evaluate effectiveness, differences between the intervention group and the control group with regard to SF-36 PCS and MCS scores and KCCQ scores from baseline to follow-up were analysed. Outcomes were all assessed in both an unadjusted and adjusted analysis. A general linear model37 was used to assess PCS, MCS and KCCQ12 score differences from baseline to 12 months’ follow-up in both the unadjusted and the adjusted analysis. The difference in scores was used as a dependent variable, and a dummy variable indicated treatment assignment (1 telehealth care and 0 usual care). In the adjusted analysis, the treatment effect was adjusted for the respective baseline HRQoL score (e.g. baseline MCS in the adjusted analysis of the effect on the MCS score), age, gender, weight, NYHA score,

marital status, comorbidity, job status and selfperceived social status.

Current good practice for clinical trials recommends that analyses of outcomes should account for missing data by imputation.38 For this study, the procedures followed Faria and colleagues.39 Missing baseline data were median-imputed. Missing data at follow-up were assumed missing at random (MAR) and were imputed using the ‘-impute missing value-’ command in SPSS (version 24). Five complete datasets were created. Linear regression was used for continuous variables and logistic regression for categorical variables. Imputation models included all baseline variables. The primary analysis was based on the imputed data, but complete case analyses following the same principles as for the primary were also included to check the sensitivity of the main trial findings.

# Results
## Descriptive characteristics

A CONSORT flow diagram of the patient recruitment is presented in Figure 1. Initially, 299 (145 interventions, 154 controls) patients were enrolled in the TeleCare North trial. At 12 months, 52 (35.8%) intervention patients were lost to follow-up (7 had died, 24 did not respond to questionnaires and 21 had withdrawn their consent). Similarly, 54 (35.1%) control

patients were lost to follow-up (8 dead, 43 did not respond to questionnaires and 2 had withdrawn their consent). One patient was incorrectly never randomized into one of the two trial groups. Reasons for withdrawal from the TeleCare North trial included complicated technology, frustrations with using the technology, psychological problems, health problems, lack of interest and misdiagnosis of HF.

In total, 106 patients had incomplete data (patients that were lost-to-follow-up or had missing values on questionnaire items used to calculate PCS, MCS or KCCQ12 at follow-up). Complete data (patients with non-missing values on MCS, PCS and KCCQ12 scores at follow-up) were available for 193 (93 interventions,

100 controls) of the 299 patients at the 12-month follow-up, giving an attrition rate of 35%.

In Table 1, baseline socio-demographic (gender, age, marital status, education) and health characteristics (smoking status, duration of HF, NYHA score, comorbidities, SF-36 and KCCQ12) are compared for patients included in both the primary analysis and complete case analysis. The comparisons reveal that baseline characteristics were similar across the telehealth care and usual practice groups for both patients included in the primary analysis and complete case analysis, except for a higher proportion of married patients in the telehealth care group compared to the control group (75 v. 62% married or in a relationship).

Few missing values were observed in the baseline data across all variables (1.3% of data values were missing).

## Descriptive analysis of the change in HRQoL from baseline to follow-up

Table 2 presents descriptive statistics of PCS, MCS and KCCQ12 scores at follow-up and changes in scores from baseline. The table includes description of the cohorts in the two analyses, that is the complete case analysis (n 193) and primary analysis (n 299) in each treatment arm. Positive scores between baseline and follow-up represented an improvement, while negative scores represented a decrement in the score. In general, there was a larger improvement in the MCS and KCCQ12 scores for patients in the telehealth care group than for the usual care group. The improvements in PCS score were in general small, but they were higher for the usual care group than the telehealth group. A small mean decrement was seen in the PCS score in the complete case analysis for the telehealth

care group, while the usual care group exhibited decrements in the MCS score in both the primary and complete case analyses as well as in the complete case analysis of the KCCQ12.

## Differences between the telehealth care and usual care group

Table 3 presents unadjusted and adjusted differences between treatment groups for both PCS, MCS and KCCQ12 scores from baseline to 12 months’ followup. The unadjusted difference revealed statistically significant MCS score differences between groups in both the primary analysis and the complete case analysis (p < 0.01). However, the unadjusted difference in the PCS score and the KCCQ 12 score was not statically significant between the telehealth care group and usual practice group.

In the primary analysis (n 299), the adjusted intervention effects were PCS -0.81 (95% CI 2.7–1.1), MCS 4.66 (95% CI 1.8–7.5) and KCCQ12 3.67

(95% CI -0.7–8.1). The between-group difference in the change in MCS was statistically significant (p < 0.01), while the differences in the changes in PCS and KCCQ12 scores were statistically insignificant. For the complete case analysis (n  193), the adjusted mean differences between groups in the change in summary scores from baseline to 12 months’ follow-up were PCS 1.46 (95% CI 3.6–0.68), MCS 3.91 (95% CI 0.9–6.9) and KCCQ12 3.74 (95% CI 0.9–8.8). Only the change in MCS score between groups was statistically significant (p <0.05).
