## Aims

Remote patient management (RPM) in heart failure (HF) patients has beneficial clinical effects. This analysis investigates the effects of RPM used in the Telemedical Interventional Management in Heart Failure II (TIM-HF2) trial on HF-specific self-care.

# Methods and results
From 2013 to 2017, 1538 HF outpatients (age 70.3 ± 10.5 years, 70% men, 52%/47% NYHA II/III, 65% LVEF ≤45%) with a recent HF hospitalization were included to the study and randomized to usual care (UC) plus RPM (n = 796) or UC only (n = 775), with a 12-month follow-up. Self-reported self-care behaviour at baseline and at end of study was assessed with the 9-item European Heart Failure Self-care Behaviour Scale (EHFScBS-9), obtaining 1321 patients with valid baseline and follow-up questionnaires for the analysis. EHFScBS-9 sum scores increased in the RPM group (n = 667) from 78.7 ± 17 to 84.5 ± 14 and in the UC group (n = 654) from 79.0 ± 17 to 80.0 ± 16 from baseline to 12 months [difference in means (MD) 4.58 (3.02, 6.14); P < 0.001] with highest improvement [8.66 (3.52; 13.81)] in patients living alone and having an inadequate (<70) baseline EHFScBS-9. There were differences between both groups in item ‘I weight myself every day’ [MD −1.13 (−1.24, −1.02); P < 0.001] and item ‘I take my medication as prescribed’ [MD −0.06 (−0.10, −0.01); P = 0.014]. No correlation was found between the EHFScBS-9 score and the efficacy of RPM on the TIM-HF2 primary endpoint of percentage of days lost due to unplanned cardiovascular hospitalization or death of any cause.

## Conclusions

RPM improves HF-specific self-care behaviour by achieving a better adherence to recommended HF regimen.

# Methods
The TIM-HF2 study was a prospective, randomized, controlled, parallel group, unmasked, and multicentre trial conducted in Germany.

Eligible patients were required to have HF in NYHA class II or III, a prior HF hospitalization within 12 months, diuretic therapy with LVEF >45%, or guideline directed medical therapy with LVEF ≤45%. The main exclusion criteria were depression with PHQ-9 score above 9 points and end-stage renal disease. Follow-up time was 12 months. Cardiologists performed screening of study subjects, baseline, and final study exams. Study visits at 3, 6, and 9 months could also be done by general practitioners. Patients were randomly assigned (1:1) to UC plus RPM or UC alone. Clinical variables were balanced using Pocock’s minimization algorithm. The sample size was calculated based on the results of the TIM-HF2 study (ClinicalTrials.gov number NCT00543881).

The TIM-HF2 primary outcome was the percentage of days lost due to unplanned cardiovascular hospital admissions or death from any cause. Secondary outcomes were all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, percentage of days lost due to unplanned cardiovascular hospital admissions, and percentage of days lost due to unplanned HF hospital admissions. A detailed description of endpoint evaluation and analysis has been published previously.13,15

The TIM-HF2 study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of the Charité (EA2/065/13). All patients provided written informed consent. The investigation conforms with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

## Telemedical interventional management

Daily submission of patient data to the telemedicine centre (TMC) included body weight, blood pressure, heart rate, electrocardiogram, and a self-rated general health status on a tablet. Data from the telemedical devices were transmitted automatically to the TMC, which was located at a university hospital (Charité-Universitätsmedizin Berlin), staffed with doctors and HF nurses, operating 24/7. HF nurses had a multi-year experience in clinical cardiology obtained by working in the wards, intensive care unit, emergency rooms, and outpatient clinics. They got additional qualification attending a multi-week HF training track. Physicians were advanced trainees or specialists in internal medicine or cardiology affiliated to the university department of cardiology.

Combined with biomarker measurements from study visits and clinical information, these data were used to assess patients’ risk of deterioration

and optimize RPM. Furthermore, the TMC provided patient education and communication with patients’ doctors.

Initially, patients of the RPM group received a HF nurse home visit after randomization with a telemedical device instruction and a HF education session. Patients were asked about their profession and educational level. Mental capacities, local conditions, and social circumstances were assessed. According to the patients’ need, this session was customized by the HF nurse and supported by dedicated training brochures. A chart on HF signs and symptoms was reviewed with the patient and family member. The next day, the HF nurse contacted the patient by phone to evaluate correct telemedical device handling and to answer pending questions or issues, followed by structured telephone interviews monthly. During the telephone calls, the HF nurse asked the patient about signs or symptoms and reviewed the patients self-rated health status. When deemed necessary, the nurse provided support by informing, advising, and encouraging the patient. There was a focus on the transmitted vital sign data, reported behaviour, fluid intake, and medication.

## Usual care and follow-up

Patients in the UC group received care as recommended by current guidelines for the management and treatment of patients with HF.1 Follow-up visits were performed at 3, 6, and 9 months by GP or cardiologist and the final study visit at day 365 + 28 days by cardiologist. At all times, patients’ doctors could initiate or change medication as they thought appropriate.

## Questionnaire

Self-care behaviour was measured at baseline visit and final study visit using the German version of the European Heart Failure Self-care Behaviour Scale (G9-EHFScB-9) (Supplemental material online, Appendix). The EHFScBS-9 consists of nine items rated on a five-point Likert scale between 1 (I completely agree) and 5 (I completely disagree). Higher item scores mean worse self-care. For the sum EHFScBS-9 questionnaire, every item is given an equal weight.

For calculation, the sum of the reversed items was normalized to a score of 0–100. A higher score means better self-care behaviour.16 A score of ≥70 was defined as adequate and <70 as inadequate self-care .17 The twofactor structure of the EHFScBS-9 ‘adherence to regimen’ (items 1, 5, 7, 8, 9) and ‘consulting behaviour’ (items 2, 3, 4, 6) was validated recently.18 The validity and reliability of the G9-EHFScBS have been tested.7

According to the instructions of the EHFScBS-9 development team, missing items were imputed by ‘3’ for up to three missing items—missing of more than three items results in an invalid sum score. The subscales consulting behaviour (consisting of items 2, 3, 4, and 6) and adherence to regimen (consisting of items 1, 5, 7, 8, and 9) are calculated analogously to the sum score (sum of reversed items scaled to 0–100). Valid imputations for the sum score were calculated analogously to the sum score (sum of reversed items scaled to 0–100, using imputed values).

## Statistical analysis

To assess the reliability of the questionnaire, Cronbach’s alpha was calculated for baseline and end of study visit with original items without any imputation. Differences in change from baseline to 12 months of the G9-EHFScBS sum score, the corresponding subscales, or single item were compared between the treatment groups by ANCOVA models adjusting for the respective baseline value. Interaction tests for the pre-specified sub-group analyses were done by adding the interaction term in the corresponding models.

We conducted three sensitivity analyses by applying the ANCOVA model to different datasets.

To investigate the effect of RPM in patients with inadequate (<70) and adequate (≥70) G9-EHFScBS sum score, in all following analyses, interaction tests for this split-up were implemented, and separate analyses are conducted for both strata. The primary endpoint and one secondary endpoint were analysed using permutation tests. Confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using the method described by Garthwaite which is based on the Robbins–Monro method.19 All-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality were examined on a time-to-first event basis. A competing risk analysis was applied for cardiovascular mortality. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate (cause-specific) hazard ratios (HRs).

For these models, a sensitivity analysis was calculated to analyse the full set of patients. The results for the multiply imputed datasets were pooled by Rubin’s rule or medians for the permutation tests.

All statistical analyses were two-sided, and P-values <0.05 were considered to be statistically significant. Interaction P-values were judged for relevance with a threshold of 0.15.

We used R (version 3.4.4) and Stata (version 14.2 and 16.0) for all analyses.

# Results
## Patients’ characteristics

A total of 1538 patients were included in the TIM-HF2 full analysis set (Table 1). The mean age was 70.3 ± 10.5 years, 70% were men, 435 (28%) were living alone, 52% were in NYHA class II and 47% in NYHA class III, and 65% had a LVEF ≤45%.

In the RPM group, 18% of the patients had completed a university degree, 77% had completed vocational training, and 6% of them had no vocational training at all, as revealed during the initial nurse home visit.

## Self-care behaviour at baseline and efficacy of RPM

Baseline data on self-care behaviour were available in 1522 patients (Figure 1 and Supplementary material online, Table S1). Ten patients

with missing questionnaire and six with more than three items missing were omitted from the analysis. Missing items were replaced by the number 3, according to the instructions of the EHFScBS-9 development team, in 104 patients with 1 item missing, 11 with 2 items, and 5 with 3 items missing.5

At baseline, G9-EHFScBS sum scores were comparable for both groups with means of 78.7 (±17.0) in the RPM group ( n = 7 5 3 )and \bar { 7 } 9 . 2 \ ( \pm 1 7 . 0 )in the UC group ( n = 7 6 9 ). In addition, 28.8% of the patients had a sum score <70 at baseline.

The efficacy of RPM regarding the primary endpoint and main secondary endpoints of the TIM-HF2 study was comparable between patients with inadequate (<70) and adequate (≥70) baseline self-care behaviour score (Table 2). The findings were consistent with the results of the sensitivity analysis (see Supplementary material online, Table S2).

## Change in self-care behaviour sum score between baseline and final visit

In total, 1333 valid questionnaires at 12 months were obtained, of which 138 were validly imputed (122 times 1 item, 11 times 2 items, and 5 times 3 items). Moreover, 13 questionnaires with more than 3 missing items were omitted, and 192 patients failed to complete the questionnaire at all. Thus, 1333 patients had a valid follow-up questionnaire, though 12 of these did not have a baseline questionnaire.

Consequently, there are 1321 patients with valid baseline and follow-up questionnaires which were included in the following complete case analysis of the changes (Figure 1 and Supplementary material online, Table S1). The reliability estimates for the sum G9-EHFScBS were satisfactory (see Supplementary material online, Table S3B).

G9-EHFScBS sum scores increased in the RPM group (n = 667) from 78.7 (±17.0) to 84.5 (±14) and in the UC group (n = 654) from 79.0 (±16.8) to 80.0 (±16.1) from baseline to 12 months (Figure 2) with a

higher change in favour of the RPM group (mean difference [MD] 4.58 95% CI 3.02, 6.14; P < 0.001) (Table 3).

Comparing the intervention effect on improvement in the sum score from baseline to 12 months between patients with inadequate (<70) and adequate (≥70) baseline score, a larger intervention effect was observed in patients with inadequate score at baseline (MD 3.13 [−0.31; 6.57]; P = 0.075) while in both groups, RPM was superior to UC [<70: RPM vs. UC 6.80 (3.90; 9.71); P < 0.001 and ≥70: RPM vs. UC 3.68 (1.83; 5.52); P < 0.001].

There was no difference in the intervention effect on sum score changes between patients living alone and not living alone [not living alone: 4.89 (3.06; 6.73), living alone: 3.76 (0.82; 6.70), MD 1.13 (−2.33; 4.60); P = 0.523]. But the sub-group of patients living alone and having an inadequate (<70) baseline score showed the largest intervention effect on improvement from baseline to 12 months [8.66 (3.52; 13.81)].

## Effect on the subscales ‘adherence to regimen’ and ‘consulting behaviour’

A significant improvement between RPM and UC group was shown for item 1 ‘I weight myself every day’ (P < 0.001) and item 8 ‘I take my

medication as prescribed’ (P = 0.014) (Table 3). The subscale ‘adherence to regimen’ (consisting of items 1, 5, 7, 8, and 9) showed a significant difference in change (P < 0.001) between the treatment arms, whereas the subscale ‘consulting behaviour’ (consisting of items 2, 3, 4, and 6) showed only marginal differences (P = 0.083) (Table 3). These findings were confirmed by sensitivity analyses (see Supplementary material online, Table S3A and B).

## Analysis of predefined sub-groups

The intervention effect was consistent over all pre-specified sub-groups with the exception of patients with a cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) and implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) (Figure 3).

Similar results were obtained for the sensitivity analysis of the adjusted imputed data set (see Supplementary material online, Tables S4 and S5).
