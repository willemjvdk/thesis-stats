# Influence of a symptom management telehealth intervention on older adults’ early recovery outcomes after coronary artery bypass surgery
Susan Barnason, PhD, APRN-CNS, CEN, CCRN, FAHA, Lani Zimmerman, PhD, RN, FAHA, Janet Nieveen, PhD, RN, Paula Schulz, PhD, RN, Connie Miller, PhD, RN, Melody Hertzog, PhD, and Chunhao Tu, PhD(c)

OBJECTIVE: The study objective was to examine the effect of a symptom management (SM) telehealth intervention on physical activity and functioning and to describe the health care use of older adult patients (aged  65 years) after coronary artery bypass surgery (CABS) by group (SM intervention group and usual care group).

METHODS: A randomized clinical trial design was used. The study was conducted in 4 Midwestern tertiary hospitals. The 6-week SM telehealth intervention was delivered by the Health Buddy (Health Hero Network, Palo Alto, CA). Measures included Modified 7-Day Activity Interview, RT3 accelerometer (Stayhealthy, Inc, Monrovia, CA), physical activity and exercise diary, Medical Outcomes Study Short-Form 36, and subjects’ self-report and provider records of health care use. Follow-up times were 3 and 6 weeks and 3 and 6 months after CABS.

RESULTS: Subjects (N - 232) had a mean age of 71.2 (4.7) years. There were no significant interactions using repeated-measures analyses of covariance. There was a significant group effect for average kilocalories/kilogram/day of estimated energy expenditure as measured by the RT3 accelerometer, with the usual care group having a higher estimated energy expenditure. Both groups had significant improvements over time for role-physical, vitality, and mental functioning. Both groups had similar health care use.

CONCLUSION: Subjects were able to return to preoperative levels of functioning between 3 and 6 months after CABS and to increase their physical activity over reported preoperative levels of activity. Further study of those patients undergoing CABS who could derive the most benefit from the SM intervention is warranted. (Heart Lung® 2009;38:364 –376.)

# MATERIALS AND METHODS
This study used a randomized, 2-group (N - 280), repeated-measures design with measurements taken at the time of discharge (baseline measures) and 3 and 6 weeks and 3 and 6 months postoperatively among older adult patients (aged  65 years). One group received UC and the SM intervention, and the comparison group received UC only. The study sample size of 280 was based on a power analysis of pilot data on the primary variables of physiologic functioning. Pilot data using the physical, role-physical, and vitality functioning subscales of the Medical Outcomes Study Short Form-36 (MOS SF-36) were measured at 3 follow-up times in subjects after CABS. On the basis of a correlation among the observations from the same subject that was no greater than .55, a sample size of 123 subjects per group was recommended because it would provide at least 80% power (testing at the 5% level of statistical significance, 2-sided) to detect a difference in the true means of the outcome variables of .3 standard deviation (a difference characterized as a small-to-medium effect for social science research). The target accrual for this study of 280 subjects was based a 15% attrition to allow for an ineligibility rate and loss to follow-up during the study.

The investigators44 developed the SM telehealth intervention implemented in this study to improve patients’ self-efficacy and skills related to self-care management of early recovery symptoms. The 6-week SM intervention was delivered via the Health Buddy (Health Hero Network, Palo Alto, CA) telehealth device for a total of 42 daily sessions. The SM intervention provided subjects with strategies (eg, adequate rest, pain management, progressing physical activity incrementally) designed to address commonly occurring symptoms experienced after recovery from CABS, as a means to improve outcomes (physical activity, functioning) and a longterm outcome of having less health care use.

Age-eligible participants post-CABS were recruited from December 2002 to August 2006 from 4 Midwestern tertiary hospitals. Initially, 280 patients consented to participate in the study. Data analyses were conducted on 232 subjects, because 34 subjects (23.8%) did not receive the allocated intervention in the intervention group, compared with 15

subjects (11%) who did not receive the allocated intervention in the UC group. Reasons for not receiving the treatment as allocated, in both groups, included the following: subject burden (n - 36), rehospitalizations or transfer to extended care facility (n - 4), equipment malfunction (n - 3), inability to reach subject per telephone for follow-up data collection (n - 2), and inability to complete the intervention protocol (n - 3). Figure 1 depicts the study enrollment and attrition for a total of 123 subjects in the UC group and 109 subjects in the SM group.

## Measures

In addition to the demographic and clinical data collected as reported in Table I,other data measures used included the Modified 7-day Activity Interview, RT3 accelerometer (Stayhealthy, Inc, Monrovia, CA), Physical Activity and Exercise diary, and MOS SF-36.

Physical activity. Physical activity data were collected using the Modified 7-Day Activity Interview (for baseline), the RT3 accelerometer, and the Physical Activity and Exercise Diary (for follow-up times).

The Modified 7-Day Activity Interview45 was used to assess subjects’ baseline physical activity level before surgery (average kilocalories/kilogram/day expended and average minutes/day spent in moderate or greater activity). Data collected from the tool include hours per day spent in sleep and in light, moderate, hard, and very hard activity (based on metabolic equivalent test levels) and yield the total kilocalories per kilogram of body weight expended per day and total calories expended per day. The instrument has acceptable test–retest reliability.46 Construct validity and criterion validity have been reported.47

The RT3 accelerometer was used as an objective measure of physical activity at follow-up times of 3 and 6 weeks and 3 and 6 months after CABS. The accelerometer is approximately the size and weight of a pager. Subjects wore the accelerometer on their waistbands continuously, except during bathing and sleeping times, for 3 consecutive days each data-collection period. Hertzog et al48 demonstrated additional support for using 3 days of data collection, as used in this study. Reliability estimates (interclass correlations) for 3 days of self-reported recorded data collection of minutes of moderate or higher physical activity in a diary and RT3 ranged from .76 to .84 at 3 weeks, 6 weeks, and 3 months after CABS. The RT3 yields “activity counts” based on the subject’s amount of activity, which can then be converted to kilocalo-

ries expended. In this study, average daily activity counts and average kilocalories/kilogram/day expended were evaluated. The RT3 is a triaxial accelerometer that measures body motion (specifically, the electrical energy of acceleration and deceleration) during activities that involve energy cost. Triaxial accelerometers have documented validity with indirect calorimetry, with correlations ranging from .48 to .92.49-51 Reliability of this instrument has also been reported.52-54

The Physical Activity and Exercise Diary was a daily log used by subjects for each of the same 3 days that each subject wore the RT3 accelerometer at each follow-up time (3 and 6 weeks and 3 and 6

months after CABS). Subjects recorded the amount of time they spent in the various categories of activity levels (light, moderate, hard, very hard activities) and sleep. These categories were adapted from the Modified 7-Day Activity Interview.45 Diary data included average kilocalories/kilogram/day expended and mean number of minutes spent in light, moderate, hard, and very hard levels of physical activity. Three-day activity diaries, such as the one proposed for this study, have been used successfully as estimates of physical activity.55,56 The correlations between estimates of 3-day diary reports and the RT3 accelerometer demonstrated high generalizability intraclass correlation coefficents (ICCs).

Physiologic and psychosocial functioning. The MOS SF-36 version 2 . 0 ^ { 5 7 }consists of 8 subscales measuring both physiologic and psychosocial functioning (general health, physical, role-physical, role emotional, social, bodily pain, mental health, and vitality functioning). Measurements of the MOS SF-36 were taken at baseline during hospitalization (based

on the subject’s report of pre-procedure level of functioning) and at 6 weeks and 3 and 6 months after CABS. In this study, measures were not taken at 3 weeks because patients are still recuperating from their acute hospitalization after CABS, and there was a need to minimize subject burden in the study. Subscale scores have a range of 0 to 100, with

higher scores indicating better or higher functioning. Reported reliability, using Cronbach’s alpha, ranges from .78 to .93.58-60 The instrument also has reported discriminant validity.59 Physiologic functioning was measured using 3 of the MOS SF-36 subscales: physical functioning (10 items), role limitations caused by physical problems (4 items), and vitality (4 items) or the perceptions of energy and fatigue. Psychosocial functioning was measured by 3 of the MOS SF-36 subscales: mental health (5 items), social (2 items), and role limitations caused by emotional problems (3 items). In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha for the physiologic subscales (physical, role physical, and vitality) and psychosocial subscales (role emotional, mental, and social) for all data-collection times ranged from .89 to .90.

Health care use. At each follow-up time period, subjects were queried about whether they had accessed any health care providers/services, specifically if they had been rehospitalized, to the emergency department, or to their health care provider (eg, primary care provider, cardiologist, and surgeon). If the subject had accessed any health care services, the frequency and purpose of the use were recorded. For those patients who reported use of health care, the hospital or health care provider was contacted by mail to validate the date of the hospitalization or health care visit and to determine the reason for the health care encounter. This information cross-validated the subjects’ self-reports.

## Findings

The sample was composed of 232 subjects (83% were men and 17% were women), and 86% of the sample were married. There were 109 subjects in the SM group and 123 subjects in the UC group. Subjects had a mean age of 71.2 (4.7) years. The majority of subjects had an average of 13 or more years of formal education, and approximately 80% of subjects in both groups participated in CR after hospital discharge. There were no statistically or clinically significant group differences on demographic (eg, age, length of stay, race, and marital status) or baseline clinical variables (eg, comorbidities, body mass index, discharge hemoglobin, and CR participation). ## Physiologic and psychosocial functioning

Physiologic and psychosocial functioning were measured using subscales of the MOS SF-36. Although there were no significant (P  .05) interactions or main effects for group, there were some significant time effects for both physiologic and psychosocial functioning using RM-ANCOVA with baseline measures as the covariate; refer to Table IV for physiologic measures of the MOS SF 36 and Table V for the psychosocial measures of the MOS SF 36. ## Health care use

The groups in this study had similar rates of rehospitalizations, emergency department visits, and clinic visits for cardiac-related problems. In the SM group, 20 of 109 subjects (18%) compared with 18 of 123 subjects (15%) in the UC group were rehospitalized over the 6 months after CABS. Three subjects (3%) in the SM group and 4 subjects (3%) in the UC group had 2 to 3 rehospitalizations during the 6 months after CABS. Similarly, 20 patients (18%) in the SM group, compared with 18 subjects (15%) in the UC group, had emergency department visits during the 6 months after CABS. Sixty-six subjects (60%) in the SM group and 65 subjects (53%) in the UC group had 1 reported clinic visit during the study period. Thirteen subjects (12%) in the SM group and 4 subjects (3%) in the UC group had 2 to 3 clinic visits during the 6 months after CABS. Clinic visits reflected routine types of care (eg, follow-up of diagnostic studies [lipid panels] and routine follow-up with surgeon after CABS).
