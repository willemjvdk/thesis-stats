# Materials and methods
## Study design

The present pilot study is a double-blind, randomized, crossover clinical trial with two arms. Participants were randomly assigned to experimental intervention, a customized cognitive training (CCT-C) or sham intervention (CCT-non-C). In the first phase, participants assigned to Group A started with the CCT-C intervention over a 6-week period, while participants in group B started with the CCT-non-C. Participants were instructed to connect from home with their computer to perform their assigned intervention during the 6 weeks, once a day, from Monday to Friday. After a 3-month washout period, the second phase commenced, in which the groups were crossed over, namely group A performed the CCT-non -C intervention, while group B did the CCT-C intervention for additional 6 weeks. Evaluations were conducted pre- and posttraining by a study examiner blinded to the allocation of participants. Pre-testing occurred immediately before starting the interventions (t1). Post-testing took place immediately following the interventions (t2/t4) and after the 3-month washout period (t3) (Figure 1) A follow-up neuropsychological assessment was performed 6 months after treatment had terminated (t5). A more detailed description of the protocol study was exposed in a previous work (26).

## Randomization

A researcher blinded to the assessment procedure performed a stratified restricted random assignment of participants using the rand () function of Microsoft Excel software (Microsoft Excel 2010 for Windows). Participants were stratified based on sex (male or female) and type of stroke (ischemic or hemorrhagic). The same blinded researcher wrote up a series of instructions about the assigned interventions (GNPT® or ictus.online) and mailed them in a sealed envelope to each participant at the time of the first and the third neuropsychological examinations (t1 and t3) (Figure 1).

As adherence strategies, we offered flexible schedules to perform the exercises to all patients enrolled in the study, as well as phone calls to confirm assessment dates and periodic progress reports to keep participants informed on their personal progress in the study.

## Participants

Forty former patients from Institut Guttmann Neurorehabilitation were recruited. Of these forty, thirty completed all neuropsychological assessments required to be included in data analysis (Figure 2). As former patients, they all had received CCT-C during admission at the post-acute stage of stroke and were evaluated before rehabilitation discharge (t0) (Figure 1).

Inclusion criteria for the trial are as follows: (i) a first-ever stroke diagnosis (ischemic stroke or hemorrhagic stroke), (ii) age over 18 years at stroke onset, (iii) a time of evolution of 12 months or more since stroke occurred (chronic stage), (iv) cognitive impairment confirmed by pre-intervention neuropsychological assessment, and (v) willingness to give written informed consent to participate in the study. Exclusion criteria are as follows: (i) diagnosis of language deficits or aphasia, (ii) motor impairment concerning upper limbs that may incapacitate them for computer use, (iii) severe alteration of the visual field or perceptual problems, (iv) health status that may require further intervention or admission to a medical center during the study, (v) neurological or psychiatric history, or (vi) substance abuse.

We conducted a neuropsychological screen evaluation to check exclusion criteria (i) and (iii) which consisted of an orientation and language test (orientation, word-repetition, visual-naming, and order comprehension subtests of Test Barcelona-R) (24). The cutoff needed to participate in the study was 100% proficiency on these tests.

A next-of-kin (of legal age) was recruited for each participant (Group A, n = 9; Group B, n = 11) to participate in all assessments to rate their perception of the participant’s performance in daily life activities. All recruited patients understood the objective, and implications of the study and were able to give informed consent.

## Ethical considerations and informed consent

Ethics approval has been received from the Care Ethics Committee of Fundació Institut Guttmann, and the study has

been conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (27). The protocol follows the recommendations of the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials Statement (SPIRIT) 2013 (28).

## Interventions

## Experimental intervention

The customized intervention (CCT- C) consisted of the application of Guttmann, NeuroPersonalTrainer® (GNPT®), a homebased supervised and customized tele-rehabilitation platform developed by Institut Guttmann. This CCT-C allows therapists to configure and schedule rehabilitation sessions, consisting of a set of personalized exercises, using an automated process that adjusts the level of difficulty according to patient’s assessment and performance throughout the sessions (25–29). The neuropsychological assessments done at t1 (for group A) and t3 (for group B) were used to establish the cognitive profile of each participant. Once a rehabilitation session is defined, the participant executes the assigned tasks, sending the results back to the server located at Institut Guttmann, such that therapists can asynchronously see the performance. A researcher from the study group was exclusively dedicated to supervising this process.

The treatment consists of a set of 1-h sessions, five sessions per week for 6 weeks. A series of cognitive exercises of attention, memory, and executive functions were conducted in each session. Exercises of attention included tasks of sustained attention and vigilance (e.g. “Bingo” task), selective attention (e.g. to find a pair or pictures among a set of distractors), and divided attention (e.g. to solve a mathematical operation while paying attention to the color of the corresponding box). Memory tasks trained verbal memory (e.g. to memorize series of numbers) in addition to working memory (e.g. to memorize the order of appearance of a series of stimuli). Finally, executive functions were trained with inhibitory control tasks (e.g., go/ no-go tasks) and cognitive flexibility (e.g. “Circles” task, where

the patient has to select different stimuli based on the background color of the screen, which changes every few seconds).

## Sham intervention

As sham intervention, we developed a home-based supervised platform (CCT-non-C) that was not customized and followed a similar design as the telerehabilitation platform GNPT®. After logging in, participants accessed a daily session, which presented four videos of 10 minutes each. After each video, the participant had to complete a three-question quiz about the contents of the video. This made each session last approximately 1 hour in total. The difficulty level of the questions was minimal as well as independent of the results of the neuropsychological assessment and remained stable regardless of the execution of the participants.

## Measures

## Neuropsychological performance-based measures

We used performance-based measures to assess attention, memory, and executive functions. For attention measures, we used Conner’s Continuous Performance—CPT-II (30) to explore sustained attention and Trail Making Tests A and B (31) for evaluating selective and divided attention. To assess verbal memory, we used Digit Span Forward from the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS-III) (32) to assess immediate verbal memory and Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test—RAVLT (33) to explore short- and long-term verbal memory. To rate executive functions, we used Digit Span Backwards and Letter-Number Sequencing from WAIS-III to study working memory, Stroop Color, and Word test (34) for the evaluation of inhibitory control and a Spanish phonemic fluency test (35). Finally, we included speed of processing measures as the Hit response index from CPT-II (30) and Digit Symbol-Coding test from WAIS-III.

## Rating measures

Rating measures were applied to the patient (self-report) and their recruited next-of-kin (informant-report) to rate subjective cognitive functioning in daily life. We used the Patient Competency Rating Scale (PCRS) (36), which assesses selfawareness and where higher scores indicate better functioning. We also applied the Rating Scale for Attentional Behavior (RSAB) (37), a rating scale designed to approach attentional difficulties in daily life; Prospective and Retrospective Memory Questionnaire (PRMQ) (38), developed to study memory complaints; and Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function – Adult Version (BRIEF-A) (39), a questionnaire that rates selfregulation and executive function behavior. In these three questionnaires, low scores indicate better functioning.

## Statistics

Comparisons between groups to study differences in evolution and within groups were performed. Non-parametric statistics were applied as the Shapiro-Wilk test showed that our data were not normally distributed. We used the U-Mann Whitney test to study differences between groups (independent samples). For within-group analyses, we used the Signed-rank Wilcoxon test for paired samples. For these analyses, we also used Spearman correlation to study the relationship between next-of-kin reports and patient’s reports on functional questionnaires for controlling deficit awareness.

All statistical analyses were performed with “R” (40). The level of significance was set at \begin{array} { r } { p < 0 . 0 5 } \end{array}. We used Cohen’s d to calculate the effect size when appropriate.

# Results
## Sample characteristics

Demographic variables were analyzed, and no significant differences were found between group A and group B regarding gender, education, age at stroke, time since stroke, and type of stroke (see Table 1).

We found no within-group differences between t0 and t1, meaning that cognitive scores remained “stable” throughout the period of time between rehabilitation discharge and recruitment, and therefore, we could assume that there had not been any cognitive change before the study.

Analyses of next-of-kin and patient’s report in rating-scales showed positive and significant correlations between both groups (PCRS r = 0.57; p < 0.0001; RSAB r = 0.52; \mathrm { ~ p ~ } < \ 0 . 0 0 0 1; PRMQ r = 0.24, p = 0.015; BRIEF-A r = 0.37, \mathsf { p } = 0 . 0 0 0 1 ).

## Study 1. Within-group analyses

Within-group analyses revealed no differences between t1 and t2, and neither between t1 and t3 nor between t2 and t3. Within-group significant differences were found in the decrease of scores in PRMQ in Group A between t1 and t4 ( \mathtt { p } = 0 . 0 2 , \mathtt { d } = 0 . 5 ). This difference remained at follow-up, since mean differences between t4 and t5 in PMRQ were not statistically significant ( \mathtt { p } > 0 . 0 5 )(see Figure 3). We also found

significant differences between t1 and t5 in RAVLT Learning in Group B ( \mathtt { p } \ = \ 0 . 0 3 ; \ \mathtt { d } \ = \ 0 . 5 )(see Figure 4). No other significant differences within the group were found.
