## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# HF and Health-Related Outcomes
Patients with HF face a complex mix of biomedical and psychosocial issues, all of which adversely affect all aspects of patients’ health-related QOL.6 Quality of life in HF patients is decreased when compared with people with other chronic health problems and the population in general.7 Quality of life has been increasingly recognized as a salient patient outcome to study, especially in instances of chronic illnesses such as HF, and is one of the most common health outcome measures in interventional studies with HF patients. Furthermore, in a managed care environment, QOL data provide important information on the benefits and cost-effectiveness of new interventions.8

Numerous studies using nurse-directed educational interventions in various configurations with hospitalized patients at discharge have provided evidence of the benefits of integrating educational interventions with conventional care in improving QOL, reducing unplanned hospital admissions, health care costs, and mortality.9Y12

Patients with HF often experience depressive symptoms that affect health-related QOL, morbidity, and mortality.13 Prevalence rates of depression in HF patients have been found to be highly variable across studies, ranging from 13% to 77.5%.14 Patients with HF who are depressed are likely to have more HF symptoms, report a lower QOL,15 report more problems with medication compliance,16 use more health care resources, and have higher mortality rates.17 The increasing prevalence and negative outcomes associated with depression among HF patients led the Heart Failure Society of America18 to incorporate the assessment of depression into their HF guidelines.

## Effective Interventions to Help Persons With HF

## HF and Nurse-Led Structured Education

Nurse-led patient education has been described in the literature as a key component of disease management programs. Rich and colleagues19 conducted one of the earliest studies that examined the use of a nurse-led multidisciplinary intervention for HF patients in 1995. Findings from this randomized controlled trial (RCT) with 228 patients demonstrated significant reductions in 90-day rehospitalization and mortality in intervention group members. Naylor et al20 and McCauley et al21 conducted a comprehensive transitional care intervention from hospital to home for elders with HF. In this multisite RCT, patients were recruited from hospitals and home care agencies. In the intervention group, advance practice nurses substituted for using evidence-based protocols from the American College of Cardiology/ American Heart Association. Intervention patients demonstrated an increased time to first readmission or death through 1-year postYhospital discharge, reduced the total number of rehospitalizations, and decreased medical costs of elders hospitalized with HF. Quality of life was also improved at 2 and 12 weeks, demonstrating the short-term effectiveness of nurse-led interventions in improving HF-related outcomes.

Most of the studies examining structured education and HF patient outcomes have originated in hospital or clinic settings. Few studies have examined the effect of structured education by nurses on outcomes in home care patients with HF. One RCT was found that was initiated in home care. The Health Outcomes, Management, and Evaluation study22 compared the use of nurse-led, evidence-based educational protocols with

usual care in a large metropolitan home care agency. Study findings demonstrated a significant decrease in need for skilled nursing visits and a trend toward decreased hospital admissions, with no significant differences in emergency room use, physician visits, or QOL. Other home care agencies describe HF disease management programs focused on evidence-based education and assessment, but outcome data are not included.23

For people living with HF, levels of health exist along a continuum in correlation with the illness experience, and a focus on health promotion in addition to disease or symptom management expands opportunities for self-care. Clark et al,24 analyzing data from 2 interventional studies with HF patients, revealed that patients who engaged in health promotional activities reported a higher QOL. In this study, the pleasant event list used in previous trials to prevent/reduce depression with community-dwelling elders will be adopted to home care and used as the therapeutic activities component of the HEART intervention.

Research suggests that, when nurse-led education is combined with home TM, HF outcomes are improved. In the Specialized Primary and Networked Care in Heart Failure trial, patients receiving disease management plus TM had 72% less hospitalizations over 90 days.25

## HF and TM

Telemonitoring is an emerging trend in home care and a term used to describe a wide variety of interventions including telephone-based symptom monitoring, automated monitoring of signs and symptoms, and automated physiological monitoring. Automated physiological monitoring, the collection of physiological data often combined with a series of patient questions, will be used to define TM in this study.

In a review of the TM literature, Chaudhry et al26 noted that automated physiological TM holds promise for high-risk patients with HF; however, the data are mixed on whether greater frequency of monitoring leads to better outcomes. The authors identify a critical need for further research with high-quality trials to provide data regarding the effectiveness of TM, cost-effectiveness, and long-term sustainability of these.

Home care studies comparing the influence of TM to usual care on QOL are increasing in the literature. Examples of home care studies include the study of Noel et al27 that found that a home TM system consisting of vital sign and weight monitors and patient questionnaires reduced costs and improved QOL for elderly patients with complicated comorbid diagnoses. The home care study of Myers et al28 demonstrated that daily home care TM reduced the frequency of home nursing

visits, provided cost savings, and was associated with improved self-perceived QOL.

## HF and Depression

In home care settings, mild and moderate depression among older persons is disproportionately more common than in primary care settings, but usually home care nurses do not recognize depression and physicians do not treat it.29,30 Recent research describes techniques such as problem-solving therapy31 and cognitive behavioral therapy32 that have been used successfully with depressed older adults in home care settings. Common to both these methods is the use of pleasant physical, emotional, and social events that patients regularly select as a self-care strategy to alleviate depression and enhance wellness. Studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of pleasant physical, emotional, and social events with community-dwelling elders,33 Latino patients,34 and patients with dementia.35 No studies were found that incorporated the use of pleasant physical, emotional, and social activities events with older adults with HF.

In summary, a review of literature reveals gaps in the literature related to HF research in home care settings. Several recent studies to enhance home care for people with HF by augmenting the care provided with the use of TM have found mostly positive, but mixed, results.25 However, no studies were found that compared TM with usual home care and TM with nurse-directed structured education. Moreover, although the incidence of depression is high in this population, no studies were found that targeted depressive symptoms in home care patients with HF as part of the assessment of the effectiveness of a disease management program. Finally, the inclusion of health promotional activities, such as the therapeutic physical and social activities used in this study, expands and enhances current HF care and has the potential to improve health-related outcomes in this population. Thus, the development of an innovative intervention that combines nurse-directed education, comprehensive assessment, TM, and therapeutic activities has an important clinical and research relevance.

## Theoretical Framework

Over the past 7 years, the S-CHPA model5 has been used to devise interventions to facilitate self-care behaviors in patients and family caregivers. An underlying assumption of the model is that self-care is multidimensional, and fostering self-care will promote health in aging. The core dimensions of the S-CHPA model include internal and external environment, self-care ability, education, self-care activity, and outcomes.5 Within this holistic framework, an older person’s experiences, relationships, and physical

and mental health conditions influence his/her abilities for self-care and self-care activities, which ultimately affect health outcomes.

In this study, the S-CHPA model was expanded to examine the effects of the multicomponent HEART intervention on patients’ health-related outcomes. The model is directly derived from the key concepts and basic assumptions of the S-CHPA and designed to illustrate the complex interplay of personal characteristics and comorbid conditions that influence a patient’s self-care ability.

The HEART intervention is theorized to facilitate patients’ self-care ability by using a multicomponent approach that combines education, assessment, TM, and therapeutic activities to reduce poor outcomes commonly associated with HF. It is postulated that increased self-care behaviors will increase QOL, decrease depression commonly associated with HF, and decrease rehospitalizations due to HF. By intervening at a critical juncture upon entry into home care, combining best practice in HF home care nursing with optimal medical management, and educating patients and their families to maximal understanding of HF management framed within a wellness focus, the synergistic effect is anticipated to evoke improved healthrelated outcomes. The goal of this model is to provide an effective conceptual framework of self-care in older patients with HF receiving home care. This first phase of model development was guided by initial testing

of the HEART intervention in this small pilot study and is illustrated in Figure 1.

## The HEART Intervention

The HEART program consisted of the following 4 nursing activities:

(1) Evidence-based education. An intervention manual for home care nurses that incorporated guidelines for education by the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association’s current nursing guidelines36 and previous studies was framed within an 8-visit sequence that was delivered by the cardiac nurses over an 8 week period.

education content was developed for patients. Patient education materials were a combination of American Heart Association patient educational materials and educational materials developed by the investigative team and home care agency participating in the study. All educational materials were written at a 6- to 9-grade level, presented in an easy-to-read format with a large font, and handouts specific to the visit focus were reviewed with patients by the nurse and put into the patient binder for the patient to later refer to as needed. The topical sequence for each patient may vary, but all topics and protocols will be covered during the intervention period. Prior to the start of the study, nurses received evidence-based training in the HF home care intervention by the principal investigator, which was reinforced by the cardiac program manager at the home care agency throughout the study.

# Methods
## Design

A quasi-experimental, pretest-posttest design was used for this pilot study. The research design compared current nursing practice with HF patients consisting of TM

and traditional home care with new evidence-based protocols delivered by 5 nurses on the cardiac specialty team. Minimum requirements for being on the cardiac team are 2 years of hospital experience in a cardiac or critical care unit. A research grant from a private donor provided the funding for the study. The development of a partnership between the university and home care agency was guided by the principles of communitybased participatory research. Community-based participatory research methodology is being promoted by the National Institutes of Health to advance translational research, enhance research relevance, and address knowledge gaps and disparities more effectively.37

## Feasibility Objectives

To assess the feasibility of the HEART intervention for a full-scale trial with older home care patients with HF, 4 objectives were evaluated related to (1) testing and standardization of education protocols; (2) recruitment efficiency; (3) ease of use, understandability, and reliability of study questionnaires; and (4) acceptability of the intervention.

## Setting and Sample

The study took place in a large multibranch Medicarecertified nonprofit home care agency in Connecticut that services approximately 250 patients with HF per year. The agency had introduced TM about 5 years earlier with the objective of improving HF care. Telemonitoring is now offered to all patients with a primary diagnosis of HF as an addition to traditional home care and had become so common that it is considered usual care.

Following approval from the University of Connecticut internal review board, potential participants were identified by the cardiac program manager. The investigator then met with patients, who expressed interest to explain the purpose of the study, procedures, and time commitment involved; answer any questions; and obtain informed consent. Eligibility criteria included (1) English-speaking adults 50 years or older, (2) with a primary diagnosis of a HF, (3) newly admitted to home care agency (patients who have never received services from the agency), (4) those living at home, and (5) with willingness to participate. Exclusion criteria were (1) nonYEnglish-speaking adults, (2) those who received prior home care services from the agency, and (3) with inadequate mental status to give informed consent (eg, dementia).

All patients were referred to the home car agency after hospitalization for HF. Participants were assured confidentiality and informed of the purpose and benefits of the study and their right to withdraw at any time. Patients were assigned to intervention or control group

based on geographical location. The rationale for using geographic location to assign group membership was a strategy to reduce cross-group information sharing. Patients admitted to the 2 branches in the eastern region of the state were assigned to the intervention group, and patients admitted to the 2 branches in the western region were assigned to the control group.

## Instrumentation

Two instruments and a survey were used in the HEART intervention. Instruments were administered in an interview format by the investigative team. The Minnesota Living With Heart Failure (MLHF) questionnaire38 is a 38： 21-item, self-administered questionnaire designed to measure the effects of HF and treatments for HF on an individual’s QOL. To measure the effects of symptoms, functional limitations, and psychological distress on an individual’s QOL, the MLHF questionnaire asks each person to indicate, using a 6-point, 0- to 5-point Likert scale, how each of the 21 facets prevents them from living as desired. The total summary score can range from 0 to 105; a lower score reflects better health-related QOL. The MLHF has demonstrated reliability and validity in several studies.39 Most recently, the internal reliability coefficient was estimated at 0.86. Other research findings provide evidence of the concurrent and construct validity of the scale.40

The Patient Health Questionnaire 9 (PHQ-9)41 is a brief, 9-item, patient self-report depression assessment specifically developed for use in primary care. The 9 items of the PHQ-9 come directly from the 9 Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition signs and symptoms of major depression. The PHQ-9 was completed by 6000 patients in 8 primary care clinics and 7 obstetrics-gynecology clinics. Construct validity was assessed using the 20-item Short-Form General Health Survey, self-reported sick days and clinic visits, and symptom-related difficulty. Criterion validity was assessed against an independent structured mental health professional interview in a sample of 580 patients. Patient Health Questionnaire 9 scores of 5, 10, 15, and 20 represented mild, moderate, moderately severe, and severe depression, respectively. Results were similar in the primary care and obstetricsgynecology samples. A recent randomized trial with depressed older adults described the reliability and the criterion validity of the PHQ-9 to be excellent and reported a test-retest reliability of 0.86 to 0.91.42 Rationale for the use of the PHQ-9 is based on literature describing the PHQ-9 as an increasingly used clinical tool that is brief and easy to use and score, and specifically queries the American Psychiatric Association’s Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition depression criteria, making it easier for the clinician to diagnose depressive disorders.43

Hospital admissions rates were assessed through self-report during the postintervention interview. Data related to number of hospital readmissions due to exacerbation of HF were collected by the investigators at the 90-day follow-up visit. Chart reviews were also used to obtain rehospitalization information.

A postintervention survey was developed by the investigative team in collaboration with the home care partners to assess the knowledge gained and satisfaction with the HEART intervention.

# Results
## Study Sample

The pilot study sample consisted of 24 older adults with systolic HF. Twelve patients were assigned to the intervention group and 12 to the control group. No statistically significant differences between the control and intervention groups across baseline demographic characteristics were found. Patients were typically elderly with a mean age of 79.04 (SD, 11.8) years, white (84%), and retired (64%) and completed high school as their highest level of education (40%). Most participants were either married (32.4%) or widowed (32.0%). The sample consisted of 14 women and 10 men who reported an average of 1 hospital admission (64%), 1 emergency room visit (44%), and 2 outpatient physician visits during the 90 days before the study began.

The numbers of comorbidities and medications were similar between groups. Study patients had between 2 and 4 comorbid diagnoses, with the most common diagnoses being hypertension (66% intervention, 58% control), coronary artery disease (25% intervention, 33% control), and diabetes mellitus (33% intervention, 25% control). The number and type of HF medications were also similar across groups. All patients were on a combination of medications repre-

sentative of current HF management guidelines. Outcome and Assessment Information Set (OASIS) data were used to classify patients in New York Heart Association HF categories, with most patients (75%) being in class III.

Baseline differences in QOL and depressive symptoms between the intervention and control group participants were also assessed. Although not statistically significant, control group patients reported a slightly lower QOL (58 vs 52; P = .57) and higher rate of depressive symptoms (6.5 vs 7.3; P = .52) compared with intervention patients. A demographic profile of study participants is presented in Table 2.

## Objective 1: Evaluation of Education Protocols

# Patient Educational Materials
The educational component of the HEART intervention for patients was found to be overall feasible and effective, and we identified areas for refinement. To provide an indication of the amount of learning that took place with intervention patients, a 13-item postintervention survey was used to examine patients’ perceptions of the knowledge gained from the HEART intervention. Questions 1 through 9 on the survey examined patients’ perceptions of the knowledge gained from the HEART intervention. Descriptive statistics were used to evaluate patients’ responses to the 9-item Likert scale. Patients were asked to rate their level of agreement to statements that pertained to increased knowledge related to symptom recognition, diet, medications, exercise, managing stress, incorporating therapeutic activities in their lives, and communicating with their doctor. Most patients either agreed or strongly agreed with all of these statements, suggesting that the education materials were feasible and effective. Table 3 displays the survey questions and patients’ responses. Another indication of the usability and effectiveness of

the educational materials was that several patients reported continued use of their education binder for a variety of information such as diet, medications, and the therapeutic activities list at the conclusion of the study.

Evaluation of patient education protocols highlighted areas for refinement. For example, fidelity measures, not previously in place in this pilot study, for patients to record their compliance with medications, diet, and symptom management along with recording the frequency of pleasant events and exercise will be developed in the next phase of the study to enable examination of the protocol adherence and the dose-response relationship on several variables. In addition, in phase 2 of the study, the Dutch Heart Failure questionnaire44 will be administered to measure the actual knowledge gained by patients rather than their perceptions of knowledge to facilitate comparisons in knowledge and satisfaction between intervention and control group patients.

## Training of Nurses in Administration of the HEART Intervention

The evaluation of the training of nurses in the HEART intervention was also found to be overall successful. Nurses reported that the training session and intervention manual were extremely effective in assisting them in organizing the assessment and educational components of the study into their home visits and integrating best-practice protocols in their practice. A fidelity form was developed for nurses to record accomplishment of the session goals for each home visit. Analysis of fidelity forms revealed that all 12 patients in the intervention group received 100% of the assessment and education component of the HEART intervention. To accommodate different levels of patient understanding and individualize the educational content based on patients priorities and needs, the sequence of topics varied with all topics covered within the 8-visit intervention.

An area identified to enhance nurses knowledge of evidence-based HF care and ability to collaborate with physicians to optimize patient outcomes was further training. Home care nurses often have limited informa-

tion and typically do not collaborate with physicians or ask questions related to topics such as optimal pharmacological management, but this can be changed with education.45 In phase 2 of the study, the training session will be increased to two 4-hour sessions. Further information will be requested from referring physicians to home care related to ejection fraction and type of HF (systolic/diastolic), procedures will be put in place for nurses to have access to hospital records, and the nurse training will include collaboration and empowerment strategies to facilitate a multidisciplinary approach to HF management.

## Objective 2: Evaluation of Recruitment Efficiency

To evaluate recruitment for the HEART intervention, enrollment data were reviewed, and inclusion/exclusion criteria were evaluated. Over the 6-month period of enrollment, a total of 55 patients were screened for eligibility. Thirty-one patients were excluded because of ineligibility, with the most frequent reasons for exclusion being they had received previous services from the agency for HF (51%) or refused TM (35%). Of these patients, 24 patients with a primary diagnosis of HF met the study criteria and agreed to participate in the study. On average, 4 patients were enrolled in the study per month. Our initial rationale for excluding patients who had received previous home care services was based on the assumption that a proactive approach, initiated at the point of first admission to home care, represents a teachable moment; an intervention that follows soon after admission captures patients and families when they have questions about the future.

Evaluation of enrollment data revealed 3 of 5 HF referrals to the home care agency had received previous services and thus were excluded from the study. Based on this finding, the inclusion criteria will be modified to include all HF admissions. Modification of the HEART pilot inclusion criteria will facilitate increased enrollment and generalizability of the intervention. Data can then be analyzed to examine outcomes based on disease severity.

## Objective 3: Testing of Study Questionnaires

## MLHF Questionnaire

Study instruments were evaluated for ease of use and reliability, and the postintervention survey was evaluated for face validity. The MLHF questionnaire, as well as all questionnaires and data collection forms, was administered using an interview format to determine the ease and length of administration by the investigative team and eliminate missing data. The MLHF proved to be efficiently and effectively administered, with the questionnaire completed within 10

## HEART Postintervention Survey

The 13-item survey consisted of 9 questions on a Likerttype scale and 4 open-ended questions that resulted in quantitative data along with qualitative responses. An expert panel, consisting of 3 nursing colleagues with expertise in the area of HF in home care, was invited to evaluate the survey for content validity. Clarity and relevance were rated with a 4-point scale. Eight of the 9 items were deemed to be clear, and 1 item was reworded based on panel suggestion; all survey items were judged to be relevant. The HEART postintervention survey also proved to be easy to use and straightforward for patients.

## Objective 4: Evaluation of the Acceptability of the HEART Intervention

Acceptability of the HEART intervention was assessed with open-ended questions 10 to 13 on the postintervention survey. Qualitative analysis of these questions provides evidence of the acceptability as well as the effectiveness of the HEART intervention.

Patients were asked to provide responses to 4 openended questions. (1) What are your overall feelings related to the home care intervention? (2) What was done especially well by the home care nurses? (3) What could have been better about the home care intervention? And (4) what changes would you suggest in the home care intervention for optimal long-term implementation? Qualitative data generated from the 4 openended questions were analyzed using Krippendorff’s47 method of content analysis consisting of the following processes: (1) Comments were coded into 2 mutually exclusive and exhaustive categories, positive and negative comments designated with a value of 0 and 1, respectively. (2) Categories with similar content were

clustered, allowing identification of central themes and the frequency of their occurrence. Of the 12 surveys analyzed, 32 positive and 5 negative comments regarding the experience were identified. Four prominent themes illustrative of patients’ perceptions of the HEART interventions were developed after analysis of the open-ended questions.

## Theme 1: The TM Experience

Telemonitoring was a new experience for patients in this study, and for most, it was a positive one. All of the 12 participants discussed TM during the poststudy interview. For most patients (83%), TM was a good experience that made patients feel secure and assisted them in developing a self-care schedule. One patent stated, ‘‘I feel safe with the monitor. It takes my weight and blood pressure every morning, and I know the nurse will call if something is wrong.’’ Another patient commented, ‘‘The monitor has helped me get a routine. It rings and then I do the monitor and take my meds.’’ For 2 patients, the TM experience came with some challenges. For 1 patient, the ringing of the TM caused anxiety: ‘‘I get nervous when it rings; I feel I have to rush over to it, even though the nurses said I can reset it. I almost fell once.’’ For the other patient, waiting for the monitor to ring was the problem. I had to plan my life around it and make sure I was home at noon.’’

## Theme 2: The Nurse-Patient Relationship

The relationship that patients built with their cardiac nurse emerged as the most significant aspect of the intervention experience. Repeatedly, patients used words such as ‘‘knowledgeable,’’ ‘‘competent,’’ ‘‘caring,’’ and ‘‘outstanding’’ to describe their nurses. The trust that developed in the nurse-patient relationship was described by a patient: ‘‘My nurse was great, and I trusted her. If I have gained weight, I always knew she would call. It was good having the same nurse who knew so much about heart failure.’’ Another patient discussed the expertise and caring of her cardiac nurse: ‘‘I looked forward to Amy’s visits, she took the time to make sure I understood my HF. She was always so friendly and caring.’’

## Theme 3: Empowerment

It was apparent that most patients were empowered with their new knowledge. As a patient stated, ‘‘I learned a lot about HF that I did not know before. I liked the booklet of information and having the nurse go over it with me.’’ One patient described how the use of the pleasant event list assisted her in transitioning to a new environment: ‘‘Since being discharged from the VNA, I moved to another state. I packed my list of activities in my suitcase. I have been using it, and it has

helped me meet people. I do not feel as down as before.’’ Another patient stated, ‘‘I never realized there was so much to learn about HF, and I feel as if I know a lot about HF now that will help me stay well.’’

## Theme 4: The Road to Self-management

By the end of the 8-week intervention that coincided with the end of the 60-day certification period, most patients (75%) were ready for discharge and on the road to self-management of their HF. A patient describes his readiness to self-manage his HF: ‘‘I liked being a part of this study. I understand my HF better now, and I feel I will be able to manage it on my own.’’ Another stated, ‘‘I know what I have to do to keep things under control.’’ A few patients (25%) would need to continue home care to stabilize their HF or because of comorbid conditions. One patient described her progress: ‘‘I have come along way, but I’m still receiving home care services because my medications were changed and I began to gain weight again.’’

Patients’ overall satisfaction with the intervention was high, and all 12 patients felt the program was valuable and practical. The recommendation to continue the HEART intervention was unanimous. The few suggestions for improvement focused on technical problems with the TM and consistent scheduling of visits. One patient’s comment was representative of the study cohort: ‘‘I think the agency should keep doing this program. I think it’s great that there is a special HF program, and many patients will benefit from it.’’

# TM Outcomes
Telemonitoring outcomes of intervention patients were assessed by recording the number of phone calls placed to patients by TM nurses and abnormal findings on daily data. All intervention patients except one were contacted by the TM nurses at least once. An average number of 6 phone calls were made to each patient. The most frequent reasons for contacting patients in this group were decreased blood pressure (41%), weight gain (33%), and increased shortness of breath (25%). Most calls were managed on the phone, and a few required an additional nursing visit. Telemonitoring nurses contacted the physician for any finding over the established parameters, which resulted in 4 patients having their medications adjusted and 2 started on oxygen.

# Potential Efficacy of Health-Related Outcomes
Although not the primary focus of this pilot study as the analysis of any type of pilot study should be mainly descriptive,46 a secondary aim was to assess the potential of the HEART intervention to positively affect psychological and physical outcomes in patients with HF. Three outcomes, QOL, depressive symptoms, and hospitalizations were evaluated.

A preliminary analysis of outcomes using repeatedmeasures analyses revealed that participants who completed the HEART intervention demonstrated a significant increase in QOL and decrease in depressive symptoms compared with their control group counterparts at the study end point (F = 8.99, P = .007; F = 35.10, P = .001). Preintervention MLHF scores for patients in the intervention group (mean, 52.7 [SD, 18.7]) were significantly lower after the intervention (mean, 42.5 [SD, 17.9]). No change was observed in the MLHF scores before (mean, 57.5 [SD, 17.0]) and after the intervention for those in the control group (mean, 58.0 [SD, 17.0]). Mean PHQ-9 scores were 6.50 (SD, 4.5) in the intervention group and 7.30 (SD, 4.9) for control group patients. The mean overall improvement in the PHQ-9 scores in the intervention group was 3.0 points compared with 1.1 points in the control group, which proved to be significant (F = 35.10, P = .001).
