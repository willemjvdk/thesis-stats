# Methods
## Recruitment

This is a longitudinal study of a randomized controlled trial (RCT) evaluating the effects of a 9-week iCBT programme on depression compared to an ODF. The design, procedure, and the results of the study have been reported previously.10 In brief, a total of 144 CVD patients with at least mild depression were randomized to 9 weeks of either iCBT (n = 72) or ODF (n = 72). Patients with CVD were recruited from four locations in the southeast of Sweden. Those who were interested in participation were asked to register online and to answer a depression questionnaire [i.e. Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9)]16 and a questionnaire regarding background characteristics. Inclusion criteria for participation were at least mild depression (i.e., PHQ-9 score >_5),16 age above 18 years, NYHA class I–III, no CVD-related hospitalizations in the last 4 weeks, ability to speak and read Swedish, and access to a computer or a comparable device and an Internet connection. Eligible patients were contacted and invited for a clinical telephone interview to ensure that the inclusion criteria were fulfilled, whereafter randomized into 9 weeks of either iCBT or ODF.

The study was approved by the Regional Ethical Review Board in Linko¨ping Sweden (no. 2016/72-31) and was registered at clinicaltrials.- gov (NCT02778074).

# Results
## Population

The flow chart of the participants is presented in Figure 1. Of the 144 patients were included, a total of 22% (iCBT n = 14 and ODF n = 18) had missing data at the 12-month follow-up, leaving 112 participants with original data at 12 months. A drop-out analysis of those with missing data at the 12-month follow-up did not reveal any significant differences in background characteristics or depression score at baseline, except for heart failure, which was more common compared to those with no missing data (42% vs. 15%, P = 0.001).

At baseline, there were no significant differences in the characteristics or diagnoses between those randomized to the iCBT or ODF group (Table 1). The mean age was 63 years (SD 12) and 62% were men (n = 89), 62% retired (n = 89) and 74% married or in a relationship (n = 106). Most participants had atrial fibrillation or flutter 45% (n = 65), followed by myocardial infarction or angina 34% (n = 49), and heart failure 26% (n = 38). Most of the participants had at least one comorbidity, 81% (n = 116). Hypertension 53% (n = 76), diabetes 15% (n = 21) and stroke 13% (n = 19) were the most common comorbidities.

## The long-term effect on depression in the iCBT group

We found significant differences in estimates of fixed effects between baseline and 12-month follow-up (PHQ-9 P = 0.001, MADRS-S P = 0.001). There was no significant change between 9-week and 12-month follow-up (PHQ-9 P = 0.65, MADRS-S P = 0.438) or between 6- and 12-month follow-ups (PHQ-9 P = 0.686, MADRS-S P = 0.72) (Table 2).

## Comparison of the long-term effect on depression between the iCBTand the adjusted ODF group

Of the participants in the adjusted ODF group, a total of 60% (n = 27) completed the 12-month follow-up. A drop-out analysis within the adjusted ODF group did not reveal any significant

differences in background characteristics or depression score at baseline, except for heart failure, which was more common compared to those with no missing data (45% vs. 18%, P = 0.003). The descriptive statistics for the original data over time for the four measurement points for both groups are shown in Supplementary material online 3.

Comparing the differences between the iCBT and ODF group at 6 and 12 months using PHQ-9 as the dependent variable, estimates of fixed effects revealed no significant difference between the groups at 6 and 12 months (group, P = 0 . 2 7 8 ;interaction effect, time - group, P = 0.099) (Table 3). In addition, we did not find any significant difference in estimates of fixed effects between the groups at 6 and 12 months in the LMM analysis using MADRS-S as a dependent variable (group, P = 0 . 1 1 9 ;interaction effect, time - group, P = 0.002). Figure 2 illustrates the changes over time between each time point.

## Factors associated with changes in depression at 12-month follow-up in the iCBT group

Of the participants in the iCBT group, a total of 81% (n = 58) completed the 12-month follow-up. A drop-out analysis within the iCBT group did not reveal any significant differences in background characteristics or depression score at baseline, except for heart failure, which was more common compared to those with no missing data (39% vs. 13%, P = 0.023) and the number of performed modules (mean = 3.2 vs. mean = 6.2, P = 0.001).

Exploratory variables not included as possible exploratory in the regression model were: gender (P = 0.238), age (P = 0.530), living situation ( P = 0 . 2 5 2 ), education (P = 0.484), alcohol consumption ( P = 0 . 7 5 0 ), myocardial infarction and angina ( P = 0 . 9 4 7 ) ,, atrial fibrillation (P = 0.422), number of comorbidities (P = 0.946), NYHA class ( P = 0 . 6 4 6 ), at

baseline prescribed antidepressants (P = 0.211), at baseline prescribed anxiolytics (P = 0.472), and number of logins (P = 0.854). Variables included in the multiple regression analysis were PHQ-9 score at baseline, occupational status, diagnosis of heart failure, and number of performed modules. In the regression analysis, occupational status (P = 0.060) and the number of performed modules in the iCBT programme (P = 0.410) were found not to be significant and were excluded from the model. A higher score on the PHQ-9 at baseline (beta = 0.275, P = 0.028) was more likely to have improvements in PHQ-9 scores at 12 months, whereas a diagnosis of heart failure was associated with decreased improvement in PHQ-9 scores at the 12-month follow-up (beta = 0.323, P = 0.010) (Table 4).
