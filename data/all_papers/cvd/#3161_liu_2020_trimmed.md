## A R T I C L E I N F O

## A B S T R A C T

Objective: 1) Evaluate the efficacy of e-Counseling vs. Control to promote lifestyle behaviors at 4 and 12-month follow-ups, 2) examine whether these behaviors changes were associated with lower blood pressure (BP), and Framingham Risk Index (FRI) at 12-month.

Methods: Hypertensive patients (n = 264) were randomized to the e-Counseling or the Control group. Primary trial outcome was BP and secondary outcomes included exercise and diet behaviors. This study presented the results of secondary outcomes. Linear mixed models evaluated treatment effects at 4 and 12-month. Treatment-by-sex exploratory analyses were conducted if no main treatment effect was observed.

Results: Daily steps significantly improved in e-Counseling vs. Controls at 12-month. Urinary sodium at 12-month did not significantly differ between the groups, but treatment-by-sex analysis showed that e-Counseling females lowered urinary sodium relative to Controls at 12 months. Improvements in steps and dietary sodium were significantly associated with improvements in BP and FRI at 12-month.

Conclusion: This hypertension e-Counseling protocol can promote long-term lifestyle behavior changes. Adherence to the lifestyle behavior change was associated with BP and FRI reduction at 12-month.

Practice implications: The hypertension e-counseling protocol has the potential to improve hypertension care and intervention reach.

# 2. Methods
This study analyzed behavioral outcomes of a double blind, multi-centered randomized controlled trial: REACH (www.ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT01541540). These behaviour outcomes were secondary outcomes in the trial. The primary outcomes of the trial was blood pressure. Details of the study design of the REACH trial have been described [7].

## 2.1. Participants

Participants were recruited from the Heart and Stroke Canada website. Following logon, all participants were prompted to confirm if they would like to be contacted for a research project on high BP and whether they resided near our recruitment sites: Toronto, Vancouver, London, Grey Bruce region of Ontario, and Prince Edward Island province. A research assistant from the central site in Toronto then contacted prospective participants to conduct the initial screening call. Inclusion criteria required that participants were diagnosed with stage 1 or 2 hypertension (baseline blood pressure > = 140/90 if no medications; > = 130/85 if on medications; an unchanged prescription for > = 2 months if on medications), and between the ages of 35–74 years. Hypertension diagnoses were confirmed with the participant’s family doctor at baseline if they were not prescribed anti-hypertensive medication. Exclusion criteria included: diagnosis of kidney disease, major psychiatric illness (e.g. psychosis), alcohol or drug dependence in the previous year, pregnancy, sleep apnea, institutional residence or an inability to comprehend English. The study was conducted between February 2012 and March 2016. All participants gave written informed consent.

## 2.2. Study design

REACH was a 12-month, multicenter, 2 parallel group, doubleblind, randomized controlled trial. Eligible participants underwent study assessments at baseline, 4- and 12-months at one of our recruitment sites in Toronto (n = 174), Vancouver (n = 39), Grey Bruce region of Ontario (n = 19), London (n = 15), or Prince Edward Island (n = 17). Recruitment took place from January 2012 to December 2015. After confirming participant eligibility at the baseline assessment, participants were randomly assigned to e-Counseling or Control. Both the e-Counseling and Control groups were informed that they would receive e-messages over 12 months that would support a heart healthy lifestyle, as an essential component of blood pressure management. Participants were not given any financial compensation for participating in the study. However, reimbursements for travel to the assessments was provided (up to $21 CAD per visit). A step-counter was also provided for participating in the study.

## 2.3. Study interventions

The e-Counseling and Control interventions were organized into 28 self-guided e-sessions (28 sessions in total) and they were delivered to the participants via email on a set schedule. The emails contained web-links to each internet-based session for e-Counseling and Control groups. For the initial 4 months, participants received weekly emails, followed by biweekly emails for the subsequent 4 months, and finally monthly emails for the last 4 months. The delivery of the intervention was fully automated.

The Control intervention included content representative of the e-based support provided by the Blood Pressure Action Plan of the

Heart and Stroke Foundation of Canada. Each session was delivered as an email newsletter article. The content of the articles included information aimed at improving self-help skills for managing blood pressure, reviewing lifestyle and medications with healthcare professionals, monitoring BP, increasing exercise and dietary intake of fruit and vegetables, adhering to medications, decreasing dietary sodium and alcohol, and becoming smoke free.

The e-Counseling intervention used key components from motivational interviewing and cognitive-behavioral therapy to promote adherence to self-care behaviors. A detailed description of the weekly sessions was previously published [7,8]. The content of each session included original videos, online handouts, selfmonitoring tools and self-help resources, in order to promote and reinforce the motivational and cognitive-behaviour strategies used in the intervention. Controls received educational self-help resources that reflected conventional e-based support for heart healthy lifestyle change from heart health organizations, such as the Heart and Stroke Foundation. In the e-counseling intervention, we extensively used original videos featuring members of our team to help build a (virtual) therapeutic relationship with participants. We developed three types of videos (up to 10 min duration) featuring team members in their respective expert fields (diet, exercise, medications, and smoke-free living). The types of videos consisted of: 1) dramatic vignettes that reflected and validated participant experiences as fictional characters learned to accept the diagnosis of hypertension and then as they planned and carried out lifestyle changes with the support of a healthcare professional or peer; 2) expert-type presentations with self-help guidelines for adhering to self-care behavior; 3) an open discussion among peers that provided positive role modeling and guidance.

During the initial sessions, the REACH e-Counselling intervention guided participants through an assessment of their stage of readiness to adhere to self-care behaviors, according to Prochaska’s Transtheoretical Model of health behavior change [9]. Based on motivational interviewing components, e-counseling sessions were designed to help participants resolve ambivalence about behavior change and build readiness for change. This was achieved by validating the participant’s initial stage of readiness, guiding them to select an intervention goal that was matched to their readiness stage, reinforcing their active and collaborative role in the REACH intervention, and helping them to resolve ambivalence for change by linking their behavior change goal to a salient personal priority.

In the subsequent sessions, participants then selected their priority for behavior change from a list of behaviors, including exercise (30–60 minutes of moderate intensity dynamic exercise, 4–7 days per week), diet, smoke-free living, and adherence to antihypertensive medications based on the Canadian Physical Activity Guidelines [10], Canada’s Food Guide [11] and the Canadian Hypertension Education Program [12]. Cognitive-behavioral strategies were used to reinforce participant efficacy for initiating and sustaining change. Specific strategies included educating participants about how to set manageable behavioral goals for self-care adherence, outlining progressive steps in the change plan for self-care, facilitating performance-based feedback with self-monitoring tools for BP and self-care behavior, providing rewarding feedback about progress in initiating or sustaining behavior change, maintaining virtual peer support and positive behavioral modeling via video material, and reviewing guidelines to manage stress to sustain therapeutic change in self-care [13].

## 2.4. Physical activity behaviors

Daily physical activity was recorded on a triaxial digital pedometer (LifeSource/A&D XL-18CN Activity Monitor) [14]. Participants wore the pedometer for 7 continuous days following

each study assessment at baseline, 4 months and 12 months. An extremely high or low step count (i.e. 2 standard deviations from the population mean) were identified through the week to ensure that the participants in fact wore the pedometer for the entire day. Average daily step count was calculated using 3 randomly selected days during the week, and 1 day from the weekend, which is in keeping with conventional procedures for estimating daily step count [15].

## 2.5. Dietary behaviors

Dietary sodium intake was measured by collecting 24 -h urinary sodium at baseline and 12-month visits. Participants collected urine samples over a 24 -h period and returned these specimens to our clinic assessment sites for analysis. Adherence to recommended guidelines for daily intake of fruits and vegetables were evaluated using the NIH/NCI Diet History Questionnaire (DHQ) [16] at baseline, 4-, and 12-months. The sum of daily servings of fruit and vegetables were computed to determine the combined daily servings of fruits and vegetables consumed.

# 2.6. Cardiovascular outcomes
Systolic BP, Diastolic BP, and the Framingham 10-year absolute risk index for cardiovascular disease (CVD) were recorded at baseline, 4-, and 12-months. BP was assessed by a validated protocol for automated BP assessments using the BpTRU device [12]. Participants were seated for at least 5 min prior to activation of BpTRU. Next, an appropriately sized BP cuff was applied to the left arm of the subject by a trained research assistant. The initial BP measurement was discarded. The research assistant left the room once the BpTRU device was cued to complete a series of 5 BP recordings at 1-minute intervals. Blood samples were taken by the hospital/clinic lab using conventional procedures to assess the 12 - h fasting lipoprotein cholesterol profile. The Framingham Risk Index was computed using SBP, Age, Sex, Cholesterol (Total cholesterol and High-density lipoprotein), treatment for hypertension, smoking status and diabetes status [17].

## 2.7. Statistics

Baseline group differences were analyzed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) and chi-square tests for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. The criterion for significance was set to p < 0.05, 2-sided.

Linear Mixed Models (LMM) with a random effects intercept evaluated whether e-Counseling compared to the control group resulted in greater improvements in study behavior outcomes at 4 months, and 12 months. If a main treatment effect was not observed in each LMM analysis for e-Counseling vs. Control, as hypothesized, then we examined the treatment-by-sex interaction. All significant interactions or main effects in our statistical models were followed up with post-hoc contrasts, using a Bonferroni correction for the statistical significance criterion of p < 0.05. Intention-to-treat approach was used to analyze our outcome variables. Missing data were checked to establish that data were missing at random. Multiple imputation was applied to address missing data.

Multivariable linear regression models were used to evaluate whether reaching the recommended target for physical activity and diet at 12 months was independently associated with improved cardiovascular outcomes at 12 months. Dependent variables in each regression model included SBP, DBP, and the FRI at 12-months. Predictors for each regression model included baseline values of the dependent variable, sex, age, treatment groups and whether participants reached the recommended self-care behavior targets

recommended by the Canadian Hypertension Education Program. Categorical variables were created to identify whether the participants reached greater or equal to 10,000 steps per day, 8 servings of fruit and vegetables per day and less than or equal to 87 mmol/L of urinary sodium (2000 mg per day). Predictors of change in the FRI included both antihypertensive and lipid lowering medications while excluding participants with established CVD. In a post-hoc analysis, we used regression models to evaluate whether reaching multiple self-care behaviors targets (defined as reached greater than or equal to 10,000 steps per day, 8 servings of fruit and vegetables per day, and less than or equal to 87 mmol/L of urinary sodium) resulted in lower SBP, DBP and FRI at 12-month.

Sample size estimation for this study was based on the primary outcome study [8]. Briefly, a previous meta-analysis found a weighted mean difference in SBP of -4.69 mm Hg, with a pooled standard deviation of 18.81 [19]. A total sample required to replicate this outcome with a 2-parallel group design, type 1 error = 5% and power = 80%, was 528 participants. We estimated a 23% attrition rate for a “low contact” e-based trial [18]. This resulted in a total sample estimate of 624 participants.

# 3. Results
## 3.1. Demographics & baseline characteristics

In total, 264 hypertensive patients were enrolled in the REACH trial (Fig. 1). No significant differences in baseline characteristics were found between the e-Counselling and Control groups (Table 1). At baseline, the mean number of steps per day for both groups was lower than the Canadian age-norm [20]. However, the mean reported consumption of daily fruits and vegetables at baseline was in line with the recommended servings per day according to the Canadian Food Guide [11].

## 3.2. Blood pressure and framingham risk index

Changes in blood pressure and Framingham risk index outcomes were previously published [8]. Briefly, the magnitude of SBP reduction did not differ between groups at 4 months, but there was significantly greater reduction for e-counseling versus Control at 12 months ( 10.1 mmHg [95% confidence interval (CI), 12.5, 7.6] versus 6.0 mmHg [95% CI, 8.5, 3.5]; P = .02). The magnitude of change in DBP did not differ significantly between e-counseling versus control at either 4- or 12-months. Among participants without established cardiovascular disease (e-Counseling, n = 128; Control, n = 122), the magnitude of FRI reduction was significantly greater for e-Counseling versus Control at both 4 (2.1% [95% CI, 2.9, 1.3] versus -0.6% [95% CI, 1.5, 0.2]; P = .005) and 12 months ( 1.9% [95% CI, 3.3, 0.5] versus 0.02% [95% CI, 1.2, 1.7]; P = .02, respectively).

## 3.3. Physical activity behaviors

The change in daily steps did not significantly differ for the e-Counseling group relative to Control at 4 months (p = .17) (See Table 2). However, at 12 months, daily steps were significantly increased from baseline only in the e-Counselling group [962 steps (95% CI, 105,1820), p = .02] but not in Control [-312 steps (95% CI, -1198,574), p = .90]. Daily steps at 12-months were significantly greater for the e-Counselling group relative to Control (P = .02) (See Table 2).

## 3.4. Fruit and vegetable consumption

The change in total daily fruit and vegetables did not significantly differ between e-Counseling and the Control at 4

and 12 months (Table 2). However, we observed a significant main effect of time only at 4 month suggesting that participants significantly increased in total daily fruit and vegetable consumption from baseline to 4 months in both Control and the e-Counseling groups [0.72 servings/day; (95% CI, 0.01,1.4), p = 0.045].

## 3.5. Sodium consumption

24 -h urinarysodiumwas collected at baseline and 12 months. No significant group difference was observed at 12-month (p = .29). However, exploratory analysis revealed that changes in 24 -h urinary sodium at 12-month was associated with a significant sex by treatment interaction (p = .035). Only females in the e-Counselling group showed a significant reduction in 24 -h urinary sodium at 12- months relative to baseline [Urinary Sodium: -17.5 mmol/24 h (95% CI, -34, -0.14); p = 0.045]. Among females, the magnitude of sodium

reduction in the e-Counselling group was significantly greater compared with the Control group at 12 months [Urinary Sodium: -23.4 mmol/24 h (95%CI, -43.4, -3.3); p = 0.02]. Among men, 24 -h urinary sodium at 12 months did not significantly change from baseline for the Control group [Urinary Sodium: -20.6 mmol/24 h (95%CI, -40.4, 0.4); p = 0.06] and e-Counselling groups [Urinary Sodium: 7.2 mmol/24 h (95%CI, -26.6, 12.0); p = 0.46]. No significant difference between e-Counselling and Control groups for Urinary sodium were observed for men at 12 months relative to baseline [Urinary Sodium: -20.1 mmol/24 h (95%CI, -43.9,2.1); p = 0.08].

# 3.6. Behavioral determinants of cardiovascular outcomes
Participants that reached greater or equal to 10,000 steps per day at 12-months showed a lower resting SBP (4.17 mmHg) and DBP (-2.48 mmHg) at 12-months compared with those

participants that reached less than 10,000 steps (p < 0.05). Participants that excreted less or equal to 87 mmol/L of urinary sodium also showed a lower resting SBP (-5.13 mmHg) at 12- months compared with those participants that excreted more than 87 mmol/L of urinary sodium (p < 0.05) (Table 3). The post-hoc regression analyses demonstrated that reaching multiple self-care behavior targets (physical activity, > 10,000 steps per day; fruit and vegetable >8 servings of fruit and vegetables per day; and <87 mmol/L of 24 -h urinary sodium) were associated with a step-wise reduction of resting SBP and DBP at 12-months (Table 3).
