## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# Methods
The heart failure home care (HFHC) Trial was a multicenter, randomized controlled clinical trial with blinded endpoint evaluation, designed to compare a control group receiving standard heart failure care with enhanced patient education and follow-up (SC) with a test group managed with computer-based telephonic heart failure monitoring system (HFMS: Alere Day Link Heart Failure Monitoring System, Alere Medical, Reno, NV).

Eligible patients were randomized between April 2002 and September 2005. Data were collected, compiled, and analyzed at the University of Pittsburgh. The trial used an independent adjudication event committee to classify deaths, hospitalizations, and adverse events and was monitored by an independent Data Safety Monitoring Board. The trial was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Pittsburgh, Case Western University, and Mount Sinai Medical Center. Informed consent was obtained from all patients before study enrollment.

## Study Population

The study population was elderly ($65 years old); women and non-Caucasian males, primarily African Americans and Hispanics

who carried the diagnosis of heart failure secondary to systolic dysfunction, and had symptoms despite optimal treatment with pharmacologic therapy. To ensure that baseline therapies were identical in the 2 treatment arms of the study and that patients were receiving adequate and appropriate medical therapy we focused this study on patients with systolic heart failure. All patients were Medicare beneficiaries and had to have been hospitalized for heart failure within 6 months of randomization.

The trial was conducted at 3 sites with affiliations to a major academic medical center: Pittsburgh, PA (University of Pittsburgh); Cleveland, OH (Case Western Reserve University); and Miami, FL (Mount Sinai Medical Center). However, a unique aspect of this trial was that patients were recruited through the development of cooperative networks that had been established with primary care groups in each of these cities. Thus, this trial assessed the utility of the HFMS in a ‘‘real-world setting’’ rather than within the confines of the academic medical center. The study design and clinical outcomes were reported in detail elsewhere.10

## Procedures

After eligibility was assessed patients were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to either SC, which included patient 1-on-1 education, education to clinicians, an effort to use evidenced-based optimal medical treatment, and a commercially available digital home scale with management by primary physician ; or the intervention group (SC plus HFMS). HFMS consists of a home-based disease management program to monitor and to detect early signs and symptoms of heart failure using telecommunication equipment; such early detection could allow practitioners to focus their clinical resources on patients needing interventions. Each primary care physician who participated in the trial and at least one nurse from each primary care office have undergone a didactic training program lead by either University of Pittsburgh Medical Center in Pittsburgh, Case Western Reserve Hospital, or Mount Sinai Medical Center. The training program was either the ‘‘Advanced Heart Failure Scene’’ program or a similar program. In addition, each participating physician was provided with copies of the Guidelines for the care of patients with heart failure published by the Heart Failure Society of America. In addition they were provided with the updated American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association Heart Failure Guidelines. Each site’s project coordinator visited the primary care physicians to familiarize the participating sites with the schedule of evaluation. Patients assigned to HFMS were contacted to arrange the delivery and setup of the device. The system includes an electronic scale and an individualized symptom response system (DayLink monitor) linked via a standard phone line to a computerized database staffed by trained nurses. Patients were instructed to weigh themselves and respond to heart failure symptom questions daily. Each patient’s primary care physician was responsible for selecting the monitoring parameters according to their patients’ disease status.

The patients were asked questions such as: did you wake up with shortness of breath during the night; did you use an extra pillow last night, are your feet more swollen than usual; are you more tired than usual; and are you coughing more than usual? The HFMS nurses reviewed the transmitted data daily (7 days/week, 365 days/year) and contacted the patient to verify any changes observed in heart failure symptoms or weight. Changes in weight beyond a prespecified amount or changes in symptoms were reported to the attending primary care physician. There was a standardized alert in which physicians individually choose the

parameters for their patients. If, on review by the HFMS nurse, benchmarks were met for weight alerts or symptom alerts, the HFMS nurse immediately contacted the patient to ensure that the alerts had been met. After nurse-patient interactions, the primary physician was immediately notified of the change in either weight or symptoms by a fax report. Comments by an HFMS nurse were added when data were sent; however, medical recommendations were not made to the physicians. This report also graphically demonstrated trends in both weight and symptoms. A follow-up call was made to the physician’s office to ensure that the information was received. During periods of clinical stability, reports were faxed to the physician’s office at predetermined points as requested by the physician. Physicians were then able to adjust medications, schedule an office visit, or initiate other therapeutic changes with the goal being to prevent further deterioration and to stave off the need for a hospitalization. Patients randomized to SC were provided a digital home scale and instructed to weigh themselves daily and record heart failure symptoms. All participants were provided with educational materials and information as to when they should seek medical attention related to the worsening of their heart failure.

## Data

All patients were seen in their primary care physician’s office by the study nurse coordinator during their baseline and 6-month end-of-study visits. During this initial visit, the study nurse coordinator conducted a 1-to-1 educational session with the patient which included the ‘‘Living with Heart Failure Booklet’’ (Copyright University Hospital of Cleveland). Patients were also contacted by telephone 30 days and 3 months after randomization by non-medical personnel masked to treatment assignment to collect clinical data, including vital status, the type and date of cardiovascular-related hospital visits, and the administration of 2 quality of life instruments, the Medical Outcome Study 12 Item Short Form11 and the Kansas City Quality of Life Questionnaire.12 Deaths were ascertained either through interview with caregivers or through the national death index. Medicare claims data were received on each subject who had a medical claim in the Medicare administrative data system.

## Assignment of Costs

We used the information from the Medicare claims data to determine the cost to Medicare as well as the total costs for those services covered by Medicare.13 We looked at all services, not just those related to cardiac failure, in case improved congestive heart failure care led to offsets in cost elsewhere. We set Medicare costs at the amount that Medicare paid on a given claim. We estimated total costs to be the amount that Medicare determined to be the reasonable cost for a given service. These 2 costs differ because the Medicare beneficiary has to pay a deductible and/or cost-insurance for some services. (Because there are no costsharing requirements for hospice or home health services, total costs and costs to Medicare are identical for these 2 services.) We assigned the cost for covered services to each patient by determining first the claims that covered the 6-month period during which the beneficiary was enrolled in the study. If a given claim covered a period that started before the enrollment into the study (or ended after enrollment into the study), we determined the proportion of time that the beneficiary was in the study and allocated that share of the costs to the patient. We then summed these costs. Because outpatient drugs were not a Medicare-covered benefit

during this period, we estimated drug costs in the following way. We obtained data on the most common drug, frequency, and dosage and its Redbook cost for each category of medication listed on the form (ie, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor, b-blocker). These costs were obtained from a large study of other elderly patients. We determined the average per diem cost. We assumed that the subject was on the drug until we had other information. If a patient was found to have changed drugs (eg, in month 1), then we assigned half of the first month’s cost to the initial drug and half to half of those costs to the second drug. For the patients who withdrew from the study, we assumed that they continued the drug they were using at the last interview before withdrawing until the end of the study. Because Medicare did not cover drug costs during the time of the study, these were assigned to the total cost category only. We received data on the 6-month cost of the HFMS from the company ($804) and we assigned those costs to each patient randomized to HFMS. Because the patients randomized to SC were given scales, we assigned each patient in SC a cost of $25. We counted these costs as Medicare costs.

## Types of Costs

We determined the average 6-month costs for the following types of Medicare covered services: hospice, durable medical equipment, home health, outpatient, physician, and inpatient care. Inpatient costs include both hospital and nursing home costs. However, they do not include physician costs that are incurred during hospital stay. These costs are included in physician costs. The intervention costs and imputed drug costs are also included.

## Statistical Analyses

We first checked the data to see if there were any outliers. The distribution of costs was log-normal after outliers were removed. Therefore, because of the non-normality distribution of the cost variables by types of services, the Wilcoxon nonparametric test was used to test the difference in costs between the 2 study arms. We then used multivariate regression to determine the effect of treatment assignment and other variables (age, gender, race, heart failure classification, whether the patient died, body mass index, path into the study, and study site) on total Medicare costs and total costs on the log scale.

# Results
## Overview

We had Medicare data for 304 patients (146 were randomized to SC and 158 to HFMS). We had fewer patients for this study than were included in the primary report because we were either unable to access the patients’ Medicare claims data or the patients were enrolled in Medicare health maintenance organizations and thus they did not have any direct Medicare claims.

## Baseline Characteristics

With the exception of race, the baseline characteristics of the patients were similar across the groups (Table 1).

## Outliers

We examined the data to determine if there were any outliers. The data were skewed and we identified 2 outliers

($142,500 and $157,500), which we excluded from all further analyses (Fig. 1).

## 6-month Costs of Treating Heart Failure

We found that the average 6-month costs to Medicare were $16,820 per patient (standard deviation $22,034), whereas the average total 6 month medical costs were $19,053 (standard deviation $23,352).

## Costs by Study Arm

The mean costs, their standard deviation, and median costs in total and by type of service for each of the study arms for both Medicare and total costs per patient were analyzed (Table 2). Average Medicare costs were significantly higher (28%) for patients randomized to the HFMS than for patients randomized to SC. The difference in overall costs exceeded the cost of the intervention. We also found that, with the exception of hospice, the mean costs to Medicare of patients randomized to HFMS were higher than those randomized to SC for each type of service. However, none of these individual cost differences were statistically significant. The pattern for the median costs was slightly different. Although the median of Medicare costs for patients assigned to HFMS was higher than for patients

assigned to SC, the median costs for most of the individual cost components were lower. The pattern for total medical costs was somewhat similar: mean total costs were significantly higher for patients randomized to HFMS compared with those randomized to SC, whereas the mean medical costs of 2 of 8 component costs were higher, but not significantly so.

# Multivariate Regression Results
We examined the effect of treatment assignment in a regression model that included gender, race, heart failure classification, body mass index (BMI), whether the patient died within the 6-month interval, pathway into the study, and recruitment site.

The Medicare costs of patients randomized to HFMS were about 49% higher than the costs of the patients randomized to SC (Table 3). The Medicare costs of patients with New York Heart Association (NYHA) Class III were 57% higher than the costs for patients with NYHA Class II. The Medicare costs of patients who were recruited immediately from hospital discharge were 54% higher than the Medicare costs of patients who were recruited from a physician’s office. Finally, the Medicare costs of patients recruited from Miami were about 66% higher than the costs of patients who were recruited in Pittsburgh, approaching a significance level of 5%. There were no statistical differences in the Medicare costs between patients who were recruited from Pittsburgh and those who were recruited from Cleveland. The BMI effect was not significant.

The total costs of treating subjects randomized to HFMS were 33% higher than the costs of subjects randomized to

SC (Table 4). This result was statistically significant. One reason that there was such a difference between the relationship between HFMS on total costs and on Medicare costs was because drug costs were not included in Medicare costs. Drug costs were lower (although not significantly lower) for patients assigned to HFMS than for patients assign to SC (Table 1). The other variables that had a statistically significant effect on the costs of treating patients with heart failure were NYHA classification (subjects with NYHA Class III were 48% more costly than those with NYHA Class II); patients who were recruited at hospital discharge were 42% more expensive than those who were recruited from physician offices, and patients who were recruited from Miami

were about 55% more expensive than those recruited from Pittsburgh. There was no difference in costs between the patients recruited from Pittsburgh and Cleveland. Again, there was no significant effect from BMI.
