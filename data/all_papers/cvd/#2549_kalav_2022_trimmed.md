## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# 2 | METHODS
## 2.1 | Aim

This study aims at evaluating the effect of CCM-based interventions on primary outcomes (self-efficacy, QoL, patient satisfaction) and secondary outcomes (ADL, metabolic control variables, stroke knowledge level, healthy eating habits, physical activity, taking regular medication, measuring blood pressure regularly, living in harmony with the disease, outcomes regarding telephone interviews) in patients with ischemic stroke.

## 2.2 | Design

The research is a monocenter, single-blinded randomized controlled experimental study. In the study, the

StrokeCARE intervention protocol was applied (Figure 1). An acronym named “StrokeCARE” was created to keep the interventions, which were performed within the scope of the protocol, easily in mind and to be expressed easily.

The interventions were specific to the four components of the CCM (Figure 2). Interventions specific to the selfmanagement support component were assisted with the implementations suggested within the scope of the 5A methodology (Morais et al., 2015). A randomized controlled trial based on the Consolidated Standard of Reporting Trials (CONSORT 2010: Updated guidelines for reporting parallel group randomized trials) was performed.

## 2.3 | Setting and participants

The research included all the ischemic stroke patients treated as in-patients in the neurology clinic of Akdeniz University Hospital and meeting the sampling criteria. Participants were recruited between September 2018 – September 2019 (N = 68). The inclusion criteria were as follows: meeting the criteria of the TOAST classification of ischemic stroke; having space, time, person orientation; receiving the scores of “0, 1, 2, 3” according to the Modified Rankin Scale (mRS) during discharge; 18 years

old and older; diagnosed with ischemic stroke as a result of computed tomography (CT) scan and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for the first time; literate; able to use a phone; no disability of verbal communication; agreeing on participating in the study. The exclusion criteria were as follows: diagnosed with a psychiatric disorder; having advanced liver and kidney disease; having malignancy or another neurological disorder; not allowing for access to their information. The criteria of exclusion from follow-up during the study were as follows: choosing to leave the study; not reached for at least four phone calls.

## 2.4 | Preparation of the education booklet

The education booklet to be used within the scope of the StrokeCARE intervention protocol was prepared through the sources of the current literature and international stroke associations according to the principles of the CCM self-management support component (American

Stroke Association; National Institutes of Health; UPMC Stroke Institute). The booklet contains general information about stroke, ischemic stroke risk factors, suggestions for controlling modifiable risk factors, and information related to the management of the frequent symptoms of a stroke. The quality of the booklet was evaluated according to the DISCERN measurement tool and experts' opinions. As a result of Kendall's W, expert opinions were found to be consistent with each other ( \mathrm { W } ^ { \mathrm { a } } = 0 . 6 5 4 ; p < . 0 0 1 ).

## 2.5 | Intervention group

The first interview with the patients, applying the measurement tools to obtain baseline data, and providing discharge education were held face-to-face before patients were discharged from the clinic. The measurement tools were applied to the patients for 20 min. During the implementation of the measurement tools, the education subjects needed primarily by the patients were determined considering the responses of the patients to the questions. Before discharge, discharge education was given to the patients for 30–45 min with the booklet which was prepared based on the CCM and contained information and suggestions related to self-management strategies. Following the education, the education booklet and blood pressure follow-up form were given to the patients.

Telephone follow-ups and sending reminder messages were performed after discharge. Patients were followed up by telephone in line with the education booklet in the 1st, 2nd, 4th, and 8th weeks after the discharge. During the telephone interview, the patients were asked questions within the scope of the ASK component of the 5A methodology. Within the scope of the other components of the 5A methodology, the patients' general health status and behavioral changes regarding the risk factors were discussed, and recommendations were given when necessary. The phone calls took 15–20 min. In unexpected/ unpredictable situations developing within the 12-week period, the neurologist was consulted, or patients were directed to outpatient clinics in case of requirement. Short reminder messages related to self-management strategies were sent to the patients seven times in total within 12 weeks in order to help them with their education. Giving one-to-one education at the clinic, telephone follow-ups after the discharge, and sending short reminder messages were carried out by the researcher who had previous experience of working in an intensive care unit and at the same time had been teaching in a nursing faculty for at least 6 years.

When the patients came to the outpatient clinic check-ups at the end of the 12-week period, the initial scales were re-applied face-to-face. The final evaluation

of the patients who did not keep the outpatient appointment was made face-to-face in a suitable environment within the hospital.

The data related to the metabolic control variables of the patients at the end of the 12-week period and at the baseline were obtained from the hospital electronic registration system by the researcher. Blood pressure measurement was evaluated by the researcher at the baseline and at the end of the 12-week period.

## 2.6 | Control group

There were no interventions in the control group during the study, and routine patient care was carried out. Routine patient care included standard hospital care given to all of the patients with ischemic stroke in the clinic during their stay in the hospital. There were no educational interventions or other services for patients within the scope of standard hospital care. The data related to the metabolic control variables of the patients at the end of the 12-week period and at the baseline were obtained from the hospital electronic registration system by the researcher.

## 2.7 | Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated by using the Danielsoper statistics program. While the sampling size was calculated in this study, the calculations were made based on the “Stroke Self-Efficacy Questionnaire”, which was also used in another similar study and as the dependent variable in our study (Topcu, 2017). “Cohen's d ^ { \ast }was found to be 0.728 using the mean values and SD of the intervention group in a similar study. According to the result of the calculation based on the 80% power and 0.05 error margin as well as the effect size obtained from a similar study, the sampling size was found to be 62 patients for our study (HyLown Consulting, 2017; The Digital Home of Dr. Daniel Soper, 2017). However, considering the losses, the number of patients included in the sampling was 10% higher than the calculated value, and it was planned to include 68 people with ischemic stroke (34 intervention, 34 control) in the study. In repeatedly measured data, the effect size is different from Cohen's d calculated for the t test, and it is calculated with the eta square ( \acute { \boldsymbol { \eta } } ^ { 2 } ). The effect size is interpreted as follows: \dot { \eta } ^ { 2 } < 0 . 0 1points at a very small effect size, \dot { \boldsymbol { \eta } } ^ { 2 } = 0 . 0 1 - 0 . 0 6indicates a small effect size, \dot { \eta } ^ { 2 } = 0 . 0 6 \ – 0 . 1 4refers to a moderate effect size, and \dot { \eta } ^ { 2 } > 0 . 1 4signifies a big effect size (Cohen, 1988). As a result of statistical power analysis, effect sizes were found within the range of 0.01–0.21.

## 2.8 | Randomization

Simple randomization was applied as the randomization method. An independent, blind statistician who was not in the research process created the randomization list using a website (https://www.randomizer.org/) in a computer environment. Patients eligible for inclusion criteria were determined at each clinical visit by evaluating the patients' MRI/CT results from the electronic patient records and according to mRS by the researcher who carried out the education and telephone follow-up interventions. The situations in which patients in the intervention and control groups shared the same room were rare. In such cases, patient interviews were held at different times and in a separate empty room in the clinic. Allocation concealment was not possible as the intervention involved education and telephone followups that were performed by the researcher. The analyses were carried out by an expert statistician. The researchers were not involved in the data analysis. Participants did not know the study section they were included in. Participants were also unaware of the study hypotheses and were blinded by not being informed about the hypotheses or objectives of the study.

# 2.9 | Outcome measures
Regarding the demographic characteristics of the patients, information such as age, gender, marital status, educational status, level of income, working status, people with whom they lived together, other chronic diseases, alcohol consumption-smoking, and need for care support was obtained from patients and patients' files.

In our study, the primary outcomes (self-efficacy, QoL, patient satisfaction) were evaluated using the Stroke Self-Efficacy Questionnaire (SSEQ), The Stroke Specific Quality of Life Scale (SSQoL), and the Patient Assessment of Chronic Illness Care (PACIC).

The SSEQ consists of 13-item and evaluates stroke survivors' daily functional activities and self-management. Each item is scored on a four-point scale (zero “not at all confident” to three “very confident”). The score that can be obtained from the questionnaire is between zero and 39 (Jones et al., 2008; Topcu & Oguz, 2018). A higher score shows a higher self-efficacy. In our study, Cronbach's alpha value was found as .86 for the SSEQ.

To evaluate the QoL, the SSQoL was used. The original scale consists of 49 items and 12 domains. Each item is ranked on a five-point Likert scale (one “completely agreed” to five “completely disagree”). It was converted into 48 items during the adaptation process to the

Turkish population. The scale items were aggregated under similar subgroups as in the original scale. The subdomains of the Turkish version of the scale are activities, social and family roles, language, vision, energy, mood, personality and thinking. Higher scores indicate better functioning. The domain scores are composed of unweighted averages. The summary score is composed of an unweighted average of all domain scores. The increase in scale scores indicates that individuals with chronic diseases have a high level of satisfaction with the care they receive (Hakverdioglu & Khorshid, 2012; Williams et al., 1999). Cronbach's alpha value was found as .96 in our research.

Patient satisfaction measurement was evaluated via the PACIC, a short 20-item tool. The 20 items were aggregated into five a priori scales based on the key components of the CCM. These subscales are patient activation, decision support, goal setting, problem solving, follow-up. Each scale is scored by averaging the items completed within that scale, and the overall PACIC is scored by averaging scores across all 20 items. Each item is evaluated on a five-point Likert scale (one “never” to five “always”) (Improving Chronic Illness Care [ICIC], 2021). The increase in scale scores indicates that individuals with chronic diseases have a high level of satisfaction with the care they receive (Glasgow et al., 2005; ICIC, 2021; Incirkus & Nahcivan, 2011). Cronbach's alpha value was found as .95 in our research.

In our study, Modified Barthel Index (MBI) was used to evaluate ADL, which is one of the secondary outcomes. The MBI consists of 10 items including transfer, mobility, stairs, feeding, dressing, grooming, bathing, toilet use, bladder, and bowels. Each item is scored on a five-point scale. The total score ranges from zero to 100, with “0” indicating full dependency and “100” indicating full independence (Kucukdeveci et al., 2000; Shah et al., 1989). In our study, Cronbach's alpha value was found as .90 for MBI. Some laboratory values and blood pressure were monitored within the context of metabolic variables. Laboratory findings were obtained from the hospital electronic registration system. The other secondary outcomes (stroke knowledge level, healthy eating habits, physical activity, taking regular medication, measuring blood pressure regularly, and living in harmony with the disease) were obtained using the Stroke Self-Management Evaluation Form. Telephone Interviews Evaluation Form was created to use during the telephone interviews.

Primary and secondary outcomes were evaluated at the baseline and at the end of the 12-week period. Patient satisfaction was evaluated in both groups only at the end of the 12 weeks.

## 2.10 | Data collection

The research data were collected between September 1, 2018 and September 1, 2019. Within the scope of the

StrokeCARE intervention protocol, patients in the intervention group were followed up with four telephone calls and seven short messages within the 12-week period. The flowchart of the study is given in Figure 3.

## 2.11 | Validity and reliability

To increase the validity and reliability of the data, measurement tools confirmed to have good reliability were used. To support the SSEQ, which is one of these tools, the Stroke Self-Management Evaluation Form was prepared considering the information of the sources of the current literature and international stroke associations. Education sessions were held for each individual in the intervention group by using the standard duration and teaching methods.

## 2.12 | Ethical considerations

Ethics committee approval was obtained from Akdeniz University Clinical Research Ethics Committee. An informed consent form was applied to inform the patients in the intervention and control groups who were included in the research and to obtain their consent. It was stated verbally and in writing that the information obtained at the end of the research would not be used anywhere other than the research report, and individuals could leave the research at any time. The privacy of the participants was protected.

## 2.13 | Data analysis

All the data were entered, checked for missing values, and analyzed using SAS version 9.4 statistical software. The numeric results were expressed as mean ± SD, and categorical results were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test. Variables were tested for normal distribution using a one-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test to use a parametric or non-parametric test. All the variables were normally distributed and therefore parametric statistical methods were used. Comparisons of the differences between scores at the end of the 12 weeks and at the baseline were performed between intervention and control groups using an independent samples t test for all the variables. According to this, changes in scores between the two time points regarding SSEQ, SSQoL, MBI, and metabolic control variables were calculated for each group, and then the mean changes were compared between the two groups. An intention to treat analysis was performed due to the loss of data in the intervention and control groups. Patient satisfaction was evaluated in both groups only at the end of the 12 weeks by using the independent Student t test (two samples t test). Expert opinions were acquired for the content of the educational booklet, and Kendall's coefficient of concordance was

calculated. Cronbach's alpha coefficient was used for the reliability of the scales. A two-sided probability value of <.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

It was discovered that the patients in the intervention and control groups included in the research were statistically similar in terms of demographic and disease-related characteristics at baseline (p > .05), and the patients were distributed homogeneously into the intervention and control groups (Table 1).

Regarding self-efficacy, the SSEQ scores of the patients in the intervention and the control groups were compared in terms of the mean changes between the scores at the end of the 12 weeks and at the baseline. # 4.1 | Primary outcomes
In the literature, it was stated that the self-management empowerment interventions increased the self-efficacy

levels and/or self-management behavioral performance of the patients (Chen et al., 2018; Lo et al., 2018; Sit et al., 2016; Topcu, 2017). In our study, we found that the StrokeCARE intervention protocol had no significant effect on increasing self-efficacy in 12 weeks. In similar studies in which stroke self-management programs were applied, no significant difference was observed between the two groups, which was similar to our study results (Damush et al., 2016; McKenna et al., 2015). The fact that the stroke patients included in our research were either independent or needed a little help and the research duration was short is thought to have possibly affected

the self-efficacy results. The inclusion of the patients who received 0–3 points as per the mRS in the study can be thought as the reason for the absence of significant changes. Receiving 0–2 points from mRS indicates that the severity of stroke is low, and the patients are independent or need a little help. The majority of the patients received 0–2 points from mRS in our study.

No statistically significant difference was found between the intervention and control groups in terms of the mean changes between the QoL scores at the end of the 12 weeks and at baseline. In a similar study, no significant difference was detected between the groups in terms of health-related QoL, which was similar to our study results (Damush et al., 2016). In other studies in which self-management interventions were performed in stroke, there was a significant increase in QoL mean total scores in the intervention group compared to the control group (Iltus, 2018; Topcu, 2017). A recent study showed that the most important factors affecting QoL were the functional state, depression and anxiety after ischemic stroke (Jarosławski et al., 2020). Also, a present study described an inversely proportional relationship between the severity of the stroke, disability and QoL (Ramos-Lima et al., 2018). In our research, since the individuals included in the study were mostly unaffected by stroke severity and consisted of fully independent individuals, they could sustain their daily living activities. The patients reported that they experienced some negative changes in their social and family roles, work-life and psychological conditions due to an average of 15 days of hospitalization. An insignificant slight increase was observed in the mood subscale score of the patients in the intervention group at the end of the 12-week period in our study. Another study found that impairment in health-related QoL was common at 3 months after minor ischemic stroke (Sangha et al., 2015). Our study results highlighted that the effect of stroke on QoL should be monitored for more than 12 weeks.

In our study, the mean scores of the patients' carerelated satisfaction levels in the intervention group were found significantly higher than the control group at the end of the 12 weeks. It was discovered that the majority of the patients in the intervention group were satisfied with the education booklet, telephone follow-up, and short message service. In similar studies, there was a significant improvement in satisfaction levels of the patients regarding treatment and care services, ability to perform ADL or self-management behavioral performances (Arts et al., 2008; Chumbler et al., 2015; Eames et al., 2013; Lo et al., 2018). It is thought that interventions performed within the scope of the CCM in patients with ischemic stroke contribute to increasing their level of knowledge related to the disease.

# 4.2 | Secondary outcomes
In our research, the control group had more improvement in ADL than the intervention group at the end of the 12 weeks. As a result of a CCM-based study on stroke patients, it was found that post-discharge care management interventions did not have a significant superiority over the functional results (K. Allen et al., 2009). In studies where self-management empowerment intervention was applied, unlike the result of our study, a significant increase was detected in the realization of ADL by the patients in the intervention group and their functional recovery levels (Chen et al., 2018; Geng et al., 2019; Sit et al., 2016). It is thought that the level of recovery in stroke may change according to the patient and may require a process although it is fast in the first weeks. It has been emphasized that patients severely affected by stroke are less likely to receive appropriate secondary prevention than those who are mildly affected by stroke (Rudd et al., 2004). So, independent or slightly dependent patients were included in our study. The mean MBI total scores of the intervention and control groups at baseline were 9 6 . 5 6 \pm 6 . 9 6and 8 9 . 0 9 \pm 1 2 . 4 0, respectively. At the end of the 12-week period, these values were 9 8 . 8 7 \pm 3 . 3 5and 9 8 . 1 0 \pm 3 . 7 9in the intervention and control groups, respectively. According to these results, it can be said that the level of independence was better in the intervention group at baseline and at a good level in both groups at the end of the study. The medication protocols of both groups were the same, and at the end of the 12-week period, positive feedback percentages of the patients in both groups regarding taking medicine in time were high. Also, it is stated that health literacy in stroke patients could contribute to how patients interpret symptoms, navigate care, and participate in treatment evaluation and decision-making (Tian et al., 2018). The higher improvement in ADL in the control group may be explained in this way. Because today, patients can obtain information about their diseases more easily thanks to computers or mobile phones with internet access, it may be suggested that mobile applications are added to the StrokeCARE intervention protocol.

In our study, a statistically insignificant decrease was found in LDL, total cholesterol, triglyceride, and systolic and diastolic blood pressure values. There was also a statistically insignificant increase in the HDL value of the intervention group. The studies conducted on secondary prevention and the management of the risk factors, unlike our study results, a significant decrease occurred in the LDL and/or triglyceride levels of the individuals in the intervention group (Cheng et al., 2018; Geng et al., 2019; Iltus, 2018; Spassova et al., 2016). It can take 3–6 months to reach a lower LDL level only with diet

and exercise. Also, some patients had to obey the diet provided at workplaces after they returned to their work lives following discharge, and they could not allocate time for physical activity as they had to fulfill their household responsibilities in our study. The absence of significant changes in cholesterol values in our study may be related to these reasons. Another reason for the lack of significant changes in other laboratory values in our study is the low number of patients whose laboratory values could be obtained at the end of the 12-week process. Some patients did not keep their appointments. Also, most of the laboratory findings at the end of the 12-week period were not repeated due to hospital-related reasons.

In CCM-based or non-CCM-based studies, similar to our research results, even though a decrease in systolic blood pressure was observed, no significant difference was found between the control and intervention groups (Cheng et al., 2018; Sarfo et al., 2018). However, it was reported that systolic/diastolic blood pressure of the patients in the intervention group decreased significantly in the 3–12-month studies (Geng et al., 2019; Iltus, 2018; Leistner et al., 2012; Spassova et al., 2016). In our study, at the end of the 12-week period, the frequency of patients who said “I don't measure/get it measured regularly” regarding blood pressure measurement made up the majority in both groups. It can be said that the StrokeCARE intervention protocol has no effect on improving blood pressure.

In similar studies, consistent with our study results, positive developments were found in patients' knowledge about stroke (K. Allen et al., 2009; Eames et al., 2013; Geng et al., 2019). The StrokeCARE intervention can increase the awareness of patients correlated with stroke risk factors, and recurrent strokes can be reduced by including self-management programs in the health system.

It is important in secondary prevention to identify unhealthy nutritional behaviors together with the patients and to change these behaviors by setting targets. It is assumed that frequent discussion of the suggestions for patients' development of healthy eating habits in the CCM-based telephone follow-up and short reminder messages sent about the topic may have a positive effect on patients' compliance with their diets.

In similar studies, consistent with our study results, no statistically significant difference was detected between the intervention and control groups in terms of physical activity and exercise (Iltus, 2018; Wan et al., 2016), but in one of the studies, unlike our results, a significant increase was discovered in the physical activity level of the patients in the intervention group (Iltus, 2018). It is believed that encouraging patients for

physical activity and short reminder messages sent on the subject in the the CCM-based follow-ups had a positive effect on patients' physical activity behaviors; however, behavioral changes need to be observed for a longer time.

For the CCM-based interventions to be effective, studies involving a multidisciplinary team with a longer follow-up period are considered necessary. It is considered important to plan interventions such as telehealth, video-based education, or stroke health education by using mobile applications to improve stroke literacy and self-management skills within the scope of CCM-based secondary prevention programs in future studies. Including patients with severe stroke and their families in these studies may be useful in evaluating the effectiveness of the model.

## 4.3 | Limitations

In the research, 12-week follow-up findings were evaluated to examine the effects of the interventions based on self-management strategies on primary outcomes and secondary outcomes. The research was limited to patients who have had an ischemic stroke in a single institution. Most of the patients' initial laboratory findings were not repeated at the end of the 12-week period due to hospital procedures. The absence of a tool with proven validityreliability in Turkish for the evaluation of selfmanagement was a limitation. A multidisciplinary team and a hospital-based system were needed to handle all the components of the CCM. Another limitation of the study was the inclusion of individuals who could use their phones to answer calls in the post-discharge period and whose speaking skills were not affected.

## 5 | CONCLUSIONS

It was observed that the StrokeCARE intervention protocol increased patients' knowledge levels about stroke and raised awareness in terms of the risk of secondary stroke. However, these interventions did not have a significant effect on the values of the patients' ADL and metabolic control variables and partly contributed to the empowerment of the healthy living behaviors. It was discovered that CCM-based implementations did not improve the QoL and self-efficacy in ischemic stroke patients but had an effect on increasing patients' satisfaction. It is believed that strategies determined according to the components of the CCM increased the patients' satisfaction levels. It was determined that the CCM is especially applicable in line with the components of “self-management support, decision support, delivery system design and clinical

information systems” in patients suffering from ischemic stroke. However, studies carried out by a multidisciplinary team with longer follow-up and integrating the model into the hospital system are needed. More research based on the CCM, including patients with severe stroke, is also needed to empower selfmanagement.
