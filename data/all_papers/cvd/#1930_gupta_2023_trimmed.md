## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# Methods
We conducted a pragmatic single-centre pilot study to assess critical implementation outcomes needed to scale up the hypertension programme (NCT04585880). The study was approved by the institutional review board.

## Patient identification and recruitment

Patients aged ≥65 years with uncontrolled hypertension were enrolled from six PCP clinics for a 3-month duration. Uncontrolled hypertension was defined as (i) SBP >140 mmHg at current visit and documented history of hypertension, (ii) SBP >140 mmHg at current visit and at another visit in the last 18 months, or (iii) SBP >160 mmHg at current visit. BP measured at an emergency department, inpatient, or surgical visit was not considered in determining eligibility as these readings are less likely to reflect usual BP control. We chose an age cut-off >65 years as it is the age of Medicare enrolment and several outcomes for the larger study will be obtained from the Medicare data. Moreover, the reduction in cardiovascular events from BP lowering is likely to be more evident in older individuals. Using these criteria our EHR

Reporting Workbench (Epic Systems, Verona, WI) identified potentially eligible patients and referred them at the point of care (i.e. in real-time) to the study team for pre-screening and enrolment. We applied broad inclusion criteria to recruit men and women, all racial and ethnic minorities, patients with multiple comorbidities, frailty, mobility limitations, and very old adults who are often excluded from explanatory studies. Patients with illness that could affect the safety or study completion, such as unstable psychiatric disorder, mental retardation, or other reasons that make them unable to sign their own consent, end-stage kidney disease on dialysis, or expected life expectancy <2 years were excluded. The study was designed to be pragmatic (Supplementary Table 1) to allow transition to the real-world clinics. Clinical pharmacists from the PCP team delivered the intervention with flexible protocols. Phone visits reduced patient burden while continuing frequent contact. Potentially eligible patients were identified by the EHR. We used the EHR for initial patient identification. However, since all exclusion criteria couldn’t be done through the EHR, we had study coordinators screen and determine eligibility.

Potentially eligible patients were contacted within 2 weeks of their primary care visit to determine eligibility. These eligibility criteria included access to a compatible smartphone or other devices, such as IOS, Android, or Amazon Kindle or Fire devices, sufficient fluency in English, adequate vision and hearing to complete study procedures, and interest). Informed consent was obtained remotely.

## Intervention

All participants received an FDA-approved electronic BP cuff (QardioArm, Qardio Inc.) and educational material on hypertension in preparation for the baseline visit. During the baseline visit, study coordinators instructed patients on use of the BP cuff and the correct method of BP measurement. After a 5-min rest, three standardized BP measures, 1 min apart were obtained. If a BP reading was different by ≥20 mmHg from other two readings, it was discarded, and a fourth reading was taken to replace it. A symptom checklist and patient surveys were also obtained.

After the baseline visit, participants were randomized to either a control group (usual care with education) or the vCCC group (Supplementary Fig. 1). Randomization was done remotely. The study coordinator randomized the participant in the REDCap database (randomization prepared by the study statisticians) while the participant was still on the phone and provided them with instructions on how to proceed based on the study arm they were randomized to usual care with education or the virtual collaborative care clinic arm.

## Virtual collaborative care clinic

The vCCC operated as an extension (not replacement) of care provided by PCPs. Trained, board-certified, or board-eligible ambulatory care clinical pharmacists already working for the health system staffed the vCCC. The vCCC pharmacists managed hypertension. They educated patients on home blood pressure monitoring (HBPM), monitored home BP readings, adherence, and medication side effects. They used the EHR to communicate with physicians, order laboratory tests, e-prescribe medications, or make medication changes. To monitor longitudinal home BP readings, vCCC pharmacists used a BP dashboard (Qardio Inc) and a patient portal (Epic MyChart) and/or phone calls to communicate with patients. The BP dashboard used a traffic light system to organize and prioritize uncontrolled BP and individualize BP goals and alerts. The vCCC pharmacists communicated at least once a month with patients and as often as required. If a patient did not check BP regularly, pharmacists contacted the patient to address barriers to adherence.

For patients randomized to the vCCC group, the vCCC pharmacist conducted a 15-min introductory phone call, reviewed current medications and BP history, and encouraged regular HBPM (at least 10 times a week, 5 in the morning and 5 in the evening). The control group was not contacted by the vCCC and continued regular follow-up with their physicians.

The primary outcomes were programme feasibility and acceptability. Other outcomes included barrier and facilitator identification, safety, frequency of HBPM, and change in BP. Acceptability and feasibility of the programme were evaluated by assessing five programme components: automated referral, HBPM, pharmacist management, physician involvement, and the BP cuff. Only patients randomized to the

vCCC rated pharmacist management and automated referral. Validated, 4-item scales: the Acceptability of Intervention Measure and the Feasibility of Intervention Measure were used.26 Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from “Strongly Disagree” to “Strongly Agree”. A minimum threshold of 4.0 on the Acceptability and Feasibility scales was established a priori to demonstrate acceptability and feasibility, a lower bound used in other intervention assessments.27 Surveys included open-ended items. Patients evaluated programme components at the end-of-study visit while physicians and pharmacists evaluated them before enrolment and at 3 months after enrolment of the first patient. We used the Tailored Implementation for Chronic Disease (TICD) framework28 for determinant evaluation. Like other determinant frameworks, the TICD assesses motivational, organizational, and capability domains, but also includes attributes of the intervention and focuses on guideline-based clinical care delivery. Safety was assessed by comparing the symptom checklists obtained at baseline and 3 months and reporting any new symptom or event as an adverse event. Frequencies of HBPM and home BP readings were monitored through the Qardio app.

## Data collection

BP readings were obtained through the Qardio app. Demographic information (age, sex, race, and ethnicity), medical history (heart rate, BMI, and comorbidities), antihypertensive medications, laboratory values, and clinicmeasured BP were obtained from the EHR. The number of anti-hypertensive medications per drug class was summarized. Survey ratings were collected via a Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) electronic survey delivered by email.

Study coordinators administered the surveys via telephone to any patients who did not complete the survey electronically.

## Physician advisory board

We selected respected, experienced clinical leaders in the Departments of Internal Medicine and Family Medicine with credibility, commitment to BP control, quality improvement skills, and institutional linkages to participate in the pilot study. The physician advisory board (PAB) was initiated prior to patient enrolment and met monthly for the study duration. The PAB discussed study design, feedback from stakeholders, and potential adaptations to the programme in structured focus groups.

We conducted semi-structured interviews of participating pharmacists to optimize the intervention components and identify potential barriers and facilitators to implementation. Two pharmacists with greatest experience using vCCC were selected to provide in-depth feedback. Both physician and pharmacist interviews were conducted by an experienced interviewer (SDE) via Zoom. Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim.

## Analysis

Descriptive statistics (frequencies, means, and standard deviations) were calculated for baseline characteristics, including baseline demographics, medical history, use of antihypertensive medications, and laboratory results. Descriptive statistics were also calculated for home BP readings at baseline and 12 weeks and change in BP from baseline. Mean BP values at baseline and 12 weeks within each treatment arm were compared with paired t-tests. Additionally, the differences in the average weekly number of BP readings between the two treatment arms were compared using a t-test. For the acceptability and feasibility measures, summary scores were calculated for each treatment arm using the average of the responses within each survey component and compared using t-tests (patient scores) and paired t-tests (provider scores).

Qualitative data were coded using a combination of inductive coding and template analysis. One coder identified emerging themes among patient open-ended survey item responses and compared themes between patients randomized to the vCCC versus usual care. Qualitative data collected from pharmacists and physicians were coded to TICD domains. Data were combined, and barriers were assessed for potential intervention or study execution changes. Coded themes were reviewed by a second investigator and revised until consensus was reached. Potential changes were reported to the study team and evaluated for salience by the PAB before adaptations were made, resulting in a set of key recommendations for action.

We adapted procedures so that all study operations, including recruitment, study visits, vCCC visits, PAB, and investigator meetings were done remotely for the safety of the patients, providers, and study staff during the COVID-19 pandemic.

# Results
Six physicians (one from a community-based clinic, three from academic Family Medicine clinic, and two from academic Internal Medicine clinic) and five clinical pharmacists

participated in the pilot study. Physicians were experienced (number of years in practice 1 3 . 3 \pm 7 . 6 9 ), saw an average of 9 ± 0.71 patients per half day clinic, and had an average of 4.4 ± 2.30 clinics per week. All pharmacists were postgraduate year 2 ambulatory care residency trained.

During the ~4.5-month recruitment period (from October 2020 to February 2021), the six physicians saw 994 patients who were 65 years or older (Supplementary Fig. 2). The EHR Research Workbench identified 154 as potentially eligible based on BP and key demographic characteristics. Study staff review of the EHR found 62 of them to be ineligible (Supplementary Fig. 2) for various reasons, such as active cancer, decompensated cirrhosis, cirrhosis, significant grief from the loss of spouse with inability to complete examination, expressive aphasia, dementia, chronic hypoxic respiratory failure with significant weight loss, planned surgical procedures and memory problems due to stroke. Of the remaining 93, 42 declined participation, and 18 were not reachable. The study enrolled 32 patients; 1 patient did not get randomized as they could not complete baseline visit due to technical problems with the BP cuff; the remaining 31 patients completed the study, 17 in the vCCC arm and 14 in usual care arm.

Supplementary Table 2 compares baseline patient characteristics by arm. Patients in the vCCC arm had a decrease in average BP from baseline to 12-week visit ( P \ = \ 0 . 0 1 ) ., but patients in usual care arm did not (P = 0.45) (Fig. 1A). Participants in the vCCC arm self-measured BP more often than those in usual care (9.9 vs. 1.2 per week, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1B). Eleven adverse events were noted, 6 in the control group and 5 in the vCCC group. None were related to the intervention (Supplementary Table 4).

All participating patients (n = 31), PCPs (n = 6), and pharmacists (n = 5) evaluated programme components. Patients reported HBPM, the BP cuff, pharmacist management, physician involvement, and automated referral to be acceptable and feasible at the pre-specified level for demonstrating feasibility and acceptability (Fig. 2). Ratings did not differ statistically between study arm, except for patients’ perceptions of physician involvement (mean score 4.2 in vCCC vs. 3.3 in controls, P = 0.007).

Physicians and pharmacists rated HBPM, physician involvement, pharmacist management, and the automatic referral process as feasible and acceptable at baseline and 3 months at the pre-specified level, with scores trending upward over the study period (Fig. 3). For providers who responded to the survey at both timepoints (n = 9) mean ratings of five attributes increased with more experience in the programme, acceptability of HBPM (P = 0.04), acceptability of physician involvement (P = 0.007), acceptability of automatic referral (P = 0.03), feasibility of physician involvement (P = 0.006), and feasibility of automatic referral (P = 0.02).

Among PAB and pharmacist interviews, we identified 15 potential barriers to implementing the hypertension programme and 9 potential facilitators across TICD domains. Adaptations in vCCC design and delivery were proposed to address barriers and aligned them with facilitators. The resulting nine key adaptations made to overcome the barriers are listed in Table 2. Most adaptations involved reframing or emphasizing existing key components of the programme in introductory and training materials, including an emphasis on co-management, rather than relinquishment of patient

care to vCCC pharmacists; programme flexibility allowing the vCCC pharmacist to treat hypertension per physicians’ BP goals and medication preference; ensuring prompt call by the vCCC pharmacists after patients randomization to vCCC; and the role of the vCCC to reduce burden for both physicians and pharmacists. We changed the EHR pathway to access vCCC, improved the pharmacists reporting template, added a large BP cuff, and instituted a clinic staff notification campaign. To motivate patients to participate, we provided physicians with an EHR “smartphrase” informing patients about the programme to add to patients’ visit summary, and added information about hypertension, correct method of

measuring BP, and the risks of uncontrolled hypertension to patient education materials.

Qualitative results from patients were consistent with programme component ratings. Patient participants, regardless of study arm, liked the BP device and described relative advantage over other home BP devices, but did note the device’s short battery life (Supplementary Table 3). Participants valued additional pharmacist involvement over usual physician care, which they also appreciated. An attribute mentioned de novo among qualitative responses was the programme’s comprehensiveness. The vCCC participants noted positive benefits, including increased knowledge, better BP management, and control.
