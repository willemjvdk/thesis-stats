ABSTRACT. This trial compared 3 post-hospitalization nursing care models for reducing congestive heart failure (CHF) readmission charges during 180-days of follow-up. Subjects received in-person visits at baseline and 60 days plus 1 of 3 care modalities in the interim: (a) video-based home telecare; (b) telephone calls; and (c) usual care. CHF-related readmission charges were > 80% lower in the telenursing groups compared to usual care, and these groups also had significantly fewer CHF-related emergency visits. In-person visits were more than 3 times longer than telenursing visits (p < 0.0001), only partially due to added travel time. Patient self-care adherence, medications, health status, and satisfaction did not significantly differ between groups. Telenursing can reduce CHF hospitalizations and allow increased frequency of communication with patients. Recent changes in the home health care industry have resulted in the need for home care agencies to implement and evaluate new methods of monitoring patients with chronic diseases and conditions. Such changes include the worsening shortage of home nurses; earlier discharge of patients from the hospital, resulting in a “sicker” and less stable home nursing population; and pressure for home care agencies to work at maximal efficiency, particularly since the advent of the Prospective Payment System (PPS) for home health care (Health Care Financing Administration Press Office, 2000). Home telecare, the use of distance communications technologies by nurses or other ancillary providers to delivery health care services (Bashshur, 2000), holds great promise for overcoming these difficulties. In common usage, most people apply the term “home telecare” to real-time video interactions between patients and providers, using the term “distance monitoring” for other potentially valuable but less intensive approaches, such as store-and-forward transmission of data (e.g., glucometer readings). Employing home telecare to conduct home monitoring obviously allows more informa-

tion to be collected than is possible with standard telephone calls and may therefore result in a closer approximation of traditional home nursing care than is possible using the telephone. However, while home telecare has already been employed by many agencies (Miller, 1995; Kinsella & Warner, 1998; Jerant, Schlachta, Epperly, & Barnes-Camp, 1998; Mahmud & Lenz, 1995; Wheeler, 1998), until recently no rigorous evaluations of its impact on patient-centered outcomes such as health status, satisfaction with care, and health care utilization had been conducted. A particularly pressing question, given the relatively high cost of home telecare devices, is whether the ability to collect video information using such devices results in corresponding incremental improvements in patient outcomes as compared to telephone monitoring. This question is particularly salient since greater use of either home telecare or telephone monitoring would likely allow an increase in the work efficiency of home nurses. Since there is no need for physical travel to patients’ homes when either telecare or telephone calls are employed, the “windshield time” that has traditionally consumed a substantial proportion of a home nurse’s day (Hedtcke, MacQueen, & Carr, 1992) can be reallocated to direct patient care.

In a recent manuscript, the results of the first randomized trial of a video-based home telecare intervention were reported (Jerant, Azari, & Nesbitt, 2001). The one-year trial sought to begin to clarify the question of whether home telecare “adds value” to standard telephone monitoring by comparing the effectiveness of three post-hospital discharge care models for reducing congestive heart failure (CHF) related hospital readmission charges: (a) home telecare; (b) nurse telephone calls; and (c) usual outpatient care. CHF was targeted because it is the most common cause for hospital admission for exacerbation of a chronic illness in the United States for people aged 65 and older (Elixhauser et al., 2000) and it accounts for a high case load in home nursing agencies. In addition, although post-hospitalization follow-up of CHF patients using traditional home nurse visits had previously been shown to prevent readmissions and reduce overall cost of care (Rich, 1999; Rich et al., 1995), the intervention costs of such strategies were high, and their broader application would present substantial economic and logistical challenges to health systems. Thus, the problem of frequent CHF hospital readmissions appeared to be a promising target for evaluating the potential impact of home telecare strategies.

Patients randomized to the telecare group had an Aviva SL1010 Personal Telecare unit (American TeleCare, Eden Praire, MN) installed in their home at the initial in-person visit. The patient and, when applica-

ble, lay caregivers were instructed in its use. The units operated over standard analog telephone lines and allowed real-time videoconferencing with the study nurse at a central monitoring computer at the medical center. A small camera on an extension cable allowed observation of facial expressions, respiratory effort, lower extremity edema, and objects such as digital scale displays. A voice signal was transmitted simultaneously via a microphone. An integrated electronic stethoscope was utilized by having the patient or caregiver apply the device to standard heart and lung auscultation points. Posterior lung auscultation was omitted in patients without an assisting caregiver. Encounters were conducted in a similar fashion for patients randomized to telephone care, but without the video and electronic stethoscope additions. The study nurse estimated that 80% of the intervention protocol items could be assessed utilizing only audio information from a telephone.

In the trial, mean (SD) CHF-related readmission charges were $5850 ($21,094) for the telecare group and $7320 ($24,440) for the telephone group, 86% and 84% lower, respectively, than the mean charges of $44,470 ($121,214) for the usual care group. The difference in CHFrelated readmission charges between groups did not reach statistical significance due to the small sample size. However, both interventions did result in significantly fewer CHF-related ED visits ( p = 0 . 0 3 4 2 )) and charges ( p = 0 . \bar { 0 } 4 8 7 )compared to usual care. In evaluating the feasibility and impact of home nursing interventions, their impact on acute care utilization costs is of course only one outcome of interest. Other important outcomes to assess include subject quality of life, health status, and satisfaction with care, encounter content and duration, and nursing caseloads. The current report details the relative impact of the three types of home nursing follow-up interventions on these patientcentered outcomes and nursing content and efficiency indicators.

# METHODS
## Study Population

Between July 1, 1999 and June 30, 2000, all patients admitted to the University of California Davis (UCD) Medical Center Hospital with a primary admission diagnosis of CHF were screened for eligibility to participate in the trial. The study protocol was approved by the UCD Office of Human Research Protection. The enrollment criteria and randomization methods were reported in detail previously (Jerant, Azari, &

Nesbitt, 2001). In brief, eligible patients were aged 40 and older, had an active telephone line in their home, were English-speaking, and had a family physician or general internist primary care provider (PCP) in the UCD Health System. In addition, potential subjects (or a designated caretaker) needed to have vision and hearing adequate to utilize a telephone or telecare equipment. A research assistant contacted a physician from the admitting team to verify the primary admission diagnosis and assess potential subjects for the presence of study exclusion criteria. Patients were excluded if any of the following were present: a Charlson comorbidity score (Charlson, Pompei, Ales, & MacKenzie, 1987) of six or greater (equivalent to metastatic cancer, full-blown acquired immunodeficiency syndrome, or several chronic diseases with end-organ manifestations); a 15-item Geriatric Depression Scale score (Sheikh & Yesavage, 1986) of seven or greater; a Mini-Mental State Exam score (Folstein, Folstein, & McHugh, 1975) of 20 or lower; or a Symbol Digits Modalities Test (Smith, 1973) score of greater than or equal to two standard deviations below age- and education-adjusted mean scores. Initial CHF symptom status was determined using the New York Heart Association classification (Criteria Committee of the American Heart Association, New York City Affiliate, 1994). Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) (Katz, 1983) were also assessed and IADL, GDS, and MMSE scores were used to determine a global functional impairment index (Inouye et al., 1998).

## Randomization and Nursing Intervention

For patients who agreed to participate, informed consent was obtained and random assignment to one of the three care models was achieved prior to hospital discharge using sealed envelopes containing randomly generated numbers. Patients in all groups received an in-person home nurse visit shortly after discharge and a second in-person home nurse visit approximately 60 days later. During both visits, subjects completed the Medical Outcomes Study SF-36 generic health status questionnaire (Ware & Sherbourne, 1992) as well as the Minnesota Living with Heart Failure Questionnaire (MLHFQ), a CHF-specific measure that is sensitive to short-term changes in health status (Rector & Cohn, 1992; Rector, Kubo, & Cohn, 1993). Patient satisfaction with care was assessed at both in-person visits using the eight-item Client Satisfaction Questionnaire (CSQ) (Attkisson & Zwick, 1982).

Patients randomized to usual care received only the care directed by their PCP in the period between in-person visits. Patients assigned to

telephone care received scheduled phone calls from the study nurse in the intervening period, while those assigned to the video-based telecare group received scheduled home telecare visits using the equipment described in the Introduction section. For urgent questions or problems occurring between 8 AM and 5 PM, Monday through Friday, patients in the telephone and telecare groups had access, via the medium appropriate to their group assignment, to the study nurse. They were given emergency contact numbers for usual methods of care during all other hours. Patients in the usual care group did not have access to the study nurse beyond the initial and terminal in-person visits but were also provided with usual emergency contact numbers.

During all types of nursing encounters, the Visiting Nurse Association (VNA) CHF Care Steps protocol was used to guide patient assessment (Strategic HealthCare Programs, 1997). This protocol includes assessment of items such as vital signs, activities of daily living, coping skills, medication use, dietary factors, and degree of signs and symptoms such as dyspnea and weight gain. Patients are educated regarding each item, and patient-centered goals for the frequency and content of follow-up visits are developed. To help determine the adequacy of CHF medication regimens, the investigators developed a second set of algorithms based on national consensus recommendations (Advisory Council to Improve Outcomes Nationwide in Heart Failure, 1999) updated to include the appropriate use of potassium-sparing diuretics (Bertram et al., 1999). Following each encounter, the nurse reviewed her assessment with the principal investigator and, if appropriate, then sent a letter containing non-urgent recommendations for improving CHF care to the PCP. Urgent recommendations were also conveyed immediately by telephone.

# Outcome Measures
Health care utilization and charges were tracked for 180 days from the date of the first home nurse visit, as previously described (Jerant, Azari, & Nesbitt, 2001). The study nurse kept track of all recommendations for care or monitoring made to subjects’ PCPs, whether they were followed, and the rationale for the PCP’s decision to follow or not follow them. She also kept a log of all patient self-care teaching, which was recorded using the following categories: general CHF disease process; daily weight monitoring; sodium restriction; smoking cessation; alcohol moderation; weight loss (for obese patients); aerobic exercise; and medication use and adherence. The nurse also kept a log of the du-

ration of each visit and the proportion of visit time spent on travel, administrative tasks, and direct patient care.

## Statistical Analysis

Based on 1998-99 UCD Medical Center Hospital CHF admission rates and charges, it was estimated that a sample size of 69 (23 patients per group) would provide 80% power at a confidence level of 95% to detect a 45% difference in mean CHF-related re-admission charges between groups. Refer to the previously published report regarding health care utilization and charges for additional details of that analysis (Jerant, Azari, & Nesbitt, 2001). Categorical data analysis and contingency tables were used to compute p values for between group differences in most of the baseline demographic and CHF care variables. Analysis of variance was used to compute p values for between group differences in Charlson comorbidity score; CHF duration; weight; MLHFQ, SF-36, and CSQ scores; and visit duration. In all cases, data were examined prior to analysis to ensure that the assumptions of statistical models were satisfied using Shapiro-Wilk statistics. Given our small sample size, stratified covariate analyses were not conducted.

# RESULTS
## Demographics, Baseline Functional Status, and Comorbidity

During the recruitment period, 339 patients were admitted with a verified primary diagnosis of CHF, and 236 had at least one exclusion criteria. An additional 66 subjects who may have been eligible did not undergo detailed evaluation: 19 declined evaluation and 47 were discharged before the RA could evaluate them. The remaining 37 eligible subjects were randomized: 13 to the home telecare group and 12 each to telephone and usual care groups. One subject in the telephone group died due to a severe CHF exacerbation 41 days into the study, prior to completing the 60-day nursing intervention. A second telephone subject died from acute respiratory failure of unknown cause 149 days into the study.

Patient demographics are provided in Table 1 and baseline CHF characteristics, co-morbidity, and functional impairment are provided in Table 2. There was a trend towards significance (p = 0.0666) for the difference between groups in mean duration of CHF at the time of study

enrollment. This difference was mostly attributable to the presence of one individual in the telephone group who had had CHF for 248 months, twice as long as for the persons with the longest duration of CHF in each of the other two groups. The mean (SD) Charlson comorbidity score for subjects was 1.9 (1.2), which is equivalent to two chronic medical diseases without end-organ impairment or a single chronic disease with end-organ impairment. Functional impairment of subjects was moderate to high.

## Visit Number and Length

Details of the home nursing intervention are shown in Table 3. Two telecare subjects required a total of three additional in-person visits beyond their planned initial and final in-person visits to exchange malfunctioning telecare units. Thus, the mean number of in-person visits for the telecare group was 2.3 rather than 2.0. A third telecare subject received

one visit via telephone, also due to equipment problems. Finally, one subject in the telephone group died prior to his second in-person nurse visit, resulting in the mean number of in-person visits for that group being 1.9 rather than 2.0.

The mean total duration of in-person visits was 176 (45) minutes, about twice the mean total duration for home visits at UCD Medical Center during the study period. This duration was also more than three times longer than the mean total duration of 52 (14) minutes for telecare visits and more than five times longer than the mean total duration of 32 (10) minutes for telephone visits ( \mathrm { p } < 0 . 0 0 0 1 )). The mean round-trip travel time for in-person visits was 32 (16) minutes. Thus, only about one fourth of the greater length of in-person visits can be accounted for by travel time. The mean direct patient care time for home telecare visits of 27 (6) minutes was twice as long as the mean direct patient care time of 12 (3) minutes for telephone visits. Both these mean direct care times were substantially shorter than the mean direct care time for in-person

visits of 79 (29) minutes ( \mathrm { p } < 0 . 0 0 0 1 ). Mean administrative times were similar for telecare and telephone visits: 25 (12) and 21 (9) minutes respectively. However, again both these means were much shorter than the mean administrative time for in-person visits of 64 (27) minutes ( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 0 0 1 ).

## Visit Content and Impact on CHF Self-Management, Health Status, and Satisfaction

The study nurse provided education regarding daily weight monitoring, dietary sodium restriction, medication use and compliance, and exercise at nearly every encounter and information regarding the CHF disease process in about half of encounters (Table 3). Subjects’ primary care providers agreed to make the changes in the CHF treatment regimen recommended by the study nurse in only 44% (16/36) of instances. The most frequent suggestions were to add an angiotensin-converting

enzyme (ACE) inhibitor to the regimen (7), to increase the dose of a current loop diuretic (6), and to add a beta-blocker to the regimen (4). Table 4 provides the pre- and post-intervention status of CHF self-management indicators, and Table 5 summarizes CHF medication therapy and adherence. No statistically significant changes in therapeutic regimens or medication adherence were noted for any group at 60 days of follow-up compared with baseline. However, there were trends towards improved self-care in all groups, such as a small number of people reporting lower levels of exercise and higher levels of sodium intake. Regarding medications, only digoxin use increased somewhat in all three groups during the intervention period. Only one telephone patient was taking digoxin at baseline, and no telecare or usual patients were taking it at baseline. By contrast, 5/13 telecare subjects (38%), 5/11 telephone subjects (45%), and 5/12 usual care subjects (42%) were taking digoxin at 60 days.

Table 6 lists health status and satisfaction with care scores. All groups experienced slight reductions in MLHFQ scores, indicating less emotional and physical symptom burden due to CHF after the intervention. Similarly, SF-36 mental component scores increased slightly for all groups during the intervention, indicating slightly improved mental health status, and SF-36 physical component scores also increased slightly for the telecare and telephone groups, indicating somewhat improved physical health status. However, none of the differences between the groups at either baseline or 60 days and none of the within group pre- and post-intervention differences were statistically significant. No clinically meaningful or statistically significant changes in CSQ scores for any of the groups occurred as a result of the intervention.

## Technical Issues

At least one technical problem affected 76% (92/121) of telecare encounters. Poor video resolution was noted in 64% (77/121) of encounters, limiting the assessment of leg edema. Video problems were judged to be severe in only 4% (5/121) of encounters, while heart and lung sound resolution was inadequate in only 2% (2/121).
