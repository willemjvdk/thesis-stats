# 2. Methods
## 2.1. Study design

The European study on effectiveness and sustainability of current CR programmes in the Elderly (EU-CaRE) is an international multicentre parallel RCT. The study was conducted in accordance with the applicable legislation and guidelines. Six European cardiac institutions across five European countries participated in the EU-CaRE RCT: Isala Heart Centre (The Netherlands), Bispebjerg University Hospital, Copenhagen, Denmark (Denmark), Assistance Publique – Hopitaux ˆ de Paris (France), Bern University Hospital (Switzerland), Servizo Galego de Saúde (Spain) and Radboud University Medical Center (Netherlands). The study protocol was approved by all local ethics committees and informed written consent of all participants was obtained [15]. More information about the study protocol could be found in this publication [15].

## 2.2. Intervention

The intervention has been previously described [15]. In short, patients were given a 6-months home-based mobile CR programme program followed by a 6-months follow-up period without telemonitoring and coaching. Home-based mobile CR programme involved a homebased CR program in which patients were equipped with a

smartphone and connected heartrate belt. Patients were able to measure and record training mode, duration and intensity (determined by continuously recorded and simultaneously displayed heart rate). Subjects were instructed to exercise at a moderate intensity for at least 30 min at 5 days/week. Moderate intensity was defined as the intensity above the first ventilatory threshold determined at baseline CPET. They were contacted weekly by telephone for motivational interviewing the first month, every other week the second month and from then on monthly until completion of the home-based mobile CR programme program at six months. All researchers involved in the present study received training on motivational interviewing during the initiation visit. Physical activity patterns were discussed by telephone using a similar standardized mode of operation across study sites. After 6 months the smartphone and heart rate belt were handed in. In the subsequent six months, patients received no further coaching or feedback.

# 2.3. Outcomes
Patient assessment and measurements in both groups were performed at baseline, six and twelve months.

## 2.3.1. Physical fitness

The primary endpoint was the comparison of both groups with respect to changes in physical fitness from baseline to follow-up at six months. Physical fitness was defined as peak oxygen uptake (VO peak). All sites used the same standard operating procedures for calibration, instruction and measurements for CPET according to international guidelines in all tests within each patient. Equipment varied between sites, but identical protocols were used. Patients started with a 3-min warm-up at 5 W. Subsequently an individualized ramp protocol was performed to reach VO2peak after 8–12 min. We aimed to surpass the first ventilatory threshold and a respiratory exchange ratio > 1.1.

In case of mask intolerance, signs of mask leakage or short test duration (< 3 min) VO2peak was calculated with a validated formula using maximal workload. One patient was not able to perform a CPET on a bicycle or a treadmill, a six-minute walking test was executed to estimate VO2peak using a validated formula [16]. Raw data of all tests were analysed at the core lab (Bern) by an automated procedure on raw data files using MATLAB software (MathWorks® version 9.3, release R2017b, Natick Massachusetts, USA). Visual quality control was performed by one experienced operator and in case of doubtful quality by a second operator.

## 2.3.2. Quality of Life

QoL was assessed with the second version of the 36-item short form health survey (SF-36v2). The SF-36 score will be divided in the physical component summary (PCS) and mental component summary (MCS).

## 2.4. Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS (version 26) in the Department of Medicine & Life Sciences (Hasselt University, Hasselt, Belgium). All available data was used; no data imputation was performed for missing values. Categorical data was characterised by number and percentage. Shapiro-Wilk tests were used to assess the distribution of the data. Continuous data was characterised by mean ±

standard deviation and median. Significances of the differences between continuous variables were tested by independent sample t-test. The \chi 2test was used for comparison of categorical variables. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

## 2.5. Cost-effectiveness analysis

The costs considered for the cost-effectiveness analysis of the RCT are direct costs 1) of the mobile telemonitoring guided CR programme and 2) of the care utilisation recorded during the observation time from randomisation to the end of the study (CCU). Direct costs of CR were assigned only in the mobile CR group, and not in the control group. The costs for mobile telemonitoring guided rehabilitation programme consisted of 1) costs for the use of the heart rate monitor and the MobiHealth device and 2) personnel costs for individual coaching of the patients by telephone. For the MobiHealth device a fixed price of €638 per patient was assumed and €76 per coaching contact. As the number of actually received coaching by telephone ranged from 2 to 10 contacts in the mobile CR group, the calculated costs in mobile CR programme ranged from €790 to €1398. The majority of patients (55.6%) completed the scheduled 10 coaching contacts, so the mean cost of mobile CR programme was €1303. Information on care utilisation was collected at the scheduled patient visits (visit 1–3) in the centres and by monthly telephone calls with the patients. The data was registered as adverse events, outpatient clinic visits, or cardiac-related testing. Visits at the general practitioner were not included in the cost calculations. Events were included only if they occurred after the time of randomisation and were not interventions already planned at that time (e.g., staged procedures). After screening of all documented events, the following events were deemed cost-relevant: adverse events involving suspected or confirmed acute coronary syndrome, stroke, decompensated heart failure, or cardiac arrhythmias and bleedings requiring hospitalisation, or cardiac interventions like PCI and CABG. In addition, the following procedures were retained: computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, nuclear testing and cardioversion. Because not all centres were able to specify comparable hospital costs for each medical service due to varying organisational structures across the participating countries, the steering committee had decided to calculate costs from the perspective of the payer (usually the health care insurance). In order to get a homogeneous basis for the cost calculations of the total trial population, the steering committee decided to use the costs incurred in the Netherlands (specifically in the participating Isala center) for the primary analysis.

Costs and outcomes (utilities) were compared by calculation of the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) (ICER = costintervention group – \cos \mathrm { \tt t _ { c o n t r o l } } \mathrm { \tt ~ g r o u p } ) /(effectivenessintervention group – effectivenesscontrol group)). The incremental cost represents the difference in total average cost per patient between the intervention and control group. Outcome refers especially to the change of VO2peak and QoL (measured by Physical Component Summary Score and Mental Component Summary Score of the SF-36 questionnaire) during the follow-up period. For the calculation of confidence intervals of the ICER, 5000 bootstrap samples are generated by random sampling with replacement, independently from the two treatment groups.

# 3. Results
## 3.1. Baseline characteristics

In total, 162 patients were included in the analysis. The data of 17 patients that were lost in follow-up were excluded from this analysis. Only 26% of eligible patients who declined centre-based CR participated decided to take part in the present study.

The patients in the intervention group (n = 79) had a mean age of 72.3 years and were predominantly men (85.5%). The patients in the control group (n = 83) had a mean age of 73.3 years and were

predominantly men (78.5%). Only the presence of hypertension at baseline was significantly different between the intervention and control group ( P \ : = \ : 0 . 0 3 4 ). Table 1 provides an overview of the baseline characteristics.

## 3.2. Healthcare utilisation

The average follow-up of patients was in both groups 383 days. During the observation time, 30 patients were rehospitalised for cardiovascular reasons (fifteen in both intervention group and the control group). In the intervention group, the reasons for rehospitalisation were angina pectoris with PCI (n = 4), angina pectoris without PCI (n = 7), and because of other cardiac reasons (n = 4). In the control group, the reasons for rehospitalisation were angina pectoris with PCI ( n = 6 ), angina pectoris (n = 2), and because of decompensated heart failure (n = 3). During the observation time, 20 patients presented to the emergency unit without subsequent hospitalisation (7 in the intervention group and 13 in the control group). Table 3 gives an overview of the healthcare utilisation during the observation time.

The average cost per patient in the intervention group for the use of the mobile CR programme was €1303. The total intervention cost was therefore €102.955. ## 3.4. Cost-effectiveness

## 3.4.1. Aerobic capacity

The ICER was €1085 per 1-unit [ml/kg/min] improvement in change VO2peak between baseline and the end of the 6-month mobile CR programme. The average difference in VO2peak between baseline (T0) and the end of the 6-month mobile CR programme (T1) was significantly higher in the intervention group than in the control group ( P = 0 . 0 0 5 ). However, the total costs for the intervention group were also significantly higher (P ≤0.001).

The ICER between baseline and the end of follow-up after 12 months (T2) was €1103 per 1-unit [ml/kg/min] improvement in change VO2peak. The average difference in VO2peak between baseline and the end of follow-up after 12 months was also significantly higher in the intervention group than in the control group ( P = 0 . 0 2 3 ). However, the total costs for the intervention group were also significantly higher (P = 0.005).

The distribution of the points in the cost-effectiveness scatter plot (Fig. 1) further illustrates that the mobile CR programme for elderly was more effective but slightly more costly than no CR both between baseline and the end of the mobile CR programme and between baseline and the end of the follow-up at 12 months.
