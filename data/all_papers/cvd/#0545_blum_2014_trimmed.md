# Methods
## Sample and Procedures

From June 2001 through January 2005, 206 subjects were recruited from the heart failure services at the University of Maryland Medical Center, the Baltimore Veterans Administration Medical Center, and a number of private cardiology practices in the Baltimore/Washington DC metropolitan area. The project was approved by the University Institutional Review Board, and a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act waiver was granted to search a computerized report of heart failure admissions to identify potential subjects. Table 1 lists the inclusion and exclusion criteria. These criteria represent an effort to minimize the contribution of other chronic entities that may be associated with increased hospitalization, resource utilization, or inability to participate in the monitoring activities.

## Randomization Visit

At the randomization visit, a heart failure research nurse coordinator performed an in-depth history, medication review, chart review, brief physical examination, and the Mini-Mental Status Examination (MMSE).3 The Medical Outcomes Survey Short

Form (SF-36)4 and the Minnesota Living With Heart Failure Questionnaire (MLHF)5,6 were administered in face-to-face interviews to decrease the amount of missing data. All subjects were given written material about heart failure and self-management activities. The subjects were randomized to monitored (telemonitored) or usual-care (control) group.

## Monitoring Protocol

Remote monitoring of daily weights, blood pressure, heart rate, and 15-second heart rhythm strip was performed with the use of the Philips Electronics E-care System. Each piece of equipment had a unique unit number that was associated with a specific subject. Data transmitted wirelessly by the hub was encrypted and then decrypted by the server software when received and assigned to the database file for the subject. The transmitted data were then compared with individually assigned parameters based on the subject’s admission and subsequent evaluations. Readings that were outside these parameters were flagged for the nurse practitioner who did the monitoring. This nurse practitioner, who had extensive experience in the management of heart failure patients, contacted the subject to gather more information. If appropriate, she adjusted medications, which were usually diuretics. Compared with many earlier studies, most patients were already on target doses of evidence-based medications. There were no specific protocols regarding the management decisions, and decisions were based on the nurse practitioner’s experience and/or consultation with the subject’s cardiologist. If no flags were noted over a period of 1 month, the subjects were called just to maintain contact, provide encouragement, and answer any questions they might have.

## Follow-up

All subjects were followed until death or the end of the project on December 31, 2006. The SF-36, MLHF, and medication review were repeated at 6 months and 1 year. All patients were given ready access to routine specialized heart failure care, being provided with phone numbers and outpatient visits as clinically appropriate.

## Medicare Claims Data

Medicare Medpar and Denominator files for all subjects from June 1, 2001, to December 31, 2006, were merged with the MCCD data files for analysis to identify hospitalizations during the study period. Variables added to the data analysis included dates of death not already recorded, numbers and dates of hospitalizations, lengths of stay, and a breakdown of claims and payments by Medicare. Times were calculated from the date of randomization.

## Statistical Analysis

SPSS version 18 was used for all analysis; t tests and repeated measures analysis of variance were used for comparing normally distributed continuous variables between the usual-care and monitored groups. Chi-square was used to compare the 2 groups according to categoric variables. The Mann-Whitney U test was used for

comparing the 2 groups regarding continuous variables that were not normally distributed. Kaplan-Meier curves were used for time-to-event analysis. Initial power analysis assumed Medicare costs of \$ 123,824\pm21,250(the average Medicare cost in 1998 of patients with a diagnosis of heart failure) in the control group. With the goal enrollment of 309 subjects in each group, this resulted in power to detect a decrease of $4,724 (30%), assuming a 5 .05.

Retrospective power analysis of the control group excluding outliers showed a power to detect a decrease of $18,103 (50%).

# Results
## Sample

A total of 206 subjects signed informed consent to participate in the project (104 randomized to monitoring and 102 to usual care). Two subjects declined to continue after signing consent, and no data were collected on them, one in the usual-care group and one in the monitored group. One subject completed all of the first visit data and then declined to accept the monitoring equipment when it was delivered. These subjects were eliminated from the data analysis, leaving 203 subjects; 101 in the usual-care group and 102 in the monitored group. Over the course of the project, 94 subjects died. Table 2 compares the usual-care and monitored groups at baseline. Subjects were followed for a mean of 802 6 430 days with a range of 33e1,614 days and a median of 823 days. The mean follow-up for the usual-care group was 763 6 427 days and for the monitored group was 839 6 430 days.

After the 1st year of randomization, CMS compared sample characteristics of the demonstration project cohorts with a representative sample of beneficiaries to document that results of the demonstrations were representative of the targeted populations. The CMS analysis of the sample characteristics for this study concluded that the subjects adequately reflected a representative population of patients

with discharge diagnoses of heart failure within the previous 30-day and 1-year periods.1 The cost of care for these subjects were consistent with potential for decreasing Medicare costs at the outcome levels set by the demonstration project.

## Hospitalizations and Medicare Payments

Cohort-Level Analysis. Over the course of the 4 years of the study, 154 participants were hospitalized with a total of 625 hospitalizations. Seventy-four of the 101 subjects in the usual-care group (312 hospitalizations) and 80 of the 103 subjects in the monitored group (313 hospitalizations) were hospitalized at least once (P 5 .51). The average time to first hospitalization after randomization was 240 days (95% confidence interval [CI] 200e281) with a median of 138 days. There was no significant difference in the time to first hospitalization between the 2 groups (Fig. 1).

Figure 2 demonstrates total Medicare payments for each group. Note that there were outliers which might bias estimates of costs for the majority of hospitalizations. Therefore, analyses were done with and without outlier hospitalizations. Outliers were defined as length of stay and Medicare payments that were 3 standard deviations above the mean values for the whole cohort. Hospitalizations with length of stay O75 days or Medicare payments O$121,000 were excluded. Seventeen hospitalizations (2.8%) met this definition (14 monitored and 3 usual care).

Subject-Level Analysis. Table 3 presents the average number of hospitalizations per subject in the 2 groups and the mean for total number of days hospitalized for

the duration of the study per subject in each group, both with and without outliers. These numbers include all subjects, including those who were not hospitalized. Also in Table 3 are the mean and median Medicare payments for inpatient stays and ED visits per subject for the term of the study. Mean differences are not statistically significant.

After excluding the outliers, Medicare payments for patients randomized to telemonitoring were not significantly higher than those randomized to Usual Care. Differences in ED expenses also were nonsignificant. No outliers were identified for the heart failure hospitalizations, and there were no significant differences between the 2 groups.

## Thirty-Day Readmissions

Cohort-Level Analysis. All readmission intervals were calculated for admissions that occurred after the first admission after randomization. For the entire trial, there were 203 30-day readmissions in the total cohort, with an unadjusted readmission rate of 30% of total hospitalizations. The usual-care group had a total of 114 30-day readmissions resulting from 264 admissions (43%). The monitored group experienced 89 30-day readmissions resulting from 257 admissions (35%; P ! .05) The lower readmission rate for the monitored group was only present in the first year of enrollment. (Table 4) Even though the 30- day readmission rate for the monitored group was significantly less in the 1st year, the cost to Medicare for inpatient care was not higher for patients in the monitored group.

Subject-Level Analysis. In the year following randomization, 118 subjects were admitted (176 total admissions). Of these 118 subjects, 108 (91.5%) had $1

readmission within the year following discharge from that hospitalization. In the 1st year, the readmission rate was 45.3% for the monitored group and 62.5% for the usualcare group. There were no differences between the groups in Medicare expenditures.

In their first year of enrollment, the usual-care group had 19 patients with $1 admission and 14 patients with $1 readmission within 30 days of a discharge. Ten patients had O1 30-day readmissions. A single subject in the usualcare group had 23 30-day readmissions. The monitored group had 32 patients with $1 admission and 11 patients with $1 readmission within 30 days of a discharge. Five patients had O1 30-day readmissions. Although the number of 30-day readmissions was significantly lower in the monitored group (P ! .05), the number of patients with 30-day readmissions was not significantly different between groups.

Over the course of the study, the usual-care group had 74 patients with $1 admission and 34 patients with $1 readmission within 30 days of a discharge. Eighteen patients had O1 30-day readmissions. The monitored group had 80 patients with $1 admission and 33 patients with $1 readmission within 30 days of a discharge. Eighteen patients had O1 30-day readmission. The number of 30-day readmissions per patient between the 2 groups over the course of the study was not different.

## Mortality

There were a total of 94 deaths in the cohort over the course of the study. Forty subjects died within the 1st year of randomization and 31 within the 2nd year. There

were no differences in the number of deaths or survival time between the monitored and usual-care groups. Figure 3 shows a Kaplan-Meier curve comparing deaths in the 2 groups.

The number of days alive and out of the hospital was derived by calculating the number of days from randomization to death or end of the study and subtracting the cumulative number of days in the hospital. The usual-care group was alive and out of hospital for 724 6 433 days and the monitored group for 799 6 429 days; P 5 .207.

## Health-Related Quality of Life

Repeated-measures analysis of variance was performed for the SF-36 composite physical and mental scores as well as the MLHF total, physical, and emotional scores. All scores improved statistically over the year with the 3 measurements (P ! .001), but there was no difference between groups. Table 5 presents the change in scores over the 1st year.

## Adherence

Participants in this study transmitted a mean of 6 days per week (95% CI 5.93e6.03) the 1st year to a low of 5 days a week (95% CI 4.76e5.33) in the 4th year.

## 

In the present study, home telemonitoring in a well treated group of heart failure patients decreased the number of 30- day readmissions for patients who were hospitalized in the 1st year, but it did not decrease total real costs. These results occurred in a study of well matched subjects receiving guideline-based treatment regimens. All patients had cardiologists, most with expertise in heart failure, involved in their care. Patients in the monitored group were managed by a single nurse practitioner who was experienced in heart failure and had the authority to act on changes in weight and symptoms based on that experience. This nurse practitioner had direct access to the subject’s cardiologist if consultation was indicated.

Early randomized trials of home telemonitoring had mixed, but mostly positive, results showing significant mortality, health-related quality of life, and cost benefits. 7e11 Although some reviews and meta-analyses suggest possible benefits,12 16 others demonstrate important limitations. 17e19 The present study helps to explain why subsequent randomized controlled studies of home telemonitoring have demonstrated no benefit from telemonitoring on all-cause readmission or death in 180 days,19,20 cardiovascular death,20,21 heart failure hospitalization,19,20 length of stay,19,21 or number of hospitalizations.19

In each of these recent studies, as well as the study reported here, the monitored and usual-care groups were both on well described optimal medical therapy for heart failure. Because earlier studies did not demonstrate optimal therapy, we think that the recent data support the conclusion

that good medical therapy is the key to the survival and prevention of hospitalization benefits. This does not preclude other, as yet unidentified, benefits of home telemonitoring in some subgroups.

## Hospitalizations and Cost

Overall, Medicare payments for the monitored subjects were not decreased compared with the usual-care group. There may have been changes in where the money was spent, with ED payments lower for monitored patients, but this did not result in lower overall costs. Although it is tempting to conclude that the telemonitoring and contact with the nurse practitioner made ED visits unnecessary, this care did not decrease admissions to the hospital. Our finding that telemonitoring did not result in fewer heart failure or all-cause hospitalizations is consistent with earlier studies. 20,21

## Thirty-Day Readmission Rates

The present study showed some differences in the 1st year that did not persist. There was a decrease in 30-day readmission rates for patients hospitalized during the 1st year of the study, which was not seen subsequently. Earlier studies showing positive effects on hospitalization7,11 also failed to show sustained effects, although they did not specifically measure 30-day readmissions. Even studies that reported significant decreases in length of stay8,12 reported that this did not persist past 6 months of follow-up. Similarly, Choudhry et al20 looked at 180-day readmission rates and found that telemonitoring did not prevent rehospitalizations.

These findings raise many questions about current efforts to decrease cost of care and demonstrate quality of care by focusing on 30-day readmissions. In this very well managed cohort of HF patients, 30-day readmissions were close to one-third of admissions, and efforts to reduce these

readmissions, though significant, did not persist and had no impact on cost of care. This was a population with significant chronic disease burden, with multiple comorbidities and advanced heart failure. These data challenge the notion that rehospitalization is a quality issue rather than an issue of disease stage and progression.

## Mortality

Unlike a number of other studies of home telemonitoring,8e 10 there was no mortality benefit of monitoring in the present subjects. Mortality in the 1st year of enrollment was O19% and O15% in the 2nd year. This analysis did not show a mortality benefit in the number of deaths overall, the number of deaths per year of enrollment in the study, or the number of days from randomization until death. Earlier studies have frequently shown that monitored patients were more likely to be on evidence-based medications and doses.8 This could easily explain a mortality benefit. In the present study, however, all patients in both groups were maximally treated.

## Health-Related Quality of Life

All dimensions of both the SF-36 and the MLHF improved over the course of the 1st year in the study, but there was no difference in the degree of improvement in the 2 groups. Benatar et al7 also noted improvement in health-related quality of life for both groups. It may be that forming connections with subjects with heart failure and attending to their health over the course of a year by collecting data, inquiring about their well-being, and providing encouragement and information may be sufficient to improve their health-related quality of life. However, the telemonitoring itself did not improve assessments of health-related quality of life.

## Adherence

The issue of adherence has been raised repeatedly in the critical reviews of studies of home telemonitoring as a possible explanation of neutral or negative findings.18 In the present study, adherence was excellent and consistent with earlier studies that have reported adherence.19,20

## Study Limitations

There are distinct advantages to using actual payment data for determining costs of care. The limitation of using administrative databases, however, includes assumptions about the accuracy of the data entry and coding. Furthermore, outliers can be problematic in studies such as the present one, and indeed, 2 subjects in the monitored group who received left ventricular assist devices and 1 who received a heart transplant had extremely high costs. Nevertheless, the results were similar when outliers were excluded.

Newer technology could yield different results, and the present study can not be used to make conclusions about different technology. However, the movement toward implanted monitors is a technology based on an entirely different theoretical framework. In contrast, most home monitoring programs continue to use weight measurement as the key monitoring parameters. Therefore, the present study should still be relevant.

It is possible that the unusually good care led to the present results. One of the criticisms of some earlier trials is that the usual-care group was poorly characterized and treated, so that the significant results seen in the monitored group were hypothesized to be as likely from the guideline-based medical treatment received by the monitored group as from the monitoring. We felt that having both the monitored and usual-care groups on the best evidence-based treatments would be the best test of the monitoring as an intervention. Compared with earlier studies, subjects in both groups received care from cardiologists and heart failure specialists and an experienced nurse practitioner. The results of this study emphasize the importance of good medical treatment first and foremost in the care of patients with HF. This also implies that the study is applicable only to such patients.

## Conclusion

In the present study, telemonitoring decreased 30-day readmission rates for 1 year, but these findings did not result in lower total costs, decreased hospitalizations, improved symptoms, or improved mortality. These findings raise the question as to whether 30-day readmission rate is an appropriate surrogate for excellent care. The findings also raise questions about the role of telemonitoring and remote monitoring in general in the care of patients with heart failure and other chronic diseases.

A number of questions remain unanswered. Are there patients who will benefit? Perhaps a rural population with less medical access might demonstrate more benefit from

telemonitoring. This study has demonstrated no decrease in cost of care, but other end points could also be important. One of the difficulties involved in telemonitoring is the problem of treating numbers rather than treating people. For example, completely stable weights in a patient retaining fluid might occur if that person lost dry weight and replaced it with fluid. Telephone contact and questions and answers through an interactive computer system may not replace an in-person exam.

Proof of benefits and improved outcomes need to be demonstrated before assuming that telemonitoring strategies are effective and cost efficient. Studies are essential to identify whether there are patient populations who will benefit from this technology.
