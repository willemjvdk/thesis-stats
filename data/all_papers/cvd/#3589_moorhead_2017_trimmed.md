# Methods
This Institutional Review Boardeapproved and emonitored 12-week study enrolled adults (18 years of age or older) with uncontrolled hypertension (systolic blood pressure 140 mmHg) taking at least 2 antihypertensives and uncontrolled diabetes mellitus type 2 (A1C -7%) on metformin or sulfonylurea. Although not required, some subjects were also taking a statin. 9-12

Subjects were recruited from May 1 to October 6, 2015, and the last subject’s visit was on December 30, 2015. Every subject signed an informed consent before any study procedures.

Subjects were assigned to use DH for 4 (5 sites; n ¼ 41 subjects) or 12 (4 sites; n  41 subjects) weeks or receive usual care (7 sites; n 31 subjects) based on a cluster-randomized design. All DH subjects received sensor-enabled medicines in replacement for their antihypertensives, metformin or sulfonylurea, and statin (based on subject consent) for the duration of the study. Usual care subjects received standard medical care including medication changes, patient education, and any relevant counseling; however, they did not use DH during the study.

DH data from each subject was collected continuously as it was uploaded directly to the cloud. Clinical parameters on each subject were collected via electronic case report forms at each visit.

Subjects used DH along with sensor-enabled medicines for treating hypertension, high cholesterol, and diabetes. Each sensor-enabled medicine is a capsule containing an active medicine plus an ingestible sensor with a specific code for the medicine. On enrollment, each subject was prescribed at least two sensor-enabled antihypertensives, plus sensor-enabled metformin or glipizide. DH subjects were required to use sensor-enabled antihypertensives only (DH medication panel included lisinopril, losartan, hydrochlorothiazide, amlodipine, metformin, glipizide, and atorvastatin); however, they could use nonpanel diabetes and cholesterol medicines. Subjects could switch to DH panel medications at equivalent doses. Subjects set schedules for each of their sensor-enabled medicines at the beginning of DH use and with subsequent medication changes and were able to schedule multiple medications for each dose time.

Analyzed data included deidentified subject study ID, daily medical schedules, date and time of each medication ingestion, and date and time when the subject was within Bluetooth

range of the mobile device. Only DH subject data were included in this study; no usual care subject data were included. To assess the incremental impact of DH medication reminders on medication, two assumptions were made: 1) the subject saw (“seeing”) the reminder when he or she was within Bluetooth range of the mobile device within 30 minutes of the scheduled medication dose reminder; and 2) medication consumed within 2 hours after seeing the reminder was considered to be the result of seeing the DH medication reminder. If the subject was not in Bluetooth range within 30 minutes of the reminder, the subject was considered to be “not seeing” the reminder.

## Efficacy of dose reminders to improve adherence

This analysis included subjects who used a DH medication reminder and were deemed to have seen the reminder at least 10 times during use. Criteria allowed an adequate sample size to statistically compare intrasubject differences between frequencies of medication taking when “seeing” and “not seeing” DH medication dose reminders.

Figure 2 details possible scenarios when a patient sees or does not see DH medication dose reminders. The dose time is preset by the patient in the DH patient app for each medicine as prescribed by the health care provider.

In scenario 1, the patient took all of the scheduled medications before dose time. Scenarios 2-6 represent situations in which patients did not take any of the scheduled medications or took only some, but not all, by dose time. In these situations, the medication reminder could help to make the patients take their medications. Because the reminder displays only the dose time and not the dosage information, we assumed that the reminder influenced only when the patient should take the medications and not which medications or how many pills to take. Therefore, the measured outcome is a binary variable that indicates whether the patient took any medications after seeing or not seeing the reminder.

This analysis looked only at the scenarios where the patient did not take medication(s) before appearance of DH medication reminder(s) on the mobile device, whether or not it was seen by the patient (scenarios 2-6). Instances where the subject took all medications before the medication dose reminder (scenario 1) were not included in the analysis. In this case, the patient is not expected to take any more medication at dose time when the reminder is displayed. Cases where medication

was ingested after the 2-hour window (scenario 6) were considered to be the same as medication being missed (scenario 5).

## Safety assessment of medication dose reminder

A concern with the medication dose reminder is the potential for overdosing (i.e., the patient takes a medication before the dose time and then takes additional medicine after seeing a medication dose reminder). This analysis included all subjects who used DH along with sensor-enabled medications during the study.

Potential scenarios for this analysis were: 1) the patient takes the correct number of doses of each medicine daily, that is, no overdosing; 2) the patient takes additional doses of 1 or more medicines and the additional doses occur after seeing the dose-time reminder (within 2 hours of dose time); 3) the patient takes additional doses of 1 or more medicines, but the additional doses are taken before seeing the dose-time

reminder; and 4) the patient takes additional doses of 1 or more medicines either before or after the dose time, and the DH medication dose reminder was not seen. Scenario 2 cases were considered to be overdose events that were associated with seeing the reminder.

## Data analysis

Baseline values and ingestion data were summarized descriptively. Means of continuous variables were reported as mean ± SD. Categoric variables (included dichotomous variables) were summarized as proportions (% of total).

Overall adherence was calculated from the mean daily adherence by day and by subject. Each subject’s daily adherence was calculated as average adherence to each medicine on that particular day and was calculated as the number of pills detected by the sensor patch divided by the number of pills expected for each medicine (maximum value is 100% per day). Daily on-time adherence was also calculated as the number of pills for each medicine detected by the sensor patch within 2 hours before or after the scheduled dose time divided by the number of pills expected for each medicine.

The outcome for each dose window (within 2 hours after the scheduled dose time) is binary: either medication is taken (at least 1 pill is taken) or not taken (all pills are missed) within this window. The number of dose windows and number of medication doses taken after “seeing” and “not seeing” were totaled. The proportion of medication taken was calculated for each state, “seeing” and “not seeing.” The probability of taking medications was then calculated for the population for each state (seeing and not seeing) in scenarios 2-6.

The proportions of medicine taken for seeing and not seeing for each patient (scenarios 2-6) were calculated with the use of the following formulas:

\mathrm { P _ { s e e i n g } = }(number of windows where medication was taken after seeing)/(total number of windows for seeing)

Pnotseeing ¼ (number of windows where medication was taken after not seeing)/(total number of windows for not seeing)

The difference between the proportions was calculated for each patient to compare the 2 proportions ( \mathrm { P _ { s e e i n g } - \mathrm { P _ { n o t s e e i n g } ) . } }. The total number of samples (i.e., dose windows) per subject varied depending on the prescribed frequency of medication doses (e.g., twice daily or 3 times daily), the duration of DH usage, and the proportion of days that the subject used DH. For each subject, we tested whether or not there was a statistically significant increase in taking medication during the 2-hour window. The null hypothesis for each test was that there is no difference between \mathrm { P _ { s e e i n g } }and \mathrm { P _ { n o t s e e i n g } }for each subject. The alternate hypothsis is that \mathrm { P _ { s e e i n g } }is greater than \mathrm { P _ { n o t s e e i n g } . }A 1-tailed 2- proportion z-test was performed at alpha level 0.05 for each subject. The mean difference between \mathrm { P _ { s e e i n g } }and \mathrm { P _ { n o t s e e i n g } }for all subjects was grouped by their mean daily adherence. Mean daily adherence was used (as opposed to mean daily timing adherence) because it was the measurement of interest in the main study.

A mixed-effect logistic regression analysis was used to determine the logarithm of the probability for the entire subject sample of taking medication after seeing or not seeing the medication dose reminder compared with not seeing the medication dose reminder. The dependent variable is the

outcome of a dose window, which is whether the subject took medication (scenarios 2-6). The predictor is the dichotomous variable: seeing or not seeing the medication reminder in the dose window. Mean daily adherence, demographic characteristics (age, ethnicity, gender, mobile device usage, and employment), and presence of a psychiatric comorbidity (i.e., a psychiatric diagnosis, such as depression, anxiety, or bipolar disorder) were included in this analysis as potential covariates. Subjects were used as random effects. Alpha level 0.05 was used to select the predictors to be included in the model. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was used to determine how well this model fit the sample data.

For the overdose safety analysis, the frequency of overdose events was calculated as the number of dosing windows in which the medication dose reminder was seen and overdosing occurred divided by the total number of dosing windows in which the medication dose reminder was seen. The probability of overdosing was then estimated based on that frequency. The Clopper-Pearson method was used to calculate a 95% confidence interval (CI) for the overdosing probability, assuming that all subjects behave about the same regarding the potential to overdose.

Analyses were performed on R (R Foundation, version 3.2.2) for statistical computation and graphics, lme4 (version 1.1.11) for building generalized linear mixed models, Resource Selection (version 0.2-4) for Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-offit test, and GenBinomApps (version 1.0-2) for Clopper-Pearson CI.

# Results
There were 57 subjects included in the dose reminder efficacy study and 74 subjects in the safety study. Table 1 presents demographics for each study population. In general, the population was largely poor; more than one-half of the subjects had an annual income of $20,000 or less. In addition, a large portion of the population had less than a high school education. Most (>94%) had daily experience using a mobile device such as a smartphone. A large portion (42%) of the cohort were nonwhite and all of the subjects identifying themselves as Hispanic were also white. Many subjects (23%-24%) had a psychiatric comorbidity.

## Safety and the risk of overdosing

There were no cases of overdosing following a patient seeing a medication dose reminder in the 2851 dose windows where the subject had seen the medication dose reminder. The upper limit of the 95% CI for the chance of overdosing after seeing the medication dose reminder was estimated to be 0.105% (1 in 952 dose windows).

Of 8236 windows, there were 25 windows where patients took more pills than scheduled. We inspected these ingestions manually. There were 16 windows where extra ingestions were taken before the dose time, but these were actually late ingestions from the previous dose window. In 1 window, a patient had a dosage increase but updated the medication schedule late, so it appeared that the patient was taking additional doses. There were 6 windows which did represent overdose events; however, the patients were not within Bluetooth range before taking the additional pills and, therefore, these overdose events were not deemed to be related to the reminder. The upper limit of the 95% CI for overdose events out of the 8236 windows was 0.143% (about 1 in 700 dose windows).
