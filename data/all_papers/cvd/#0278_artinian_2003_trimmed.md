## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# METHODS
## Design

Potentially eligible patients were identified on arrival for scheduled CHF clinic visits by screening charts. Eligible patients were given a detailed explanation of the study and written informed consent was obtained. Following baseline data collection and using a random numbers table, patients were randomized either to usual care or to the Webbased intervention plus usual care. The Wayne State University Human Investigation Committee approved the study protocol.

At baseline all patients received a digital home weight scale and an automated home blood pressure monitor. The patients also received a $10.00 incentive payment at a 4-week follow-up visit and $15.00 at a 3-month follow-up.

## Setting and sample

A non-random sample of outpatients (n  18) was recruited from the CHF clinic of the Detroit Veterans Affairs Medical Center. Inclusion criteria were scheduled outpatient age 18 years, symptomatic left ventricular dysfunction, an ejection fraction of -40%, ownership or rental of a home or apartment with a telephone, ability to read and understand English, and plans to remain in the city during the study period. Exclusion criteria were history of dementia; mental illness; symptomatic infectious disease or advanced liver disease; self-reported substance abuse; hemodialysis; coronary angioplasty within the last 60 days or coronary artery bypass graft surgery within the last 90 days; inability to participate in a 6-minute walk test (6MWT); or terminal stages of cancer.

## Data collection

Patients completed questionnaires during structured interviews, which were conducted in a private exam room of the clinic. The 6MWT25 was conducted in an undisturbed 100-foot indoor level hospital corridor near the clinic. Data collection took approximately 45 minutes of the participant’s time.

## Instruments

An investigator-developed questionnaire was used to collect demographic information for each patient. We assessed self-care using the Revised Heart Failure Self-Care Behavior Scale.4 This instrument describes 29 behaviors patients with HF must perform to some degree to regulate their own func-

tioning. Patients indicated how often they used each behavior on a scale ranging from 0 (none of the time) to 5 (all of the time). The instrument takes approximately 10 minutes to complete. A total score is determined by summing the item scores, with a maximum possible score of 145. Content validity of the scale was demonstrated through evaluations made by a panel of experts including two nurse practitioners and two self-care experts.4 The average Cronbach alpha reliability across times for the present study was 0.81.

Patients were instructed how to use their weight scale and blood pressure monitor and were asked to record their weights and blood pressures every morning. Compliance with daily weight and blood pressure monitoring was measured by examining daily weight and blood pressure logs for both treatment groups and by the compliance device for the intervention group.

Compliance with medication taking was measured through pill counts for both treatment groups and by the compliance device for the intervention group.

We assessed QOL using the Minnesota Living With Heart Failure Questionnaire,26 a disease-specific measure of health-related QOL. Patients indicated their perspective concerning disability using 21 items on a 6-point response scale (0-5), with a maximum score of 105. A lower score indicated better QOL. Two subscale scores reflected physical (8 items) and emotional (5 items) impairment.26 The physical subscale questions were rest during day, walking and climbing stairs, working around the house, going away from home, sleeping, doing things with others, dyspnea, and fatigue. The emotional questions were limitations related to feeling burdensome, feeling a loss of self-control, worry, difficulty concentrating and remembering, and feeling depressed. This questionnaire has been used extensively in HF research. Its reliability and validity have been established.26,27 In this study, the Cronbach alpha coefficients for were as follows: 0.90 (total scale), 0.90 (physical subscale), and 0.88 (emotional subscale).

Functional status was assessed by the NYHA FC.28 Functional status was also measured by the 6MWT as described by Bittner et al.25 After 6 minutes had elapsed, patients were instructed to stop walking and the total distance walked was measured to the nearest foot. Before and after each 6MWT the patient’s pulse, respiratory rate, and blood pressure were recorded.

## Independent variables

All patients received an educational booklet describing CHF self-care behaviors.29 Usual care included visits to the cardiologist in the HF clinic at regular intervals with interval frequency determined by the severity of the CHF. Intervals ranged from weekly to every several months. During each visit a pharmacist assessed medication-taking and provided patients with written information that included the name, frequency and action of their medications.

The patients randomized to the Web-based intervention group received usual care plus a MedeMonitor. The device is the size of a videocassette, retains a supply of up to 5 medications in individual compartments, and uses an alarm to remind patients daily when to take their medications, which to take, and how many pills to take. The monitor contains an additional 25 virtual compartments in which daily reminders or questions about other medications, symptoms, monitoring daily weight and blood pressure, reducing salt intake, eating heart healthy, and engaging in physical activity were programmed by the investigator. Reminders were tailored to the patient’s medical history and treatment recommendations. The monitor produced a date-

and time-stamped record of the patient’s pill taking by recording the opening of a compartment, and it recorded patients’ responses to specific questions. The monitor sat in a cradle that was connected to a telephone line. At the end of the day, each patient’s information was automatically transmitted to the Med-eMonitor server. Updates to the patient’s regimen, such as changes in the timing of daily alarms, were simultaneously downloaded to the MedeMonitor.

## Data analysis

All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 11. Descriptive statistics were used to analyze all study variables. Repeated measures analyses of variance (ANOVAs) with post hoc t-tests were used to analyze the study’s hypothesis. The significance level was set at 0.05. Content analysis was used to analyze the qualitative interview data.

## Perceived experiences of living with the Med-eMonitor

In order to understand the experiences of living with a compliance device that is linked to a Webbased monitoring system, qualitative interviews were conducted with the 9 patients in the monitor group. Data were analyzed looking for common themes and 4 were identified: Loved it, Hated it, Technical problems, Changes in Lifestyle (Table III).

Loved it. Patients either loved the monitor or hated it. Those who had a positive experience ( n =5, 56%) enjoyed the reminders the monitor provided. This group felt they were better able to stay on a schedule with their medication doses and this resulted in their feeling better. One man stated, “It reminded me about my medications and took the worry out of it. It rings; I take my medication.” Another said, “I have trouble with the 8:00 AM meds. The monitor reminded me. Without the machine, I used to forget.” One man said his blood pressure was more stable with fewer ups and downs. Another felt he needed less nitroglycerin as a result of a more regular drug schedule.

The patients in this group enjoyed the messages reminding them to take their blood pressure. Messages about exercise and diet encouraged the participants to be more active and to try to eat more healthy foods. One subject described living with the

Med-eMonitor as a unique experience. Another said it kept him jumping. Some found the machine a conversation piece that encouraged discussion about HF. Many said they hated to return the machine at the end of the study.

Hated it. Four (44%) patients did not enjoy their experience with the monitor. They felt that their life centered on the monitor. They needed to regulate their outings because the machine did not shut off when they were gone, and they could not take it with them. One patient put it this way, “I spent my time waiting for the machine to beep. I felt better when I was looking at my pill bottles.” The regimen established by the monitor was troublesome to some participants. One subject said, “Sometimes I wanted to stay in bed an extra 5 minutes, and it would beep. I would have to go and turn it off.”

Generally these participants already had a wellestablished routine for their medication needs. The monitor was seen as a nuisance. One participant’s wife said she had to find her husband to answer his machine if he was out of the room. Another felt that the monitor was “bugging me all the time.” Because these subjects already had a set routine in place they felt the monitor was intrusive.

Technical difficulties. Four (44%) patients experienced difficulties with the monitor. The complaint most often heard was related to the medication drawers. Four (44%) older patients had a hard time getting smaller pills out of the drawer because of its design. Two of these same patients had difficulty reading the screen on the monitor and suggested it should be larger. The color of the lettering on the screen was hard to see for some patients. In addition, many in this group felt that the message should have been longer to improve clarity. One patient could not hear the beep of the monitor, even on the loudest setting.

Changing lifestyles. Six (67%) of the 9 patients felt the monitor helped them establish a better routine. They expressed a desire to stay on schedule with their medications and stated that they planned to exercise more regularly. One person said, “Reminding me of what foods to eat helped me change the way I cook.” Another said, “I benefited very much from having this machine.”
