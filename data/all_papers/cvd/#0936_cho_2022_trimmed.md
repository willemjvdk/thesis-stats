## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# METHOD
## Participants

Stroke patients with mild to severe arm motor deficits from 11 US sites participated in the clinical trial for the original study (Cramer et al., 2019). Participants were recruited from among patients who experienced stroke 4–36 weeks prior to the trial, while excluding patients with mood or cognitive deficits. A sample size of 124 was decided on, which allows for meeting the 30 per cent non-inferiority margin for the in-clinic versus TR condition while providing 85 per cent power (Cramer et al., 2019). Among the 124 participants who were recruited, 34 were female and 90 were male. The participants were 61.44 years old on average (SD = 13.30). After being recruited, the patients signed an informed consent form and underwent baseline testing for the measures of interest. Aversive events related to therapy included arm/shoulder pain and muscle fatigue. These occurred in six members of the TR therapy group and five members of the in-clinic therapy group, but there was no resultant attrition.

## Procedures

In both therapy groups, the rehabilitation exercises were designed based on an upper-extremity taskspecific training manual (Lang & Birkenmeier, 2013) and an accelerated skill acquisition program (Winstein et al., 2016). In both groups, each session lasted for 70 min. It involved standard therapy actions, such as stretching, strengthening, and an active range of motion; these were achieved through 65 min/day of exercises and functional games. Patients also received 5 min of stroke education.

Patients in the in-clinic therapy group participated in 18 supervised sessions at the designated research centers and 18 unsupervised rehabilitation sessions in their homes over the course of 6–8 weeks. Licensed therapists hosted the supervised sessions for a continuous 70 min, and the patients used individualized instruction booklets to complete their unsupervised sessions.

Patients in the TR therapy group were provided with a TR station, which a member of the study team delivered and installed in their homes. All 36 sessions took place via this TR station. The video games were created based on already-existing popular games (e.g. Flappy Bird, Space Invaders) or familiar everyday activities (e.g. blackjack, driving, playing the piano). Games were designed to emphasize various arm motor (e.g. speed, range, and fine control). Therapists participated in the development process and advised on the actions to match standard rehabilitation exercises. In each of the 18

supervised sessions, therapists hosted a 30-min videoconference at the start of the session to guide the patients in choosing and playing the video games. After 30 min, the therapist disconnected from the videoconference, and the patient completed the remaining 35 min guided by the TR system. Although TR patients interacted with the therapists with the same frequency as in-clinic patients (i.e. three times a week), all interactions were conducted via videoconference, and the duration of the interactions was significantly shorter compared with the in-clinic therapy group (i.e. 30 min vs. full session). In addition, the interactions were mainly focused on instruction regarding the use of the game devices and selection of games. The patients provided the therapists with feedback regarding the unsupervised sessions, and the therapists adjusted their guidance regarding the games and devices accordingly. In this way, the TR patients influenced the choice of game, controlling device, and game difficulty. After each round of the game, patients were provided with numeric feedback on their game performance in order to indicate how well they were performing the exercises. The other 18 unsupervised sessions were conducted without any therapist contact; they chose the game and the game-controlling device. After each round of game in all 36 sessions, patients were provided with feedback on their game performance in numeric form, with the intent of increasing their awareness of how well they were executing the exercises.

## Measurements

Baseline assessments of the variables of interest were conducted for each patient during their initial visit to the research center after recruitment. In-clinic patients were coded as “1,” and TR patients were coded as “2.” At the end of the first and sixth weeks of therapy, additional assessments were conducted at the end of the sessions for both therapy groups. Immediately after the end of therapy and again at 30 days post-therapy, follow-up measurements of arm motor recovery were collected during return visits to the research center. In addition to the focus variables of the study, patients' medical records were obtained, and compliance levels were recorded over the course of the program.

## Goal adjustment

Goal adjustment was measured using the Optimization in Primary and Secondary Control (OPS) Scale (Heckhausen et al., 1998). The scale consists of 12 items related to goal management that were rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree). Among the four subscales (i.e. goal engagement, goal disengagement, goal re-engagement, and self-protection), we combined goal disengagement (e.g. “When I find it impossible to attain the goal, I reduce efforts toward that goal and put it out of my mind”) and goal re-engagement (e.g. “If I cannot attain the goal, I will put effort into other meaningful goals”) items as indicators of the level of goal adjustment, as conceptualized by Wrosch, Scheier, Miller, et al. (2003). All OPS items were answered with respect to participants' recovery goals. Goal adjustment was administered at baseline ( \mathbf { M } = 1 5 . 2 0 , S D = 4 . 8 8 )), at the end of the first week of therapy (M = 16.21, SD = 3.90), and at the end of the sixth week (M = 16.16, SD  =  4.74). Changes in the use of goal adjustment strategies in response to initial setbacks were calculated by subtracting the baseline goal adjustment from the goal adjustment measured after the first week of therapy. The variable was labeled ΔGoal-Adjustment, and it captured the extent to which patients adjusted their recovery goals in response to their experiences and progress during therapy (Cronbach's α values of the baseline measure and week 1 measure were .81 and .80, respectively).

## Motivational resources

The Physical Activity Enjoyment Scale (PACES) was used to measure activity-inherent enjoyment as an indicator of motivational resources. Patients were asked to indicate how they felt about the required physical activities using eight items (i.e. pleasurable, fun, pleasant, invigorating, gratifying, exhilarating, stimulating, and refreshing) on 7-point scales ( \mathrm { e . g . 1 = ^ { \mathrm { 4 } } I t ^ { \prime } s }very unpleasant, \mathrm { { } ^ { 5 9 } 7 = \mathrm { { } ^ { 6 6 } I t ^ { \prime } s } }very pleasant”) (Kendzierski & Morganstein, 2009). The PACES score was assessed at baseline ( \mathbf { M } = 3 7 . 7 1, S D = 1 0 . 9 3 ), and follow-up assessments were conducted at the end of the first week ( \mathbf { M } = 4 3 . 0 5, S D = 9 . 3 3 )and sixth week ( \mathrm { M } = 4 4 . 1 1 , S D = 8 . 9 8 ). The level of activity-inherent enjoyment was averaged between the first week and the sixth week assessment to create a variable called PACESaverage. This variable captured the overall amount of motivational resources maintained after the baseline assessment (Cronbach's α values of the baseline measure, week 1 measure, and week 6 measure were .94, .95, and .93, respectively).

## Game performance

We calculated the patients' game performance scores based on the highest scores they achieved. Different weights were given in accordance with the game difficulty chosen in each session, in accordance with convention in the context of game performance data analysis (Cheng et al., 2015). Because all 25 available video games had different scoring systems, and because not all games were played by all patients, a comprehensive analysis that collapsed all score data into one continuous measurement of game performance was not possible. Instead, we chose to focus our analyses on the game played by all patients, Flappy Bird. It was played at least once in 86.2 per cent of all sessions, and the average minutes played ( \mathbf { M } = 4 2 . 4 1 , S D = 1 1 . 9 1 )) was higher for Flappy Bird than the mean play duration of all games ( \mathbf { M } = 3 1 . 8 1 , S D = 1 3 . 6 0 ). The average score was 40.88 (SD = 26.78). Consistent with previous research (Harwell et al., 2018; Ventura & Shute, 2013; Wu et al., 2012), we dichotomized (i.e. top half vs. bottom half) the TR therapy group into high performers ( \mathbf { M } = 6 1 . 9 2 , S D = 2 0 . 1 3 ; ; coded as “0”) and low performers (M = 19.00, SD = 9.65; coded as “1”).

## Fugl-Meyer assessment

Arm motor status was assessed with the Fugl-Meyer test, which consists of 33 items that assess various aspects of arm movement (e.g. “shoulder flexion bilateral”), each of which is scored on a 3- point ordinal scale (0 = item could not be performed at all, 1 = partial performance, and 2 = p e r f e c tperformance) (See et al., 2013). The assessment was conducted by therapists during the screening process ( \mathrm { M } = 4 1 . 8 5 , S D = 8 . 6 9 ), at baseline ( \mathbf { M } = 4 2 . 7 6 , S D = 8 . 2 6 ), at the live visit immediately following the end of therapy ( \mathrm { M } = 4 9 . 6 2 , S D = 8 . 8 6 ), and 30 days after the end of therapy ( \mathrm { M } = 5 0 . 2 4 , S D = 9 . 9 6 ) . The indicator of arm motor status recovery achieved from therapy, ΔFugl-Meyer, was based on the baseline Fugl-Meyer score assessed at the beginning of the therapy, subtracted from the Fugl-Meyer score assessed at the end of the therapy (Cronbach's α values of the screening measure, baseline measure, post-study measure, and 30-day follow-up measure were .86, .86, .90, and .91, respectively).

## Patient satisfaction

Patient satisfaction level was assessed at the first therapy session ( \mathrm { M } = 5 4 . 6 2 , S D = 8 . 3 4 )and final therapy session ( \mathrm { M } = 5 6 . 3 3 , S D = 9 . 5 9 )using a 7-point Likert scale (1 = not satisfying at all, 7 = very satisfying) with 10 items related to the patients' satisfaction with the therapy program and the exercises assigned to them (e.g. “I find the home exercise module _”). We used the final session measure as the indicator of overall satisfaction with the therapy (Cronbach's α values of the week 1 measure and week 6 measure were .85 and .86, respectively).

## Data analysis

All the analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 24.0, and PROCESS Macro was used to test the moderation and mediation models (Hayes, 2017). Given previous findings indicating that patients' age and gender are associated with motivational regulation in rehabilitation processes (Leung et al., 2008; Reuter et al., 2010), age ( \mathbf { M } = 6 1 . 4 4 , S D = 1 3 . 3 0 )and gender (72.58% male) were included as covariates in all models. The baseline measure of PACES was controlled in all models to correct for any baseline differences.

Of the variables of interest, 2.2 per cent were missing. Little's MCAR test was not statistically significant, indicating that the data were missing at random ( \chi ^ { 2 } ( 1 5 0 1 ) = 2 9 1 . 5 8 4 , n s ). Multiple imputations using five iterations (Tabachnick & Fidell, 2013) were conducted to infer the missing values from existing observations (Li, Stuart, & Allison, 2015). Reported descriptive statistics and parameter estimates for our hypothesis tests represent pooled averages of five imputed dataset. We compared the results as the number of imputations increased from 0 to 5, and the results were consistent across all comparisons (Carpenter et al., 2007).

For the first hypothesis (H1), we conducted an ANCOVA to test the difference in PACESaverage among the three groups (i.e. in-clinic, TR high performers, and TR low performers). For the second hypothesis (H2), we conducted hierarchical OLS regression analyses to test whether the association between ΔGoal-Adjustment and P A C E S _ { a \nu e r a g e }varied as a function of membership in a particular treatment group. For the third hypothesis (H3), we conducted another hierarchical OLS regression analysis to test the association between ΔGoal-Adjustment and P A C E S _ { a \nu e r a g e }in the TR group and whether it varied as a function of game performance. For the fourth hypothesis (H4), we conducted mediation and moderated mediation analyses using OLS regression models in the TR group. This enabled a test of whether the association between ΔGoal-Adjustment and patient satisfaction in week 6 was mediated by P A C E S _ { a \nu e r a g e }for TR patients, and whether mediated effects varied as a function of game performance. We probed ΔGoal-Adjustment × game performance group membership and explored whether the indirect effect of ΔGoal-Adjustment on patient satisfaction in week 6 was mediated via P A C E S _ { a \nu e r a g e }and varied by game performance group membership. Patient satisfaction in week 1 was controlled in all models. Mediation was tested using the causal-steps approach, which involves examining coefficients at each step (Baron & Kenny, 1986), and the bootstrapping of indirect-effects approach (Hayes, 2017). The bootstrap sample was set at 5000, and the significant confidence interval was set at 95 per cent. A bootstrapped 95 per cent CI that did not contain zero indicated a significant indirect effect (Hayes, 2017). In addition, to capture the temporal sequence among the variables used for Hypothesis 4 testing, we conducted a supplementary analysis using the week 1 PACES measure as a mediator between ΔGoal-Adjustment and patient satisfaction in week 6, controlling for PACES baseline and week 1 patient satisfaction.

## Preliminary analysis

We conducted a preliminary analysis to examine the distribution of each variable of interest. The sample statistics for the variables of interest for both the in-clinic and TR therapy patients are provided in the Table S1. All variables of interest showed acceptable levels of variance and comparatively balanced distributions.

Table 1 shows bivariate correlations and descriptive statistics within the TR group for ΔGoal-Adjustment, the baseline PACES measure, P A C E S _ { a \nu e r a g e } , \Delta F u g l - M e y e r ,, patient satisfaction in week 1, patient satisfaction in week 6, and game performance among TR patients ( N = 6 2 ). Notable correlations included the positive association between ΔGoal-Adjustment and P A C E S _ { a \nu e r a g e } \left( r = . 3 0 , p = . 0 0 1 \right)), and the positive association between P A C E S _ { a \nu e r a g e }and game performance ( r = . 2 9 , p = . 0 2 3 ). These associations could reflect the general benefit of goal adjustment on motivational resources. ΔFugl-Meyer did not show any significant associations with other variables, supporting the results of the original study (Cramer et al., 2019).

## Game performance feedback and activity-inherent enjoyment (PACES)

We conducted an ANCOVA using the three groups of interest (in-clinic, TR high performers, and TR low performers) as the categorical independent variable and the continuous score of P A C E S _ { a \nu e r a g e }as the dependent variable. Patients' age and gender, as well as the baseline PACES measure, were controlled. The ANCOVA results showed that P A C E S _ { a \nu e r a g e }differed by group ( F ( 2 , 1 1 9 ) = 3 . 0 5, p = . 0 2 0 ).

Post hoc comparisons using Scheffé's method indicated that the mean P A C E S _ { a \nu e r a g e }score among TR low performers ( \mathbf { M } = 3 9 . 9 1 , S D = 6 . 1 2 )was significantly lower than that of both TR high performers ( \mathbf { M } = 4 4 . 8 9 , S D = 1 0 . 2 2 ) , t ( 6 0 ) = - 2 . 3 3 , p = . 0 2 3 , d = . 5 9, and in-clinic patients ( \mathrm { M } = 4 4 . 7 6 ,, S D = 7 . 4 9 ) , t ( 9 1 ) = - 3 . 1 3 , p = . 0 0 2 , d = . 7 1. In other words, the TR low performers might have particularly struggled to maintain enjoyment of the physical

therapy activities compared with the other groups because they constantly received negative feedback on their game performance. This finding supported the first hypothesis (H1) and provided context for the unexpected result from the original study (i.e. lower PACES scores among TR patients) (Cramer et al., 2019).

## Motivational resources protected by goal adjustment

Hierarchical OLS regression analyses were performed to test the second hypothesis (H2). Specifically, treatment group membership (TR vs. in-clinic) was examined as a moderator of the relationship between ΔGoal-Adjustment as a predictor and P A C E S _ { a \nu e r a g e }as an outcome variable (age, gender, and the baseline PACES measure were controlled). In the first step of the hierarchical multiple regression, ΔGoal-Adjustment and treatment group membership were entered into the model with covariates. The first model accounted for a significant amount of variance in P A C E S _ { a \nu e r a g e } ~ ( R ^ { 2 } = . 1 6 3, F ( 5 , 1 1 8 ) = 4 . 6 1 , p = . 0 0 1 )) and showed that both the main effects of ΔGoal-Adjustment ( \beta = . 1 8, p = . 0 3 2 )and treatment group membership ( \beta = - . 1 1 , p = . 0 4 4 )were significant. In the second step, the interaction term between ΔGoal-Adjustment and treatment group membership was entered into the model, and it accounted for a significant proportion of the variance in P A C E S _ { a \nu e r a g e } \left( \Delta R ^ { 2 } = . 0 3 0 \right., \Delta F ( 1 , 1 1 7 ) = 4 . 4 2 , p = . 0 3 8 ). Tests of the conditional effects of ΔGoal-Adjustment on P A C E S _ { a \nu e r a g e }differentiated by treatment group membership showed that ΔGoal-Adjustment positively predicted P A C E S _ { a \nu e r a g e }in the TR group ( \beta = . 5 3 , p = . 0 0 3 )), but not the in-clinic group ( \beta = - . 0 1 , p = . 9 3 8 )). The result was consistent with H2 and suggested that goal adjustment performed by the patients themselves benefited them in terms of preserving motivational resources (i.e. enjoyment of physical therapy activity).

## Goal adjustment of low versus high performers within the TR group

OLS regression analyses were also performed to test H3, which focused on patients in the TR group. Game performance was examined as a moderator of the relationship between ΔGoal-Adjustment as a predictor and P A C E S _ { a \nu e r a g e }as an outcome variable (age, gender, and the baseline measure of PACES were controlled). In the first step, ΔGoal-Adjustment and game performance group membership were included with covariates. The model accounted for a significant amount of variance in P A C E S _ { a \nu e r a g e }( R ^ { 2 } = . 2 5 7 , F ( 5 , 5 6 ) = 3 . 8 6 , p = . 0 0 4 )and showed that both the main effects of ΔGoal-Adjustment ( \beta = . 3 3 , p = . 0 0 8 )and game performance group membership ( \beta = . 3 0 , p = . 0 1 4 )were significant. Next, the interaction term between ΔGoal-Adjustment and treatment group membership was created and added to the regression model, and it accounted for a significant proportion of the variance in P A C E S _ { a \nu e r a g e } \left( \Delta R ^ { 2 } = . 0 5 6 , \Delta F ( 1 , 5 5 ) = 4 . 4 8 , p = . 0 3 9 \right). Supporting H3, tests of the conditional effects of ΔGoal-Adjustment on P A C E S _ { a \nu e r a g e }differentiated by game performance group membership showed that ΔGoal-Adjustment positively predicted P A C E S _ { a \nu e r a g e }among the low-performing group ( \beta = . 9 0 , p = . 0 0 1 ), but not the high-performing group ( \beta = . 1 4 , p = . 5 7 2; Hayes, 2017) (Figure 2). This pattern illustrated that low-performing TR patients who adjusted their goals were better able to protect their motivational resources in the face of poor game score feedback.

## Motivational regulation and patient satisfaction for therapy within the TR group

H4 was tested using OLS regression models that assessed whether the relationship between ΔGoal-Adjustment and patient satisfaction in week 6 was mediated by P A C E S _ { a \nu e r a g e }within the TR group ( N = 6 2 ). We also tested whether mediated effects were moderated by game performance group membership (Baron & Kenny, 1986; Hayes, 2017). Age, gender, baseline PACES, and week 1 patient satisfaction were controlled to account for autoregressive effects.

First, we regressed the mediator on the focal predictor and found that ΔGoal-Adjustment significantly predicted P A C E S _ { a \nu e r a g e } \ ( \beta = . 3 2 , p = . 0 1 2 ). Second, we regressed the outcome on the mediator and focal predictor and found that higher P A C E S _ { a \nu e r a g e }predicted increased satisfaction with the therapy in week 6 ( \beta = . 5 7 , p < . 0 0 1 ). Third, we used a bootstrapping approach to test whether ΔGoal-Adjustment predicted satisfaction indirectly via P A C E S _ { a \nu e r a g e } .. Supporting H4, the standardized indirect effect of ΔGoal-Adjustment to week 6 patient satisfaction via P A C E S _ { a \nu e r a g e }was significant ( \beta = . 1 8 , \mathrm { C I s } = 0 . 0 4 \mathrm { - } 0 . 3 3 ). ΔGoal-Adjustment did not predict patient satisfaction ( \beta = . 1 4 , p = . 3 8 4 )when controlling for P A C E S _ { a \nu e r a g e } ., indicating a full mediation.

Lastly, we included an interaction term between ΔGoal-Adjustment and game performance membership to test whether the indirect effect of ΔGoal-Adjustment on week 6 satisfaction via PACESaverage differed by game performance membership. The index of moderated mediation calculated in accordance with Hayes (2015) method was −0.38, which was significantly different from zero ( \mathrm { C I s } = - 0 . 8 4to −0.09). Next, we proceeded to conditional tests of indirect effects. In the low performance condition ( N = 3 1 )), the standardized indirect effect of ΔGoal-Adjustment to week 6 patient satisfaction via P A C E S _ { a \nu e r a g e }was significant ( \beta = . 4 4 , \mathrm { C I s } = 0 . 1 7 – 0 . 7 3 ). In this condition, ΔGoal-Adjustment did not predict patient satisfaction ( \beta = . 1 4 , p = . 3 8 4 )when controlling for P A C E S _ { a \nu e r a g e }and game performance, implying that the relationship was fully mediated (Figure 3). In the high game performance condition (N = 31), the standardized indirect effect of ΔGoal-Adjustment on week 6 patient satisfaction was not significant ( \beta = . 0 6 , \mathrm { C I s } = - 0 . 3 1 \mathrm { t o } 0 . 2 4 ).

A Monte Carlo approach was used to calculate the power of moderated mediation model ( N = 6 2 )(Schoemann et al., 2017). The result yielded statistical power of 0.86 (20,000 random samples drawn from each of 1000 replications, 95% CI). The result indicated that the non-significant mediation in high game performance condition was unlikely to be due to a type II error.

## Supplementary analysis of temporal sequence

In order to temporally separate the predictor, mediator, and outcome variables in the hypothesised mediation sequence (Hypothesis 4), supplemental regression models were conducted. These models assessed the indirect effect from baseline ΔGoal Adjustment to week 6 patient satisfaction via week 1 PACES. Models also tested whether the conditional indirect effects differed for those with low versus high game performance. Results were consistent with the main findings. ΔGoal-Adjustment significantly predicted week 1 PACES level ( \beta = . 3 2 , p = . 0 0 8 )), and week 1 PACES level predicted higher satisfaction with the therapy in week 6 \left( \beta = . 5 0 , p < . 0 0 1 \right) \left( N = 6 2 \right). Furthermore, when an interaction term between ΔGoal-Adjustment and game performance membership was included in the mediation

model, the index of moderated mediation was −0.37 (CIs: −0.89 to −0.08), indicating that the indirect effect was significantly moderated by game performance membership. As with the main analyses, the indirect effect of ΔGoal-Adjustment on week 6 patient satisfaction mediated by week 1 PACES was only significant (β = .44, CIs = 0.17–0.75) in the low game performance condition (N = 31).
