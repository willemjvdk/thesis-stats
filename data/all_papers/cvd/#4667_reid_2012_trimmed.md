# Methods
## Setting and participants

From December 2004 to December 2007, potential study participants were identified by study coordinators during an index hospitalization at two tertiary-care cardiac centres in Canada (University of Ottawa Heart Institute, Ottawa; London Health Sciences Centre, London). All patients admitted for acute coronary syndromes who: underwent successful percutaneous coronary revascularization; were not planning on enrolling in cardiac rehabilitation; had internet access at home or work; and were 20-80 years of age, were candidates for inclusion. Patients were ineligible if they: underwent coronary artery bypass surgery; had an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator; had NYHA Class III or IV heart failure; did not speak and read English; or were unwilling or unable to provide signed informed consent. The protocol was approved by the institutional Research Ethics Committees and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

The coordinator abstracted information from the patient charts regarding age, gender, cardiac history, comorbidities, and reasons for hospital admission. All participants completed a baseline questionnaire regarding their education, leisure-time physical activity,9 and heart disease health-related quality of life.10

## Randomization, blinding, and interventions

Participants were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to CardioFit or usual care using a random sequence that was computer generated by a statistical consultant in blocks of 4, 8, and 10. Sequences were generated for Ottawa and London and placed in sealed, numbered envelopes to ensure that treatment allocation was concealed until after baseline data collection. Research coordinators allocated the next available number on study entry (while the participant was still hospitalized). The research coordinator notified each participant of their allocated intervention immediately after the baseline assessment was complete. Research assistants, blinded to the participants’ treatment allocation, conducted follow-up assessments.

## Description of interventions

CardioFit intervention. Participants in the CardioFit group were visited in hospital by an exercise specialist, who presented a personally tailored physical activity plan generated by the CardioFit system, and instructions on how to access the CardioFit website. Following hospital discharge, participants were asked to log their daily activity on the CardioFit website and complete a series of five online tutorials (at weeks 2, 4, 8, 14, and 20). Each online tutorial took between 10 and 20 minutes to complete. Following each tutorial, a new physical activity plan was developed. Between tutorials, participants received emails from the exercise specialist providing motivational feedback on their progress. Participants were able to email the exercise specialist questions concerning their programme. The CardioFit website and tutorials were developed with expert advice from cardiologists, exercise specialists, and behavioural scientists; the content was designed to foster behavioural capability, self-efficacy, social support, and realistic outcome expectations. Tutorials were organized to engage self-control processes including exercise planning, goal setting, monitoring and selfregulation, and relapse prevention. A detailed graphical depiction of the intervention is provided in the supplemental data.

Usual care intervention. The usual care group received physical activity guidance from their attending cardiologist and an education booklet.

# Outcomes and follow up
Physical activity levels were objectively measured via pedometer in all participants 6 and 12 months

following randomization. Self-reported leisure-time physical activity and heart disease health-related quality of life were assessed in all participants at the baseline and 6- and 12-month visits.

Pedometer measurement of physical activity. The primary outcome was the average number of steps per day measured by pedometer (Yamax DIGI-WALKER, Yamasa Tokei Keiki, Tokyo, Japan) over a 7-day period. The Digi-Walker has an accuracy within 1% error at walking speeds of 80 m/ min.11 During each follow-up visit, the research assistant measured the participant’s stride length, programmed the pedometer, and provided a log book to record pedometer data each day. Each participant wore the pedometer for a 9-day period; data from the first and last day were discarded. Each participant was provided with a pre-paid envelope to return the pedometer and log book. Guidelines for patients with CHD promote 30 minutes of at least moderate-intensity physical activity most days of the week12 in addition to usual daily activities. This equates to 8000–9000 steps per day.13

Self-reported leisure-time physical activity. A modified version of the Godin Leisure-Time Exercise Questionnaire9 was used to gather self-reported data concerning leisure-time physical activity. Participants were asked ‘How many days in a typical week in the past 3 months did you do moderate (e.g. fast walking, easy bicycling, easy swimming, dancing) exercise for at least 15 minutes at a time?’ and ‘On the days when you did moderate exercise, how much total time (in minutes) on average did you spend per day doing these moderate exercise(s)?’ The same two questions assessed the frequency and duration of vigorous (e.g. running, jogging) activities. The total minutes of moderate and vigorous exercise per week were calculated. The modified version of the questionnaire has been previously validated in this population by our group.14

Heart disease health-related quality of life. Heart disease health-related quality of life was measured using the 27-item MacNew instrument,10,15 which measures three quality of life domains (emotional, physical, and social) as well as global quality of life. Questions were answered on a 7-point scale, where ‘1’ represents the lowest quality of life and ‘7’ the highest. The global score was obtained by calculating the average response across all questions.15 Internal consistency and construct validity of the questionnaire have been assessed and found to be high.15 A 0.5-point difference has been established as the minimally important difference for the MacNew subscales and global score.10

## Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared between groups to identify any chance differences. Missing outcome values were replaced by multiple imputations after confirming that the data were missing at random. Covariates were examined to determine if they were significantly related with the outcomes. Significant covariates were then controlled for in the primary analyses. In the primary analysis, repeated measures analysis of covariance was used to compare differences in steps per day over the 7-day pedometer recording period between groups (CardioFit and usual care) over time (6 and 12 months). Similar analyses were performed for the secondary outcomes of leisure-time physical activity and heart disease health-related quality of life. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 17.0 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, Illinois).

Sample size. Sample size calculations indicated a final sample of 200 participants would give 80% power (a ¼ 0.05; 2-tailed) to detect a difference of 1000 steps per day measured by pedometer, assuming a standard deviation of 2500 steps.16 Other research has shown that the odds of having metabolic syndrome are 10% lower for each additional 1000 steps per day.17 A pilot test showed the mean number of steps per day in 34 patients with heart disease not receiving a physical activity intervention was 6612  2638 steps 6 months after hospital discharge.

# Results
## Patient flow and baseline characteristics

From December 2004 to December 2007, 589 patients met study eligibility criteria (Figure 1) and 223 (38.0%) consented to participate. Among consenting participants, 115 were randomized to CardioFit and 108 to usual care. Among these, 223 completed the baseline assessment; 154 completed the 6-month assessment and 153 completed the 12-month assessment. Baseline characteristics of randomized study participants are shown in Table 1. The mean age was 56.4 years, 84.3% were men, 14.3% were current smokers, and mean body mass index was 29.3 kg/m2 . Most participants were working full time (52.9%) or part time (10.3%) outside of the home. This was the first cardiac event for 64.6% of participants; a myocardial infarction precipitated the hospital admission in 29.3% of participants. The two study groups were similar when demographic and clinical factors and pre-hospitalization levels of leisure-time physical activity were compared (Table 1).

## Adherence to allocated intervention

All participants (100%) assigned to CardioFit completed the in-hospital visit with the exercise specialist and received their personally tailored physical activity plan. The mean number of online tutorials completed by CardioFit participants was 2.7 of a maximum five, and 61.7% of participants completed at least three of the five tutorials (pre-specified adherence threshold). Completion rates for the tutorials at 2, 4, 8, 14, and 20 weeks were 70, 64, 56, 47, and 43%, respectively. Thirty-seven CardioFit participants emailed the exercise specialist at least once and a total of 123 emails were received during the intervention period. Of those emailing the specialist at least once, the average number of emails was 3.3 (range 1-8). Subjects raised in email correspondence included: how to exercise, 30; technical difficulties, 30; clearance to resume exercise, 23; missed tutorials or logging of physical activity, 21; symptoms, 13; and other information unrelated to physical activity, 6.

## Effectiveness of CardioFit programme

Table 2 shows the effectiveness of the CardioFit programme expressed in terms of pedometer-measured steps per day over a 7-day period as well as selfreported leisure-time physical activity at 6- and 12-month follow up. Preliminary analyses showed that age, physical activity level prior to hospitalization, first hospitalization for CHD, and being employed were significantly related to pedometer outcomes at follow up; these variables were used as covariates for subsequent analyses of pedometer data. The repeated measures analysis of covariance for pedometer-measured steps per day showed a main effect only for group. The number of steps per day was higher in the CardioFit group compared to usual care.

Preliminary analyses showed that physical activity level prior to hospitalization, and first hospitalization for CHD were significantly related to self-reported physical activity at follow up; these variables were used as covariates for subsequent analyses related to self-reported activity. The repeated measures analysis of covariance for self-reported leisure-time physical activity showed a main effect only for group. Minutes of leisure physical activity were greater in the CardioFit group compared to usual care.

Table 3 shows the effectiveness of the CardioFit programme expressed in terms of heart disease healthrelated quality of life. Preliminary analyses showed that the number of comorbidities was significantly related to heart disease health-related quality of life at follow up; this variable was used as a covariate for subsequent analyses related to this variable. For global

heart disease health-related quality of life, group, time, and group  time interaction effects were not significant. For the emotional and physical subscales of the MacNew survey instrument, a significant group effect emerged; participants in CardioFit had higher quality of life scores than usual care across time. For the social subscale of the MacNew survey instrument, group, time, and grouptime interaction effects were not significant.

## Adverse events

During the study period, two participants (both usual care) died, one participant (in usual care) underwent coronary artery bypass surgery surgery, and 10 other participants (six in usual care and four in CardioFit) were rehospitalized with chest pain.
