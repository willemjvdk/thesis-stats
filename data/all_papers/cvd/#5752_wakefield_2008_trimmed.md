## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# Materials and Methods
The study employed a randomized controlled clinical trial design. The study took place at the Iowa City Veterans Affairs Medical Center (ICVAMC), a 107-bed tertiary care referral center affiliated with the University of Iowa. The study protocol was approved by the University of Iowa Institutional Review Board (IRB), which serves as the IRB for the ICVAMC.

Between July 2002 and September 2005, trained nurses screened patients within 24 hours of admission to identify potential participants. Patients were selected for screening based on documentation of possible HF exacerbation as the reason for admission (e.g., patients admitted for HF, volume overload, and pulmonary edema). In addition to hospital admission for HF, eligibility criteria included a telephone line in the home; absence of significant vision, hearing, or other communication deficits; enrolled in the ICVAMC Primary Care Clinic; and English speaking. Patients who were cognitively impaired, resided in a long-term care facility, or who were discharged to a long-term care facility were excluded.

The sample size estimate was based on the expected reduction in 3-month readmission rates. For a two-tailed test, with a significance level set at 0.05, a sample size of 165 (55 subjects in each of three groups) would be required to provide 80% power to detect a decrease of 0.23 in readmission rates (from 43% to 20%). The estimated reduction in readmission rate was based on previous work8,9 and the readmission rate for patients at the study hospital at the time of study initiation (43%).

## RANDOMIZATION AND INTERVENTION

The project coordinator prepared sealed envelopes containing group assignments in blocks of 24. Following informed consent and baseline data collection, study nurses opened the envelope to assign subjects to one of three treatment conditions: usual care, telephone follow-up, or videophone follow-up.

The intervention, modeled on previous work, was consistent with American Heart Association guidelines.7 Medical center staff gave all subjects the usual discharge teaching (i.e., no special discharge instructions were provided for the intervention subjects). Follow-

up clinic appointments were scheduled in the usual manner for all subjects. Usual care subjects contacted their primary care nurse case manager by telephone if needed (all patients at the Medical Center are provided telephone numbers for their assigned nurse case manager). Intervention subjects contacted their assigned study nurse if needed after discharge.

Two registered nurses conducted all intervention contacts. Intervention patients were contacted three times the first week after discharge, and then weekly for 11 weeks (14 contacts over 3 months). Intervention subjects were given a symptom review checklist to help them report HF-related symptoms, a scale, blood pressure cuff, and tape measure and were instructed to measure daily weights, blood pressure, and ankle circumference to monitor fluid accumulation. Study nurses reviewed the discharge plan during the first intervention contact and reinforced it during subsequent contacts.13 During all intervention contacts, study nurses assessed subjects using the symptom review checklist. When subjects reported symptoms, nurses reviewed their data, reinforced the plan of care, and made referrals (e.g., dietitian) or contacted subject’s physician for care plan adjustments. During the contacts, study nurses had full access to subjects’ medical records through the Computerized Patient Record System (CPRS).

Study nurses also employed strategies to improve subjects’ compliance with prescribed treatment plans.13–15 This included behavioral skill training strategies (e.g., reviewing skills for monitoring blood pressure, and recommending use of a grocery list to purchase lowsodium food). Self-monitoring strategies were encouraged (e.g., keeping a daily record of weight and food intake). Self-efficacy enhancement strategies included encouraging use of community support groups; encouragement and feedback; and realistic goal setting. External cognitive strategies included encouraging use of aids such as meal plans and medication organizers.

## HOME TELECARE EQUIPMENT

For the subjects assigned to the telephone group, all intervention contacts were conducted using their personal telephone in their home.

Subjects in the videophone group used a system that consisted of a television monitor and a camera kit with a microphone and video camera (EHC 200 Sentinel Patient Station, manufactured by CyberCare Technologies, Boyton Beach, FL). The system had full duplex audio video transmission at 10–15 frames per second (FPS) (30 FPS is considered “broadcast quality”) with a resolution of 176 × 144 pixels. The unit connected through a standard telephone line using a 33.6K modem and consisted of a 13-inch

color television–type monitor, camera, and remote control device. The remote control’s large green “Start” launched the system and large red “End call” button turned it off. To establish contact, the study nurse activated the system by entering a patient’s telephone number. The patient at home responded by pushing the “Start” button on the remote control. With the system activated on both ends, the nurse and patient saw and spoke to each other during the contact. At the end of the interaction, the patient pressed the “End call” button.

During the enrollment period, CyberCare Technologies went out of business, and we contracted for newer, more reliable equipment. The TeleVyou 500SP videophone (distributed by Wind Currents Technology, Woodstock, NY) is a telephone with a built-in speaker phone, and a 5-inch color video screen. Like the CyberCare model, this phone has a resolution of 176 × 144 pixels and transmits video at 15 FPS with full duplex audio. Establishing contact is the same as using a regular telephone. The videophones were installed and removed by a research assistant, who trained subjects on their use.

## MEASURES

Demographic data included age, gender, race, education, and marital status. Data on HF severity were collected from the medical record, and included left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), length of time diagnosed with HF, and prior revascularization. Subjects were also asked how long they had been diagnosed with HF. Study nurses, trained by a cardiologist, assigned an admission New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification.

## RESOURCE USE

Resource use included readmission rate, time to first readmission, hospital days, and urgent care clinic visits during the 12-month follow-up period. These data were collected from the VA Patient Treatment File (PTF), which contains records of VA hospital stays, the VA Outpatient Clinic File, which contains records of ambulatory VA clinic visits, and the CPRS. These data sources were searched for 12 months prior to and 12 months following study enrollment for utilization data.

## MORTALITY

Mortality for the 12-month follow-up period was obtained using multiple methods, including attempts to contact subjects for followup data collection; CPRS review; PTF data; and the Beneficiary Identification Records Locator Subsystem, which contains data on veterans known to be deceased.

## QUALITY OF LIFE

Disease-specific quality of life was measured at baseline, 3 months, and 6 months following study enrollment with the Minnesota Living with Heart Failure instrument (MLHF).16 This 21-item measure assesses subjective aspects of health status in patients with HF. Responses are measured on a 6-point Likert-type scale, ranging from 0 to 5, where 0 indicates HF did not affect that area and 5 indicates that area was “very much” affected by HF. Item scores are totaled for an overall score ranging from 0 to 105, with higher scores denoting perceptions of lower quality of life. On initial testing, the instrument had good reproducibility (weighted κ on readministration at 7–21 days = 0.84) and validity (correlation with a global rating of 0.80 and the NYHA functional class of 0.60).

## STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Baseline data were analyzed using descriptive statistics (means and frequencies). Continuous data across three groups (i.e., usual care, telephone, and videophone) were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Categorical data were analyzed using the χ2 statistic. Time to first readmission and risk of death were analyzed by fitting Kaplan-Meier survival curves and estimating 95% confidence intervals for the probability of readmission or death. Cox proportional hazards models were used to compare the experience of the study groups with respect to time to readmission and mortality while accounting for potential modifying variables. Data were analyzed comparing the three groups (usual care, telephone, and videophone) as well as comparing the usual care group to all intervention subjects (combined telephone and videophone). All data analyses were conducted using an intent-to-treat approach. Data were analyzed using SAS version 9.1 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

# Results
Baseline data are summarized in Table 1. There were no statistically significant differences among the three groups for baseline demographics (using one-way ANOVA or χ2 ). There were no significant differences across the three groups at enrollment for severity of illness measures (i.e., mean LVEF, length of time diagnosed with HF [record review or self-reported]; prior revascularization; NYHA class; index length of stay; or prior year admissions or urgent care visits). While no statistically significant differences existed across the three groups for baseline severity of illness, despite randomization the subjects in the videophone group scored worse on most severity of illness measures (i.e., LVEF, length of time diagnosed, and percent with NYHA classification III or IV).

## RECRUITMENT

Figure 1 illustrates the enrollment process. Over 39 months, 853 admissions (648 unique patients) were screened; 268 patients did not meet criteria for HF. Of the remaining 585 admissions (n = 460 patients), 244 individuals (42%) did not meet study inclusion criteria, including patients who did not have a “land line” telephone at home (cell phone only n = 2 0[3.4%] and no phone n = 10 [1.7%] of 585 admissions). The most common reasons for ineligibility included discharge to a long-term care facility (29%), cognitive impairment (14%), and vision problems (13%). For 63 admissions (18%), the patient was either discharged or died before being approached to enroll in the study, and for 130 admissions the patient refused participation. Thus, 43% \left( n = 1 4 8 \right)of all eligible patients were enrolled. Of those, 49 were randomized to usual care, 52 to videophone, and 47 to telephone follow-up. At 3 months, 85% \left( n = 1 2 6 \right)completed followup; at 6 months, 74% \left( n = 1 0 9 \right)completed follow-up.

## READMISSION

The primary endpoint was readmission up to 12 months after discharge. Because these data were collected from existing VA databases, data on all subjects were analyzed for readmissions, including those who survived to 12 months (n = 112) and those who died (36 subjects (24.3%) had died before the 12 month utilization analysis).

Readmission was first analyzed as a dichotomized variable (each subject as yes or no readmitted in 1 year). There were no

significant differences among the three groups in readmission rate, or between the control and combined intervention groups in the proportion of subjects readmitted at either 3 or 6 months following study enrollment. However, a significantly lower proportion of combined intervention subjects were readmitted at 12 months following enrollment (59% in the control group versus 41% in the intervention

group; odds ratio \mathrm { [ O R ] } = 0 . 4 9 ;95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.24, 0.98; p = 0.04).

Readmissions were then categorized as to whether HF was the primary or a secondary diagnosis (using ICD-9 codes from medical records). A total of 70 subjects (n = 20 telephone; n = 21 video; n = 29 control) had at least one admission with HF as the primary (17%)

or secondary (44%) diagnosis during the 12-month follow-up period. The rest (39%) were not treated for HF. There were no significant differences across the three groups for HF-related readmissions. A lower, but nonsignificant, percentage of the combined intervention group was treated for HF (primary or secondary) (n = 23 of 41; 56%) compared to the control group (n = 20 of 29; 69%) (OR = 0.58; 95% CI: 0.21, 1.56; p = 0.28).

The time to first readmission up to 12 months following enrollment was analyzed by fitting Kaplan-Meier survival curves and estimating 95% CIs for probability of readmission. There were no significant

differences across the three groups in this analysis. However, when comparing the control and combined intervention groups, intervention subjects had a significantly lower risk of readmission (log rank test p = 0.02) (Fig. 2).A Cox proportional hazards model, which included baseline age, mean LVEF, NYHA classification, and MLHF, suggested that the combined intervention group had a significantly lower risk of all-cause readmission (hazard ratio [HR]= 0.54; 95% CI: 0.33, 0.90; p = 0.02) (Table 2).

## OTHER UTILIZATION

There were no statistically significant differences in the mean number of hospital days or the mean number of urgent care visits among the three groups, or between the combined intervention and control groups.

## MORTALITY

During the 3-month intervention period, the crude mortality rate was higher in the usual care (control) group (6.1%) compared to the telephone (2.1%) and videophone (3.8%) groups. At 12 months, the crude mortality rate was higher in the videophone group (28.9%) compared to the control (22.4%) and telephone (21.3%) groups. However, these differences were not statistically significant. A Cox proportional hazards model comparing usual care with the combined intervention group, including baseline age, mean LVEF, NYHA classification, and MLHF was not significant for mortality at 12 months (HR = 1.04; 95% CI: 0.49, 2.24; p = 0.91) (Table 2). Thus, there was no evidence of an intervention effect on mortality.

## INTERVENTION DOSE AND TECHNICAL ISSUES

The number of intervention contacts completed for all subjects was analyzed. The maximum number of contacts possible was 14. On average, the telephone group received 80% (11.4) and the videophone group received 75% (10.5) of the possible contacts. The lower completion rate in the videophone group may be due to the higher mortality rate among those subjects. Of intervention subjects who completed the 90-day intervention period (n = 82), the mean number of completed contacts was slightly more than 13 for both groups. One reason for the fewer-than-expected contacts is that the study nurses sometimes found it difficult to schedule and complete the planned three contacts in the first week postdischarge. Finally, on average 2.6 planned videophone contacts per patient were replaced using telephone contacts, due to technical issues, such as the inability to connect by videophone or transitory problems with video resolution.
