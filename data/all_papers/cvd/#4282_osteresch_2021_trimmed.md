# Methods
The study design of the IPP trial has been published before.7 Briefly, all patients hospitalized for acute STEMI or NSTEMI were screened for eligibility at two large German heart centers. Exclusion criteria were inability to participate in a prevention program (i.e., due to language barrier), <18 or >75 years of age and any major non-cardiac condition that would adversely affect survival during the duration of the study. In addition, patients with coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) in the first 30 days after MI were excluded because these patients mostly have a different early rehabilitation phase compared to those with interventional treatment; for instance, in terms of timing of physical activity programs. In order to investigate a homogenous study cohort in the IPP trial, patients with CABG in the first 30 days were excluded from the trial.

One month after discharge, after 3-weeks acute cardiac rehabilitation that belongs to the standard of care in Germany, patients were revisited and assigned to IPP for 12 months or to UC by 1:1 randomization. IPP was coordinated by non-physician “prevention assistants” that were supervised by cardiologists. The prevention program included group education sessions every month, personal telephone contacts every 3 weeks, clinical visits to intervene, if risk factors did not meet the guideline-recommended targets, and telemetric devices with online documentation of physical activity. Global cardiovascular risk factor control was assessed by the IPP Prevention Score, a sum score that evaluates six major risk factors (Table 1). The maximal score counts 15 points if

all risk factors reach guideline-recommended targets, the minimal score counts 0 points. Further study endpoints were single risk factors, medical treatment, serious clinical events, costs and quality of life.

All patients were invited to come to a personal follow-up visit at the study center 24 months after randomization to evaluate risk factor control, medical treatment, serious clinical events, costs and quality of life. If at least one risk factor did not meet the guideline-recommended targets at the 24-months visit, the patients of the IPP group were asked to participate in the randomized substudy “Prevention Boost

Trial.” In this substudy, patients were randomly assigned to a 2-months reintervention (“Prevention Boost”) versus no reintervention. The reintervention was again performed by non-physician “prevention assistants” supervised by study cardiologists. During the 2-months reintervention patients were reeducated regarding cardiovascular risk factors, such as physical inactivity or smoking. At the 36-months study visit, risk factor control and medical treatment were assessed to evaluate efficacy of the reintervention.

The trial was approved by the competent ethical committees; study design was registered under ClinicalTrials.gov (identifer NCT01896765).

Baseline characteristics were given as mean values standard deviation or medians and quartiles for continuous variables as well as absolute numbers and percentages for categorical variables. Data were tested for normal distribution (distribution analysis, Kolmogorov-Smirnov test). Comparisons were performed using the t test (parametric variables), Wilcoxon test (non-parametric variables) as well as the Mann-Whitney-U test (inter-group comparisons). To compare risk factors in the IPP versus UC group after 24 months (Table 2) as well as risk factors in the reintervention versus no reintervention group after 36 months (Table 3) additional logistic regression models were performed to adjust p values for multiple comparisons. Kaplan-Meier curves and log-rank testing were performed to analyze event-free survival in patients with IPP compared to UC (Figure 2). For economic analysis a back of the envelope calculation of the costs of IPP compared to potential costs of adverse events from a German payer’s perspective was carried out (Figure 3). The costs by adverse events were estimated using the reimbursement catalogue for hospital admissions as well as outpatient costs of interventions. In all statistical analyses p values < 0.05 were considered as significant. Statistics were performed by using SPSS (IBM, version 22).

# Results
Out of 310 initially randomized patients, 24-months follow-up was obtained in 275 patients (88.7%): 136 patients (87.7%) of the IPP group versus 139 patients (89.7%) of UC group. 5 patients (2 patients IPP group, 3 patients UC group) died during the study course, so that 24-months visits were performed in 134 patients of the IPP group and 136 patients of the UC group, respectively. Patients that refused 24-months follow-up (n = 35) were younger than patients with 24-months follow-up; however, there were no significant differences regarding sex and comorbidities. Baseline characteristics were balanced between patients assigned to the IPP group versus the UC group (Supplemental Table 1).

Risk factor control improved during the 3-week acute cardiac rehabilitation directly after MI (Figure 1). Thereafter, patients that were randomized to the IPP group further improved risk factor control during the following 12 months: IPP Prevention Score increased by 11.5% from 1 0 . 4 \pm 2 . 1points to 1 1 . 6 \pm 2 . 2points at 12 months ( \mathtt { p } <0.01). After termination of IPP at 12 months, a slight decrease of the IPP Prevention Score to 1 0 . 9 \pm 2 . 3points at 24 months was observed. In patients assigned to the UC group a continous decrease of risk factor control was observed between randomization (at the end of acute cardiac rehabilitation) and 24-months visit (Figure 1). After 24 months the IPP Prevention Score in the UC group was 18.5% lower than in the IPP group ( 9 . 2 \pm 2 . 3 points vs 10.9 § 2.3 points, \mathsf { p } < 0 . 0 1 \ ). The risk factors current smoking,

LDL cholesterol, physical inactivity, blood pressure and body mass index showed similar trends during 24-months course (Table 2): Risk factor control improved during acute cardiac rehabilitation directly after MI (an exception was blood pressure that is often low at time of MI) and a further improvement of risk factors was observed in the IPP group during the following 12 months. In contrast, a continous deterioration of the risk factors was observed in the UC group between randomization (at the end of acute cardiac rehabilitation) and 24-months visit (Table 2). At 24 months LDL cholesterol, physical activity and blood pressure were on a significantly worse level in the UC group compared to IPP in univariate and multivariate comparison.

In the IPP group a significantly better use of preventive medical therapy was observed compared to UC (Supplemental Table 2). In the first 12 months after randomization lipid lowering medication was increased more often in patients with IPP \mathrm { \dot { ( p < 0 . 0 5 ) } }; reduction of lipid lowering and blood pressure lowering medication was observed less often in the IPP group compared to the UC group ( \mathfrak { p } <0.05). No significant differences between the groups were observed anymore after termination of the prevention program at 12 months.

Two patients (1.5%) in the IPP group versus 3 patients (2.2%) in the UC group ( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { p } } = 1 . 0 )died between randomization and 24-months visit. In the IPP group a lower rate of the combined endpoint of serious clinical events (death, resuscitations, strokes, reinfarctions, revascularizations or hospitalizations due to unstable angina) compared to UC was observed (IPP: n = 17, 12.5%; UC: n = 29, 20.9%), resulting in a strong statistical trend of higher event-free survival rates in the IPP group compared to UC after 24 months (log-rank test \mathrm { p } = 0 . 0 6, Figure 2).

IPP over 12 months costs € 66,775 in total and € 491 per patient, respectively, including personal costs of the prevention assistant and the supervising physician (Figure 3). Analyzing costs of serious adverse events in both groups over 24 months, IPP was associated with € 78,768 in total and € 579 per patient for serious adverse events, while UC was associated with € 162,603 in total and € 1,170 per patient. In total, in the IPP group €1,070 incurred per patient versus € 1,170 per patient in the UC group, indicating that already after 24 months the cost savings by reduced events outweighted the costs of the 12-months prevention program.
