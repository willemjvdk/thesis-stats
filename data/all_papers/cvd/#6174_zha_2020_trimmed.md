## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# Method
## Study Design

This pilot study used a randomized controlled trial (RCT) to examine the effect of an mHealth intervention on four patient outcomes: (a) changes in systolic and diastolic BP, (b) adherence in monitoring BP, (c) perceived medication adherence self-efficacy, and (d) self-reported HRQOL from baseline to 6 months after study enrollment. Participants were 30 patients with diagnosed uncontrolled hypertension who lived in an underserved urban community. Julious (2005) and van Belle (2008) suggested that 12 participants in a health-related field pilot study are sufficient to assess changes in the study variables. Accordingly, a total of 30 participants were recruited and randomly assigned either to the mHealth group (mHealth plus standard care) or to the standard follow-up group (standard care group).

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Rutgers University (Pro20150001419) and was registered with www.ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02632838).

## Study Setting

The study took place at a local community health center in Newark, NJ. Newark is the largest city in New Jersey with a

population of 277,140, which is predominantly Black/ African American (52%) and Hispanic (33%). Newark has a high poverty rate (28%) compared with New Jersey overall (11%) (U.S. Census Bureau, 2016) and is considered a medically underserved location with a vulnerable population. In addition, the Newark population has high prevalence rates of chronic diseases such as hypertension and type II diabetes; 63% of Newark adults had a diagnosis of hypertension in 2014 (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2016). The study site is a nurse-managed, community-directed health center providing primary care to approximately 4,000 residents of four public housing units in the East Ward of Newark. The majority of residents are low-income Black/ African Americans, with fewer than 20% Hispanics.

## Study Intervention

The iHealth BP7 Wireless Blood Pressure Wrist Monitor (iHealth Lab Inc.), a Food and Drug Administration (FDA) 510K approved fully automatic wrist-cuff BP monitor was used as the study intervention. The monitor uses the oscillometric principle to measure BP and pulse rate with a Bluetooth synchronization system that can interphase with both Apple and Android devices (smartphone or tablet). There are no physical risks associated with using the monitor. This monitor complies with the international protocol of the European Society of Hypertension and is recommended for self-measurement of BP in adult populations (Wang, Zhao, Chen, Li, & Wan, 2014). The monitor is paired with an iHealth MyVitals mobile app (iHealth Labs Inc., 2012) to test, track, and share BP data. iHealth MyVitals is a free mobile application that can be downloaded from the Apple Store and Google Play. The application automatically tracks and analyzes key health vital measurements. The measurements taken by the monitor are automatically synchronized and stored in an easy-to-read format on the iHealth MyVitals application. Patients receive instant feedback, which allows them to see how they are doing and set goals for ongoing improvement.

With each monitor, the patient received a free cloud service account, where BP data were stored and securely backed up. The patient and their health care provider could log into the cloud account from any device and access the information, irrespective of where the measurement was taken. Thus, patients were able to self-monitor and manage their BP. Health care providers created master accounts through which they could access data from all users under their care, allowing them to monitor their patients’ BP remotely and intervene in a timely fashion (see Figure 1).

# Outcome Measures
BP measurement. For all participants, their BP was measured during a weekly office visit. The patient sat in an upright position with feet flat on the floor, resting for at least 5 min before

BP was measured (Bakris, 2016) using the Welch Allyn Spot Vital Signs LXi (Welch Allyn, Inc., Skaneateles Falls, NY, USA) electronic BP monitor. For each participant, BP was measured at least once in each arm, separated by 5 min. Systolic BP, diastolic BP, and pulse were measured and recorded by the community health center nurses or community health workers. For the study, the average of the BP readings was recorded at baseline, 3 months, and 6 months.

BP monitoring adherence. BP monitoring adherence for the mHealth group was calculated in two ways. For the first measure, the total number of times the monitor was used to measure BP was divided by the total number of expected times it should have been used (one BP measurement performed every day for 6 months) at home. For the second measure, the total number of office visits to measure BP was divided by the total number of expected visits (one office BP measurement every week for 6 months). BP monitoring adherence for the standard follow-up group was calculated by the second measure that was used for the mHealth group.

Perceived medication adherence self-efficacy. Perceived medication adherence self-efficacy was utilized as a proxy to evaluate patient self-efficacy in this study. Perceived medication adherence self-efficacy was assessed with the Medication Adherence Self-Efficacy Scale (MASES). The MASES was developed to measure several aspects of selfefficacy, including adherence to prescribed medication for the management of hypertension among African American

patients. The scale has 26 items rated on a 3-point Likerttype scale; a total mean score is calculated by averaging responses on all items. Higher scores indicate a greater level of medication adherence self-efficacy (Ogedegbe, Mancuso, Allegrante, & Charlson, 2003). The Cronbach’s alpha for the entire 26-item scale was .95 (Ogedegbe et al., 2003).

HRQOL. The Medical Outcomes Study 36-item Short-Form Health Survey (SF-36) was used to assess overall HRQOL. The SF-36 has been widely used to measure HRQOL in hypertensive patients (Cote, Gregoire, Moisan, & Chabot, 2004). It consists of the sum scores of eight subscales (physical functioning, physical role limitations, mental health, emotional role limitations, social functioning, vitality, pain, and general health perceptions). The eight subscales are then summarized into two composite scores (physical and psychological quality of life). Scores on the SF-36 generally range from 20 to 60, with higher scores representing increased HRQOL (Maruish, 2011). Numerous studies have demonstrated the reliability of the SF-36, with scores exceeding 0.80 (McHorney, Ware, Lu, & Sherbourne, 1994).

## Study Procedure

Recruitment and consent. Study participants were recruited using flyers and direct contact with nurses and community health workers at the health center. Potential participants were invited to attend an information session conducted by the research team. Informed consent and Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) authorization were obtained from 30 eligible patients who opted to participate in the study.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria. Participants were eligible to participate if they were (a) between 18 and 64 years of age, (b) resided in one of the four public housing units in the community, (c) had been diagnosed with uncontrolled hypertension (BP measured at 140/90 mmHg or higher for either systolic or diastolic pressure at five separate times during a 2-month period), (d) on antihypertension medication, and (e) owned and used a compatible Apple (iOS version 5.0 or higher) or Android (with operating system 3.0 or later) device. For the mHealth intervention, the application required a newer Apple or Android smartphone or tablet device for compatibility. In addition, as the application interface was only in English, participants needed to be able to read and speak English. Exclusion criteria included (a) pregnant women and (b) patients with a serious arrhythmia or preeclampsia.

Procedure. Half (n = 15) of the participants were randomly assigned to the mHealth group and half (n = 15) to the standard follow-up group. Both groups followed the protocol in place at the community health center for management of their hypertension, which consists of a weekly nursing assessment, medication management, patient education, and

follow-up. Study participants were followed for a period of 6 months, from May to November 2016. The mHealth group was instructed to wear the monitor and measure their BP at the same time each day at home in accordance with the instructions that they had received. Furthermore, all study participants completed the SF-36 and the MASES at baseline and 6 months after study enrollment.

After study enrollment, the research team provided three training sessions for the mHealth group on how to use the monitor correctly (as described by the manufacturer) and how to monitor their BP on their smartphone. The iHealth MyVitals app was downloaded on participants’ smartphones and iHealth cloud accounts were created. Two nurses and six community health workers received training on how to use the monitor and access data from the iHealth cloud service master account. The principal investigator and research assistants followed up on both groups through weekly home visits or telephone calls to ensure adherence to the study protocol during the study period. The principal investigator participated in 50% of the home visits and telephone follow-up to assure fidelity of the study.

## Data Analysis

Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 24.0 (IBM Inc., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for data analysis. All statistical analyses were two-sided. A p-value of ≤.05 was considered statistically significant. The nonparametric Friedman test was used to test whether the systolic and diastolic BP changed significantly at baseline, 3 months, and 6 months for both the mHealth group and the standard follow-up group. Wilcoxon signed rank tests were used as post hoc tests when the Friedman test found statistically significant changes among the groups. The nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the values of MASES scores and SF-36 scores at baseline and 6 months for both groups.

# Results
## Demographic Characteristics

Of the 30 patients recruited for the study, five withdrew because of discomfort in using the device (n = 2), loss of mobile phone (n = 1), and a decision to discontinue participation (n = 2). Table 1 presents the demographic characteristics of the 25 participants who completed the study. The majority of participants were women (83% in the mHealth group vs. 92% in the standard follow-up group) with an average age of 48.9 ± 8.00 and 55.5 ± 5.20 years, respectively (p = .40). All 12 participants in the mHealth group were African American. The standard follow-up group had 12 African American participants and 1 Hispanic. Study participants between the two groups did not differ significantly in their demographic characteristics.

# BP Outcomes
Table 2 shows that for the mHealth group, the Friedman test analysis revealed a statistically significant difference in systolic BP over 6 months, \chi ^ { 2 } ( 2 , n = 1 2 ) = 8 . 5 5 , p = . 0 1. Post hoc tests using the Wilcoxon signed ranks test indicated that the systolic BP decreased significantly from baseline when compared with results at 3 months (z = −2.20, p = .02, r = 0.44, medium effect size) and 6 months (z = −2.52, p = .01, r = 0.51, medium effect size). However, there was no significant change in systolic BP at 3 and 6 months (z = −1.42, p = .15). There was no significant change in diastolic BP over 6 months, \chi ^ { 2 } ( 2 , n = 1 2 ) = 1 . 0 2 , p = . 6 0.

Among the standard follow-up group (Table 2), there was no significant change in systolic BP over 6 months, χ²(2, n = 1 3 ) = 3 . 5 7 , p = . 1 7, or in diastolic BP over 6 months, χ²(2, n = 13) = 0.82, p = .66. Overall, both groups showed slightly linear reductions in systolic and diastolic BP over 6 months. However, there was no statistically significant difference in systolic and diastolic BP between the two groups at baseline, 3 months, and 6 months.

## BP Monitoring Adherence

Monitoring adherence of BP was measured by examining the rate of home measurements and rate of office visits for the mHealth group. Only office visits were measured for the standard follow-up group. The BP monitoring adherence rate for the mHealth group was 71% (1,567 out of 2,208 measures at home), whereas the BP monitoring adherence rate at office visits was 79% (227 out of 288 visits) over 6 months. The adherence rate of the standard follow-up group was 65% (203 out of 312 office visits). The mHealth group demonstrated higher BP monitoring adherence across the study period compared with the standard follow-up group.

## Perceived Medication Adherence Self-Efficacy

Table 3 shows the results from the MASES. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two study

groups at baseline, with mean scores of 64.85 and 64.75 respectively ( p \ : = \ : . 9 8 ). At 6 months, no statistical significance between the two groups was found; however, the mHealth group had higher scores on the MASES (mean scores of 69.17 and 61.00, respectively; p = . 0 6 ). Scores on the MASES may be used to infer change even when the results are not significant (Ogedegbe et al., 2003). This might suggest that the mHealth group was more likely to have greater adherence in taking prescribed antihypertensive medications and consequently experienced better hypertension control.
