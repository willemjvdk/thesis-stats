# Methods
## Patient population

Five hundred and three patients over 15 months were screened for eligibility (Figure 1). Subjects were screened at time of CR referral and again (if adherent to CR) at approximately CR session #28. Major inclusion criteria included age ≥18 years old, completing 36 on-site CR sessions, clinical stability allowing safe home exercise and a CPX test, ownership of a smartphone and not currently using a physical activity tracker. A majority of patients who did not meet inclusion criteria were screened out at time of the CR referral (eg, did not own a smartphone, medical limitations prohibiting a safe or independent exercise or CPX test). One hundred and thirty-three eligible patients

chose not to attend CR or dropped-out of CR before contact. Other reasons for exclusion are listed in Figure 1. After screening, 32 patients were recruited and randomized from Duke's CR program. All subjects provided written consent prior to participation under research protocols approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Duke University Medical Center. The study design was listed on clinicaltrials.gov (registration number NCT02431546) prior to initiation of the study and partially funded by Vida Health, Inc, San Francisco, CA. Patients were contacted and consented approximately two weeks before their anticipated CR graduation date (for Medicare, 36 sessions). All subjects were adherent to CR and on a stable regimen of medicine throughout CR. Patient characteristics are shown in Table I. During the 12 week intervention, there were five drop-outs due to a patient falling on ice and causing a knee injury; a diagnosis of a rare blood disease; a patient sustaining a severe wound due to a fishing hook accident; and two patients were lost to follow-up and did not respond to attempt at communication. In addition, one patient had their implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) re-set such that it did not allow a true max measure of peak VO . Last, one patient failed to give a good effort during the CPX test as evidenced by abruptly stopping with no symptoms, low rate of perceived exertions, low respiratory exchange ratios and admitting they could have walked much longer. Therefore, data analyses were performed on 25 patients (16 mHealth and 9 usual care).

## Pre-randomization testing and randomization

In an effort to best capture the physical activity patterns of a compliant CR patient, we provided patients a Fitbit Charge (Fitbit, Inc, San Francisco, CA) device and instruction on its use. Patients wore the device during the last two weeks of their CR program. This enabled the measurement of physical activity that included two to three supervised CR sessions of 30–60 min of aerobic exercise per week and their daily activities outside of CR. Prior to randomization, patients also had a physical exam by a physician, a maximal CPX test on a treadmill (see details below), and completed the Morisky Medication Adherence Questionnaire and the Seattle Angina Questionnaire.27,28

Following the completion of baseline testing, patients were randomized 3:1 into either a mHealth or usual care group. A 3:1 randomization

was used to enable a usual care comparison while maximizing limited resources to perform the mHealth intervention. Patients receiving mHealth continued to wear their Fitbit activity trackers and were given an exercise prescription by daily step count based on their last two weeks of CR (see details below). In addition, mHealth patients received health coaching for the duration of the study (see details below). Participants randomized to the usual care group followed standard care as ordered by their treating physician. Participants in the usual care group did not receive any specific lifestyle recommendations from study personnel, but were encouraged to follow the guidance provided to them by their physician.

## Cardiopulmonary exercise test (CPX)

Prior to randomization and at 12 weeks, subjects underwent a maximal treadmill CPX test with continuous 12-lead electrocardiogram and expired gas analysis. Expired gases were analyzed continuously using a TrueMax 2400 Metabolic Cart (Parvo Medics, Sandy, UT). The protocol started at 2 mph and 0% elevation and increases by ~1 MET per minute either by speed or elevation. In each subject, the same protocol and same metabolic cart was used before randomization and at 12 weeks. The last 30 s were averaged to determine peak \mathsf { V O } _ { 2 } .

## Collection of physical activity and exercise prescription

Following study consent, patients were provided a Fitbit, assisted in creating an account, downloading the application and shown how to use the device. Patients were asked to synchronize their device daily

to their smartphone. During the last 2 weeks of CR, all patients wore an activity tracker for the purpose of recording physical activity of daily living during a normal week. Patients wore the activity tracker continuously during waking hours (also encouraged to wear it overnight while sleeping). Following this 2-week data capture, patients randomized into the usual care group gave back the activity tracker and were asked to follow their physician's instructions on physical activity. Duke currently does not offer a phase III CR program, but patients randomized into usual care were not prohibited from seeking other programs while in our study. Usual care patients received no step prescription or feedback on physical activity during the study. The activity trackers were given back to them to wear during weeks 11–12. Each patient randomized to the mHealth intervention was given an initial personalized exercise prescription based on the average number of steps per day they performed during the last two weeks of CR. This prescription was shared with the Vida health coaches so that the coaches could better support the exercise portion of the intervention during weekly coaching sessions and text messaging. The following was used to determine step prescription:

Weeks 1–4: ↑ Physical Activity by 2500 steps over baseline activity level.

Weeks 5–8: ↑ Physical Activity by 3750 steps over baseline activity level.

Weeks 9–12: ↑ Physical Activity by 5000 steps over baseline activity level.

For example, if a patient averaged 5200 steps per day during the last two weeks of CR, then their exercise prescription for month one would have been 5200+ 2500 = 7700 steps/day. If achieved, month two would be 5200+ 3750 = 8950 steps/day. If the patient's prescribed step count was not attained at the end of the month period, the subject was instructed not to increase their steps until the step goal was attained. If a patient reached 10,000 steps per day during the study, they were instructed to maintain this amount without further increase.

Physical activity data were downloaded weekly by investigators and summarized into reports. If patients failed to synchronize device to their smartphone, or data looked suspicious (eg, unusual long gaps of no data or questionable values), a call was initiated with the patient for resolution. Data was captured on the following in both groups: steps/day, minutes/ day of exercise, distance/day in miles and floors/day. Data were also captured on minutes performed across different intensities. Fitbit reports minutes of activity in three categories: light, fairly active and very active. In an effort to describe these activities relative to government guidelines, we have reported them as low, moderate-low and moderate-high. The rationale for this was that none of our patients jogged or ran, therefore by government guidelines no patient will have exercised at a “vigorous” intensity as described by government guidelines. Fitbit “light” activity levels equate to activities of daily living, and therefore should not be included relative to government moderate exercise prescription. Based on heart rate data and observation during CR, the Fitbit fairly active and very active represent walking briskly and equates to moderate-low and moderate-high activity. Therefore, for the purpose of discussion, we have equated Fitbit's light to low, fairly active to moderate-low and very active to moderate-high for a comparison to government guidelines. Figure 5 combines both moderate intensities together.

## Health coaching

This study partnered with Vida Health (Vida Health, Inc.; San Francisco, CA) for health coaching services. Patients randomized into the mHealth group were assisted in downloading the Vida health app to their smartphone and creating a Vida account (provided by the study). Patients chose their own health coaches based on personality matching and expertise from health coach biographies provided within the application and their preference of coaching style (eg, analytical, cheerleader, drill sergeant). Vida's professional health coaches are selected by the patient from a pool of well-trained and experienced health professionals including, but not limited to, nutritionists, exercise

physiologists, nurses and health educators. Before initial contact, health coaches received a patient profile outlining medical history, test results, risk factors/problems, and patient goals along with any other pertinent information. Coaches also received each patient's exercise goal, as prescribed by steps/day. Health coaches contacted patients within three days of randomization. Over the phone, health coaches instructed patients how to use a Vida mobile phone application and outline personalized patient care plans. Based on patient preference, coaches called patients 1–2 times per week for 30–60 min. Tailored to patient preference and needs, coaching sessions were used for answering health related questions (eg,; AHA recommended blood pressure, Ha1c, lipid

values, etc.), planning (meal choices), and motivation (eg,; prioritizing time to exercise and taking medicines). In addition, coaches sent educational material via email and sent text messages to remind them to practice healthy lifestyle habits. Frequency and content of text messages were not automated, but was individual to each patient's need and request. A majority of text messaging was used to remind patient to practice healthy habits, provide motivation to exercise, set-up coaching session calls and identify topics to be discussed during coaching sessions. The study team at Duke had weekly conference calls with the Vida health coaches to discuss each patient currently in the mHealth program. These conference calls were used to change exercise prescriptions, trouble-

shoot problems or questions and discuss any potential medical issues. Patients in the usual care group received no health coaching.

## Statistical analyses

Tabular data represents mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables and percentages for categorical variables. Chi square analysis was used to compare categorical variables among patient groups at baseline. Our pre-specified primary outcomes were change in peak \mathsf { V O } _ { 2 }and physical activity patterns (change in steps/day or week and change in minutes of activity/day or week) among groups. Secondary, exploratory, outcomes included medical adherence and angina frequency. A paired t-test was used to determine if the 12 week pre-topost change differences were significant within groups. An independent-sample Student t test was performed to determine if change scores were different between groups. Pearson correlation coefficients were used to describe relationships among continuous variables. P \le . 0 5was considered statistically significant. Data analysis was performed using

the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 24 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, Ill.).

# Results relative to government guidelines
The current US Physical Activity Guidelines suggest individuals should perform 150 min of moderate-intensity aerobic activity or 75 min of vigorous-intensity aerobic activity weekly.48 All of our patients chose brisk walking as their mode of exercise. One of the most clinically relevant new findings from this study involved the amount of physical activity in which subjects participated. During CR, both groups performed well above the recommended amount of 150 min of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise per week ( 3 1 0 \pm 1 5 4min for mHealth vs 2 2 8 \pm 1 4 7for usual care; NS between groups) (Figure 5). However, 12 weeks following CR, only the mHealth group was exercising enough to meet these guidelines (294 ± 199 min/week vs 1 1 5 \pm99.9 min/week; P < . 0 5between groups). The mHealth group only decreased their exercise by 16 min/week, while the usual care group had decreased physical activity to 113 min per week ( P = . 1 2 ). If confirmed in a larger study, these findings have very significant public health implications.

These findings have implications for non-cardiac populations, as well. A very recent publication from the Women's Health Initiative-Observational Study showed post-menopausal women meeting the US Physical Activity Guidelines before and after a myocardial infarction were protected against mortality.49 Our results suggest high adherence while enrolled in CR meets the recommended guidelines; using mHealth with an activity tracker and health coaching following CR allows patients to sustain activity levels meeting the physical activity guidelines for up to 12 weeks after CR completion. Whether this will actually result in gains in health outcomes and prevention of further cardiac events in the long term will require further and larger studies. However, without a comparable mHealth program, our findings suggest highly adherent CR patients will become non-adherent and drop below government exercise recommendations shortly after CR completion.

## Health coaching

A major component of our design was the use of health coaching to improve patient engagement and self-accountability. The use of health coaches is a direct response to the AHA's Presidential Advisory Panel to address gaps in access and develop novel treatment models for the delivery of CR services.50 There was a specific call for “disease management and lifestyle health coaching interventions and case management delivered by nurses and other non-physician health professionals via the telephone, internet, and other means of communication.” The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force has recently provided a systematic evidence review supporting health coaching as an intervention to reduce CVD risk factors of physical activity and diet.51 Health coaches provide information during the “readiness to change” time period and motivation support.52,53 Based on patient feedback, we believe the use of health coaching in this study provided structure to exercise regimens, and accountability to step goals, without over burdening the patients. Several patients had several weeks of low step counts and accredited the health coaches with helping them get back on track. The weekly contact between the coaches and patients helped reinforce the components of a healthy lifestyle learned in CR and helped to bridge patients into independence following CR.

## Use of a physical activity tracker

The exercise prescription used in this study was steps/day and not target heart rate or rate of perceived exertion, as used in classical CR. Nor were the patients instructed to walk a set number of minutes per day or days per week. Our data suggest the use of steps counts per day can sustain or slightly improve functional capacity in this population. This study also demonstrated a significant relationship between steps per day and peak \mathsf { V O } _ { 2 } \left( \mathsf { F i g u r e } 2 \right). As stated earlier, it was surprising that the mHealth group's peak \mathsf { V O } _ { 2 }increased. This was especially surprising in the setting where total minutes of activity per week decreased to 148 min. We speculate that patients may have met their daily step count by walking faster and at a greater intensity, in order to spend less time during their exercise bouts. Alternatively, they may have walked faster because they felt better. During exit interviews at the end of the study, patients routinely stated that they looked at the activity tracker several times during the day and were very aware of the number of steps still needed each day to meet their goals. Although further investigation is required, using an exercise prescription based on steps per day may offer an alternative recommendation to target heart rate zones and continuous aerobic exercise for some patients even during the CR period.

## Strengths and limitations

A strength of our investigation is that we objectively measured functional capacity (peak VO2); further we measured responses over the 12 weeks following completion of CR; to the best of our knowledge, this has never before been attempted. In addition, this is the first study to use an objective measurement of physical activity (steps and different intensities of activity minutes) acutely after CR as opposed to selfreports, which are known to be inaccurate. As mentioned in the Methods, Fitbit reports time spent in three different activity intensities; unfortunately, despite attempts, we could not obtain more information from the manufacturer on how or at what MET level these intensities are determined. Not knowing the exact criteria used for determining activity levels is a limitation and precludes an estimate of total energy expenditure. These limitations could be overcome if all device companies would fully disclose the details underlying their definition of physical activity amounts, intensities, and energy expenditures.

In addition, it is unknown if the findings in this small study are generalizable to those patients who did not qualify because they did not have a smartphone, they declined, or they were already using an activity

tracker. Unfortunately, we do not know the fitness levels, activity levels, socioeconomic status or comorbidities of this group. The population studied in this investigation — patients with medical insurance who completed 36 CR sessions — may have been at a lower risk than those who do not participate at all in CR. Therefore, future mHealth studies must establish if mHealth can benefit high risk patients and those who do not participate in CR. Also, we do not know exactly what the usual care group did during the first ten weeks after CR, because they were not wearing a physical activity tracker. Their physical activity behaviors were assessed at an average of weeks 11 and 12. The age of the mHealth group was seven years younger than the usual care group. Although this was not statistically different, it may have affected results. Of the five drop-outs, four were in the mHealth group and only one in usual care. If the randomization had been 1:1 versus 3:1, more drop-outs would have occurred in the usual care group and may have potentially affected results. Finally, to the best of our knowledge, no usual care patient joined a Phase 3 CR program or other type of supervised program. Based on the usual care's decrease in peak { \mathsf { V O } } _ { 2 } ,it is highly likely their lower activity levels reported during weeks 11–12 mirror that of weeks 1–10.

The encouraging results of this study demonstrate the feasibility and efficacy in using mHealth as a delivering tool for long-term exercise adherence in a CR eligible population. Due to rapid advances in the mHealth industry, commercially available devices now exist with the ability to remotely monitor several risk factors (eg, blood pressure). Future studies should be inclusive for multiple devices and risk factors, without over-burdening the patient. A larger trial, over a longer time period, powered to evaluate clinical events, mortality and cost effectiveness is necessary to fully evaluate the efficacy of mHealth.

## Current and future state of mHealth in CR

Currently, there is a slow adoption rate for mHealth use in clinical CR programs. In the current study, it is unknown if any part of the mHealth intervention (Fitbit or health coaching) had a greater impact on the findings. In the current literature, most mHealth studies used simple interventions — such as text messaging and use of a smartphone — to collect activity levels (not a wearable device).54 Many have only studied patient perception or physical activity by self-reported questionnaires.55,56 Most lack direct measurement of fitness by peak \mathsf { V O } _ { 2 }and physical activity by a wearable device. These studies, along with the current study, suggest that improving clinically relevant outcomes (eg, functional capacity and activity level) is likely linked to some form of patient-provider data collection and feedback via text messaging/coaching or use of a wearable device. A larger trial with additional arms will be necessary to address which components are necessary and sufficient to achieve similar success.

Due to limited resources, most CR programs will not be able to implement a robust mHealth CR program until insurance recognizes its value and provides reimbursement. With proper resources, the extent to which mHealth can develop is attractive, potentially improving traditional CR enrollment, retention, compliance and providing a remote program for those CR eligible patients who cannot attend on-site CR. Furthermore, a large multi-center randomize clinical trial would also demonstrate the feasibility of engaging challenging populations not included in this pilot, such as such as minorities and those with low socioeconomic status, or those living in a rural environments.

## Summary and conclusions

Results from this pilot study suggest mHealth after completion of site-based CR can help sustain physical activity and peak \mathsf { V O } _ { 2 }gains made in CR better than usual care. The combination of wearing a physical activity tracker to monitor and achieve a step goal combined with a health coach helped recent graduates of CR maintain their cardiorespiratory fitness benefits and physical activity levels 12 weeks after

completing a site-based CR program. To explore the effects on other important cardiometabolic risk factors, larger trials should also study the efficacy of other wearable devices to monitor additional risk factors such as blood pressure, weight and blood glucose.
