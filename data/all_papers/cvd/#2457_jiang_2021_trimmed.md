## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# 2. Method
## 2.1. Study design and setting

A three-arm stratified randomized controlled trial (RCT) with repeated assessments was adopted (clinical trial registration number: NCT03108235). The development of study intervention and pilot testing was published previously (Jiang et al., 2019). In addition, a qualitative process evaluation of the study intervention was conducted following the RCT, the findings of which are published elsewhere (Jiang et al., 2021).

The study was conducted at one of the largest tertiary public hospitals in Singapore, which has a bed capacity of 1200. The department of cardiology provides diagnostic and therapeutic management of complex cardiovascular conditions, including HF, acute coronary syndrome, vascular disease and congenital heart disease. Patients admitted for HF between June 2018 to Au-

gust 2019 were approached and screened for study participation eligibility.

## 2.2. Participants

A consecutive sampling method was adopted. The inclusion criteria were patients who: (1) were aged 21 years or older; (2) had been clinically diagnosed with HF; (3) were able to read and understand English or Chinese; (4) owned and used a smartphone in their everyday lives; and (5) were able to be followed up at home after discharged from hospital. The exclusion criteria were patients who had: (1) unstable angina, resting tachycardia, or severe arterial hypertension; (2) a terminal illness other than HF that affects their self-care ability and/or completion of the study, e.g. end-stage cancer; (3) a psychiatric condition or impaired cognitive functioning that affects their understanding of the study intervention; or (4) were bed- or wheelchair-bound affecting their mobility and selfcare ability and (5) no internet access at home.

The sample size calculation was based on Cohen’s (1988) statistical power analysis. At least 53 participants per group were required to detect a between-group differences at significance level α of 0.05 and 80% power to achieve a medium effect size on our primary outcome of HF self-care for an ANOVA analysis. A total of 159 participants with 53 in each group were needed. Previous studies on the effects of psychosocial educational intervention for patients with heart disease have typically reported dropout rates between 20 and 30% (Athilingam et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2012). Taking into account an attrition rate of approximately 25%, the final sample size was 213 (71 per group).

## 2.3. Randomization procedure

To control for confounding effects on the study outcomes (Chung et al., 2006; Friedman, 2003), participants were stratified by gender and HF severity based on the New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class. Participants with NYHA class I or class II were categorized as “mild-moderate level”, while those with NYHA class III or class IV were categorized as “severe level”. There were four strata in total: (1) “male, mild-moderate level”; (2) “male, severe level”; (3) “female, mild-moderate level”; and (4) “female, severe level”.

A randomization list with the block size of 12 was generated through a computer program for each stratum. The group assignments for the first 12 participants for each stratum were prepared based on the randomization list and stored in opaque sealed envelopes beforehand. Participants were assigned a unique participant ID based on his/her stratum and sequence of enrolment. The envelope according to the participant’s ID was opened on site and the participant would be randomly assigned to one of the study groups based on the group assignment indicated. Once the envelop was used for each successfully enrolled participant, it would be discarded. In each stratum, after the initial 12 envelopes were used, the second batch of 12 envelopes would be created. This process was repeated until the sample size was achieved.

## 2.4. Study intervention

The detailed description of the HOM-HEMP intervention has been published previously (Jiang et al., 2019). Briefly, all participants in the study received usual care, comprising all medical, nursing, allied health and follow-up services provided by the study hospital. For example, an inpatient nurse may provide simple, unstructured education about HF and medication use during a patient’s stay in the ward. The patients would also have regular outpatient follow-up appointments with their cardiologist to track

their health and optimize their medications and treatment. Participants may also be referred to other allied health professionals, such as physiotherapists and dietitians, as they may have specific clinical needs for cardiac rehabilitation or because of other comorbidities, such as diabetes. In addition to usual care, the experimental group A and experimental group B also received the HOM-HEMP intervention.

The HOM-HEMP is a nurse-led home-based multi-component HF self-management intervention that adopts a psychosocial education approach. The intervention included a HF patient education and self-management toolkit and three home visits by the research every 2 weeks.

The description of the study intervention based on the taxonomy for disease management (Krumholz et al., 2006) is shown in Table 1. The toolkit provided cues (e.g., a visual reminder of fluid intake, alarm reminder for medication adherence) of self-care activities in the context of daily life. The education manual was designed to help patients and caregivers review and consolidate their knowledge of HF self-management. The content of the HF education manual was developed based on the life experiences of patients with HF in Singapore (Loo et al., 2016; Seah et al., 2015) as well as the existing HF educational materials developed by the Singapore Health Promotion Board and National Healthcare Group Heart Failure Workgroup. The education manual was available in English and Chinese, both versions were written in simple sentences with illustrative pictures to aid patient understanding.

The research nurse, trained in basic and intermediate motivational interviewing, who was a graduate student and an experienced registered nurse and mental health nurse, used a motivational interviewing-based approach to promote participants’ engagement in self-care during the home visitations. The duration of the HOM-HEMP intervention was 6 weeks.

Additionally, those in the experimental group B received an additional smartphone app developed based on the study intervention (Fig. 1). The mHealth component had various features, including (1) educational information; (2) individualized scheduled reminders for medication and medical appointments; (3) weight, blood pressure and symptom logs and (4) a chat room to talk to the research nurse. The research nurse can review patient’s symptoms through a secure web portal. Two intervention groups in the study served to explore the usability and effects of the mHealth component on the study outcomes.

# 2.5. Outcome measures
The primary outcome, HF self-care, was assessed by Self-Care of Heart Failure Index SCHFI (V6.2) (Riegel et al., 2009). The index has 22 self-reported items measuring three domains of self-care: management (6 items; scored only if patient reports breathing difficulty or ankle swelling), confidence (6 items), and self-care maintenance (10 items). Each item was rated on a 4-point Likert scale, and scores of respective domains were standardized into a range of 0-100. Better HF self-care is reflected by higher scores. A score of 70 or higher defined the cut-point for having adequate self-care (Riegel et al., 2009). The SCHFI has been shown to be valid and sensitive in identifying health-behaviors across samples of HF patients (Riegel et al., 2006; Riegel & Carlson, 2004). The Chinese translated version of this scale has also been validated among the Chinese-speaking population (Kang et al., 2015).

Secondary outcomes included cardiac self-efficacy as measured by Cardiac Self-Efficacy Scale (CSES) (Sullivan et al., 1998), anxiety and depression as measured by the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) (Zigmond and Snaith, 1983), health-related quality of life (HRQoL) as measured by Minnesota Living with Heart Failure Questionnaire (MLHFQ) (Rector and Cohn, 1992), and perceived social support as assessed by Short Form of the Social Sup-

port Questionnaire (SSQ6) (Sarason et al., 1987). All these instruments have good reliability and validity. Depending on the language preference of the participants, either an English or Chinese version of the questionnaire was used.

The CSES was used to measure patient’s cardiac self-efficacy, particularly in the following domains: “control symptoms” and “maintain functioning” (Sullivan et al., 1998). The scale has shown high internal consistency, good convergent and discriminant validities. The Cronbach’s α of control symptoms and maintain functioning were 0.90 and 0.87, respectively (Sullivan et al., 1998). The Chinese CSES has been validated by Zhang et al (2018). The Chinese CSES has good internal consistency with a Cronbach’s α of 0.926 (Zhang et al., 2018).

HADS has been widely used by researchers and clinicians in the general hospital settings (Liao et al., 2018; Loeffler et al., 2018; Muñoz et al., 2018). HADS consists of two subscales: the anxiety subscale and the depression subscale (Zigmond and Snaith, 1983). The Cronbach’s α for the HADS total score were between 0.86 to 0.929, and the Cronbach’s α for the anxiety and depression subscales were 0.70 and higher, indicating good to excellent internal consistency of the scale (Li et al., 2016; Turk et al., 2015). The scale has been translated into several different languages and validated cross-culturally, including Singapore (Beck et al., 2016).

The MLHFQ is the most commonly used disease-specific instrument that measured the effects of HF and treatments for HF on an

individual’s overall QoL, as well as the “emotional dimension” (5 items) and the “physical dimension” (8 items) of QoL (Heo et al., 2005; Rector and Cohn, 1992). The scale had test-retest reliability of 0.87 and a Cronbach’s α of 0.92. Its two subscales had adequate internal consistency with the Cronbach’s α ranging from 0.71 to 0.93 (Heo et al., 2005). The MLHFQ has also been translated into many different languages, including Chinese (Ho et al., 2007). The Chinese version of MLHFQ has been validated and showed good reliability of the overall scale and subscales with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.95 and 0.93–0.95, respectively (Ho et al., 2007).

SSQ6 measured participants’ perceived social support. The “number score” measured the number of people an individual feels they can turn to in a variety of situations when they need it, while the “satisfaction score” measured the individual’s satisfaction with perceived support in a given situation (Sarason et al., 1987). The psychometric properties of SSQ6 have also been validated in studies conducted in different countries (Furukawa et al., 1999; Teixeira and Monterio, 2011). The Chinese version SSQ6 has shown satisfactory content validity and good internal consistency of 0.90 (Chien et al., 2006).

Clinical outcomes such as the New York Heart Association (NYHA) Functional Classification (Dolgin and New York Heart Association, 1994), and numbers of unplanned health service use because of cardiac-related reasons, including unplanned hospital admissions, visits to the accident and emergency depart-

ment (A&E), and unplanned outpatient medical consultations were obtained.

## 2.6. Data collection procedure

After obtaining the participants’ informed consent, the researcher collected the baseline data. Instructions were given to participants to attempt the self-rating questionnaires (SCHFI, CSES, HADS, MLHFQ and SSQ6), as well as the sociodemographic data sheet by themselves. The researcher would go through the instruments verbally with participants if they required assistance. The clinical data, such as the primary diagnosis, length of heart failure diagnosis, and comorbidities were obtained by both interviewing of the participants and reviewing their medical records.

The research assistant who was blinded to group allocation of the participants contacted the participants and arranged appointments to collect post intervention data at 6 weeks (i.e. immediately after the study intervention) (T1), 3 months from baseline (T2), and 6 months from baseline (T3). The NYHA functional classification was rated by the research assistant trained in data collection. The numbers of unplanned health services were obtained from patients and medical records. Each data collection took approximately 40–50 min. Participants were allowed to take breaks if they felt tired during the data collection.

## 2.7. Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) for Windows, version 24.0 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp), and included descriptive and inferential statistical analyses. Descriptive statistics, such as mean, standard deviation (SD), frequency, range and percentage were used for data checking, data cleaning, sample description and summarization of outcome variables. The statistical assumption of normality of the data was examined by Q-Q plots, values of skewness and kurtosis, as well as the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Inferential statistics, such as oneway and repeated measures ANOVA, Chi-square test, Kruskal-Wallis test were used to test the study hypotheses. The significance levels for all the tests were set at p < 0.05, two-tailed.

## 2.8. Ethical approval

Before the commencement of the study, ethical approval was obtained from National Healthcare Group-Domain Specific Review Board (NHG-DSRB) (NHG DSRB Ref: 2017/00249).

# 3. Results
We recruited participants from June 2018 to end August 2019. A total of 693 patients from the study hospital were approached and screened, among which 213 (30.7%) patients were recruited,

and 480 (69.3%) patients were excluded as they did not meet the eligibility criteria. The most common reasons for not meeting the eligibility criteria included: patient or family refusal of home visits after discharge (n 278, 40.1%), language barriers (n 88, 12.7%), and patients not having a smartphone and/or home internet access (n = 52, 7.5%).

During the study, 16 (22.2%) participants from the control group, 22 (31%) participants from experimental group A, and 13 (18.6%) participants from experimental group B dropped out for reasons listed in Fig. 2. The overall attrition rate was 23.9%. 162 participants completed all the data collections. The sociodemographic and clinical characteristics were compared between those

who had completed and those who had not completed the study. Differences between the two groups were not statistically significant. The final data analysis was based on the 162 participants who had completed all the data collections.

## 3.1. Baseline data

The sociodemographic, clinical characteristics and the baseline outcome variables across the study groups were presented in Table 2. Most participants were Chinese (54.4–69.4%), married (66.1–77.6%), stayed with family (75–87.8%) in public housing estates. The majority of patients were not working (61.4–75%) and

had a monthly household income of less than S$3,000 (about USD 2236, 66.7–76.8%). At baseline, the average length of HF diagnosis of the control group was 69.54 months, compared to 50.73 months in the experimental group A, and 38.07 months in the experimental group B.

Statistically significant differences were present in the percentage of participants who were on diuretic (p=0.032) and the percentage of participants who were on digoxin (p<0.001) among the three study groups. With the exception of the perceived number of social support persons (p 0.03) that had a significant difference, no other outcomes variables at baseline had significant differences among the groups.

# 3.3. Intervention effects on secondary outcomes
Table 3 presents the results of the intervention effects on CSES, HADS, MLHFQ and SSQ6 scores. There was a significant interaction effect between group and the level of time on CSES_Control Symptoms (F 4.448, p 0.001), CSES_Maintain Functioning (F=3.974, p=0.001), HADS_Depression (F=2.522, p=0.025), MLHFQ_Physical Dimension (F 4.575, p<0.001) and MLHFQ_Total (F 4.711, p<0.001) scores. After adjusting for covariates, the interaction effect was not statistically significant for CSES_Control

Symptoms (F=1.646, p=0.159) and CSES_Maintain Functioning (F 1.776, p 0.113). Additionally, no significant interaction effect between group and the level of time on HADS_Anxiety, SSQ6_Number Score and SSQ6_Satisfaction Score before or after adjusting for covariates were observed.

Findings from the parameter estimates showed that either experimental group A or group B scored significantly higher than participants in the control group on both Control Symptoms and Maintain Functioning subscales of cardiac self-efficacy at all postintervention follow-ups after adjusting for covariates, even though cardiac self-efficacy at baseline was comparable to that of the control group. In comparison to the control group, experimental group A had significantly lower HADS_Depression scores at all the post-intervention follow-ups (p<0.05), while experimental group B had significant lower HADS_Depression score at 6-month follow-up (p=0.039). Lastly, experimental group A and group B had significantly better physical dimension of HRQoL compared to the control group at all post-intervention follow-ups. Experimental group B also had significantly better overall HRQoL than the control group at all the post-intervention follow-ups, while experimental group A had significantly better overall HRQoL than the control group at 6-week and 3-month follow-ups.

# 3.4. Intervention effects on clinical outcomes
Data on NYHA functional classification at all data collection timepoints were analysed using chi-square test and are presented in Table 4. At 6-week and 3-month follow-ups, a higher proportion of participants were classified as "severe" in the control group compared to either of the experimental groups (p 0.001).

In addition, the results of unplanned hospital admissions, A&E visits, and medical consultations related to cardiac reasons based on Kruskal-Wallis test are also presented in Table 4. When there was a significant finding from the Kruskal-Wallis test, the posthoc test for pairwise comparisons was performed. The results showed a significant difference in the number of unplanned hospital admissions (p 0.016) and unplanned A&E visits (p 0.008) at 6-month follow-up. Pairwise comparisons showed a statistically significant difference in the number of unplanned hospital admissions between experimental group B and control group (p 0.012), but not between experimental group A and control group (p=0.542) or between experimental group A and group B (p 0.447). Likewise, significant difference was found in the number of unplanned A&E visits between experimental group B and control group (p=0.006), but not between experimental group A and control group (p 0.861) or between experimental group A and group B (p 0.169).
