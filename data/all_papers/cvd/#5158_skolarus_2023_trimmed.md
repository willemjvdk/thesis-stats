## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# METHODS
Institutional review board approval was obtained from the University of Michigan and the ED site. All participants provided written informed consent. The data supporting this study’s findings are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. This study is reported following the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) reporting guideline. The Trial Protocol is presented in Supplemental Material.

## Study Design

The Reach Out trial is based on the multiphase optimization strategy framework, a framework grounded in engineering for developing, optimizing, and evaluating multicomponent behavioral interventions.18 Specifically, Reach Out was a randomized, controlled, 2×2×2 factorial design clinical trial that allocated participants to 8 combinations of mHealth components, representing the optimization stage of the multiphase optimization strategy framework. Outcome assessments occurred at 6 months and 12 months. Reach Out was designed and developed through a community-based participatory approach, and study details have been published.19–21

## Study Population

This trial was conducted in Flint, Michigan, an urban, lowincome, predominately Black community. Participants were recruited from the city’s only safety-net ED (ie, hospitals where over 25% of patients are Medicaid recipients),22 and referrals to primary care providers were made to the city’s only Federally Qualified Health Center (FQHC) network. Automated electronic health record alerts notified the research team of ED patients with elevated BP who were likely to be discharged home from the ED. Specifically, patients were eligible if (1) they had at least 1 documented systolic BP≥160 or a diastolic BP≥100 (criterion 1), (2) after achieving criterion 1, at least 1 repeat BP remained systolic BP≥140 or a diastolic BP≥90, (3) they were likely to be discharged from the ED, and (4) they possessed a cell phone with text messaging capability. We excluded patients who were critically ill, otherwise unable

to give informed consent, non-English speaking, incarcerated, pregnant, had a preexisting condition that made 1-year followup unlikely, currently used ≥3 antihypertensive agents (suggesting resistant hypertension), or had dementia/ cognitive impairment. Enrollment ended early, in March 2020, due to the research restrictions due to the COVID-19 pandemic.

## Study Procedures

Participants consented, and baseline data collection was completed during their ED visit. They were given an automated SureLife Classic Wrist BP Monitor and culturally tailored hypertension materials, including a comic book designed via a community-based participatory approach describing the Reach Out intervention, risks of high BP, the importance of SMBP, and lifestyle changes to lower their BP (Supplemental Material). Participants then entered a run-in phase of 3 weeks to determine whether a participant had persistently elevated BP. During this time, participants were prompted to text their BP once per week to the study team. Participants responding at least once and with at least 1 systolic BP≥140 or a diastolic BP≥90 were randomized. The research team contacted the participant at week 2 to assess for technical barriers if the participant had not texted a BP.

Participants were randomized via block randomization stratified by age (<64 years, ≥65 years), self-reported sex, and whether they had taken an antihypertensive within the prior 6 months (yes versus no). Randomly permuted blocks of size 8 were generated using the blockrand package in R by the study statistician and were only accessible to the project manager at the time of randomization. The sequences were uploaded into a system called TATUM by the unblinded statistician. Research assistants could not be blinded to participant randomization assignments because they facilitated some intervention components, such as provider appointment scheduling and transportation.

## Study Interventions

Reach Out tested 3 mHealth strategies: (1) tailored healthy behavior text messages (daily versus none); (2) prompted SMBP monitoring and feedback (daily versus weekly); (3)

facilitated primary care provider scheduling and transportation (yes versus no). Participants were randomized into 1 of 8 arms for the 12-month intervention (Figure 1). If a participant had been in the intervention for at least 1 month and had not texted a BP within the previous month, the research team contacted the participant for a technology check. Otherwise, the research team’s contact with the participants was only during outcomes assessments. Sample text messages are included in Supplemental Material.

## Tailored Healthy Behavior Text Messages (Daily Text Versus No Texts)

Healthy behavior text messages addressed the most important lifestyle interventions to reduce BP: salt reduction, increased fruit and vegetable intake, and increased physical activity. They were based on social cognitive theory and designed in partnership with the Flint community.20 Additionally, personalized BP feedback messages were sent comparing the participant’s most recent BP to their previously reported BP at least a week prior and whether they were at goal BP (defined as <130/80 mmHg). These feedback messages were tailored to whether the participant took an antihypertensive medication, the medication adherence level, and whether the participant had a primary care physician. Messages also included recommendations on where to find discounted medications.

## Prompted SMBP Monitoring (Weekly Prompts Versus Daily Prompts) and Feedback

Participants were sent text reminders to check their home BP and text back the result. Participants randomized to weekly prompted SMBP monitoring were sent up to 2 reminders if they did not respond with a BP. Participants randomized to daily prompted SMBP monitoring were not sent reminders. A personalized feedback message comparing the participant’s most recent BP to goal BP and providing general encouragement was texted weekly. Participants also received a monthly graph of their self-reported BPs, with tailored interpretation.

## Facilitated Primary Care Provider Scheduling and Transportation (Yes Versus No)

For participants randomized to facilitated primary care provider appointment scheduling, the research team texted 3 date/time

options to the participant for appointments with their primary care provider. Participants without a primary care provider or who were uninsured were provided follow-up appointments with the FQHC within 6 weeks of discharge from the ED. For the uninsured, the FQHC staff would then work with the participant to enroll them in Medicaid or the local county health plan for those who were Medicaid ineligible. Transportation to the primary care provider (PCP) appointment via a ride-share service or taxi was also offered. These appointment and transportation procedures were offered monthly until BP control was achieved (systolic BP <130 mm Hg). Following BP control, appointments were provided about every 3 months. Due to COVID-19, the facilitation of primary care provider appointments and transportation ended on March 16, 2020. On July 22, 2020, primary care provider appointment scheduling resumed, but transportation was not reinstated, given contact protections of ride-share services. Participants randomized to passive reminders were sent monthly text messages to connect with their doctor about their BP and contact information to find a primary care doctor if needed. The research team did not provide prescribing recommendations to the providers.

# Primary and Secondary Outcomes
The primary end point was systolic BP (SBP) change from baseline to 12 months. The primary objective was to estimate the expected change in 12-month systolic BP reduction associated with each level of the 3 mHealth strategies and determine the combination of mHealth strategies most promising for study in a future larger-scale trial. All outcomes were intended to be in-person. However, outcomes were assessed remotely for some participants at 6 months and all participants at 12 months due to COVID-19 research restrictions. In-person BP assessments were conducted by trained research team members using standard procedures using an OMRON 7 Series Upper Arm BP Monitor.23 Participants were seated quietly for a minimum of 5 minutes before their BP measurement, with legs uncrossed and feet on the ground. BP was measured on the right arm, unless medically contraindicated, with tight clothing removed. Participants were seated with their backs supported by the chair and the arm supported at the mid-sternal level. During BP measurements, participants were asked not to talk. Three consecutive BPs were taken in person. Remotely, participants were asked to provide 3 BP measurements, each 1 minute apart. If performed remotely, participants communicated these to the study team through phone calls or text messages. Participants were also asked to send a picture of their BP cuff on their wrists to confirm the correct orientation. The secondary end points were self-reported time from ED visit to arrival at the first primary care visit (in days) and attendance at ≥2 primary care visits within 12 months of randomization.

# Exploratory and Process Outcomes
Diastolic BP declined after 12 months (−3.7 mmHg, −5.7 to −1.7) among Reach Out participants (Table S6). Among participants who completed the 12-month followup, 21 (10%) did not have a PCP at baseline. Of these, 11

(52.4%) had a PCP visit (7 [70% out of 10 participants] in the facilitated PCP scheduling arm and 4 [36.4% out of 11 participants] in the passive PCP arm). Of the 83 (39.3%) participants who completed the 12-month followup and were not taking antihypertensive medications at enrollment, 24 (28.9%) started antihypertensive medication during the trial. There was no change in medication adherence among participants taking antihypertensive medications at enrollment (Table S7). A total of 73 participants (15%) did not respond to any SMBP monitoring prompts. Most participants reported that the information

in the Reach Out text messages was easy to understand (78% somewhat easy/very easy, 21% missing, and 1% somewhat difficult/very difficult). Participants were satisfied with Reach Out (78% satisfied/very satisfied, 21% missing, and 1% neither satisfied nor dissatisfied).

# RESULTS
During the 12-month enrollment period between March 2019 and March 2020, over 20819 ED patients with elevated BP were identified through electronic medical record-based automatic alerts. A total of 1373 of these ED patients were approached, 833 patients were enrolled, 488 were randomized, and 211 participants (43%) completed the 12-month follow-up (Figure 2; Table S1). The follow-up of the last participant occurred in April 2021. The baseline characteristics of each study arm are shown in Table 1. The mean (SD) age of participants was 45.5 (12.4) years; 61% were women; 54% were Black people; 49% did not have any college education. Regarding medical care and hypertension factors, 22% did not have a primary care doctor; 10% were uninsured; 21% lacked transportation to a PCP appointment in the previous year; 21% did not have a prior diagnosis of hypertension, and 44% were not taking antihypertensive medications in the 6 months preceding enrollment. Overall, there were few differences across the 8 intervention arms.

Baseline demographics and medical utilization factors varied between participants with complete and incomplete follow-ups. Older participants, women, Black participants, participants with a PCP, known diagnosis of hypertension, and those taking antihypertensives in the 6 months preceding enrollment were more likely to have complete follow-up (Table S2). Participants with

complete follow-up had lower baseline BP (143.9 versus 147.6; P=0.03) than those with incomplete followup. Incomplete follow-up was higher among participants who received daily SMBP monitoring (55% incomplete follow-up versus 45% complete follow-up, P=0.02). There was no difference in follow-up among other intervention components.

# Primary Outcome: BP
SBP declined after 6 months (−9.2 mmHg [95% CI, −12.2 to −6.3]) and after 12 months (−6.6 mm Hg, −9.3 to −3.8) among participants. BP declined in all 8 intervention arms (Figure 3 and Table S3). The 3 mHealth components, healthy behavior text messages, prompted daily SMBP monitoring, and facilitated PCP scheduling were not associated with a greater change in systolic BP at 12 months (Table 2). Participants taking antihypertensive medications in the 6 months preceding enrollment had a 6 mmHg (95% CI, 12.4 to 0.3; P=0.06) greater BP reduction than participants not taking antihypertensive medications before enrollment. Black participants had less of a reduction in BP than their counterparts. Systolic BP declined less among Black participants (2.5 mm Hg [95% CI, −8.3 to 3.2; P=0.4]) compared with non-Black participants (9.8 mm Hg (95% CI, −15.4 to −4.2, P<0.01)). In the longitudinal analysis and multiple imputation model, there was no association between the mHealth components and systolic BP (Table S4).

# Secondary Outcome: Primary Care Follow-Up
The majority (84%) of participants who completed the 12-month follow-up had a PCP visit during the trial (Table S5). PCP visits were often long after the ED visit, mean 80.2 days (95% CI, 68.7–91.6) days, and 74% of participants attended >2 visits. In the fully adjusted model (Table 3), facilitated PCP scheduling (OR, 1.7 [0.9–3.5]; P=0.11) and daily SMBP (OR, 1.8 [0.9– 3.6], P=0.11) were associated with attendance at >2 PCP visits, although with borderline significance. Participants who were taking antihypertensive therapy in the 6 months preceding enrollment (OR, 2.7 [1.3–5.6], P<0.01) or had a PCP at baseline (OR, 3.9 [1.4–10.6]; P<0.01) were more likely to have ≥2 PCP visits. Participants assigned to a primary care facilitation and transportation arm did not have a significantly lower time to have a primary care provider visit (Figures S1 and S2; Table 4).
