## ABSTRACT

ach year cardiovascular disease accounts for 45% of Eall deaths in Europe and 37% of all European Union deaths.1 CAD represents the largest proportion of all cardiovascular disease deaths. With such a high mortality

rate, high expenses are provided to prevent CAD s, their treatment, and care, becoming a major socio-economic problem. Mortality reduction of CAD cases is associated with an improved profile of cardiovascular risk factors.

These factors are major goals for cardiac rehabilitation (CR ) / secondary prevention strategy. Physical exercise is an essential component of CR , which leads to numerous health benefits in the secondary prevention of coronary heart disease (CAD ).2 Research in center-based cardiac rehabilitation (CBCR ) exercise programs has demonstrated positive effectiveness of exercise in reducing cardiovascular mortality, morbidity, and the number of rehospitalizations in patients with CAD .3 However, despite global recommendations and the recognized benefits provided by comprehensive CR , patients’ participation is low in many countries.4, 5 The main reasons for non-participating eligible patients in the CBCR include inappropriate advice from healthcare professionals and personal or technical barriers.6 In the context of barriers, it has been found that patients often report the facility distance, commuting, or inappropriate CBCR time as an obstacle.7 Development in information and communication technologies (ICT ), such as wearable sensors, smartphones, and the internet, can address the mentioned barriers and tailor CR to each patient’s needs.8, 9 Alternative model of CR delivery, which uses ICT communication between the patient and the long-distance healthcare provider, is a home-based cardiac telerehabilitation (HBCT ) model.10 Current review studies suggest that HBCT is a feasible, safe method and could be a suitable alternative for CBCR to improve cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF).11, 12 Most of the telerehabilitation studies included have focused on the short-term effect of physical exercise. Only 2 (17%) studies examined the effect of a long-term program, at least one year after the intervention. As a result, only limited data describing a long-term effect of HBCT are available. The study’s outcomes suggest that HBCT is as effective in maintaining improvements as CBCR intervention programs. However, only a small number of participants and different design of the intervention and the CR phase limit this result, offering new research possibilities.

In this paper, we reported the long-term effects of the Cardiac Rehabilitation via Global Position System (CR - GPS ) study. The CR -GPS study13, 14 was a randomized controlled trial that examined the feasibility and effect of a 12-week HBCT during phase two (II phase) of CR . The study aimed to investigate a 1-year effect of the CR -GPS on CRF , HR QL, anthropometric characteristics, and rehospitalization rate in patients with CAD compared to outpatient CR . We assumed that patients enrolled in HBCT would show higher pVO at 1-year follow-up post the intervention than patients from the control group CBCR .

# Materials and methods
During the period from August 2018 to May 2019, we performed a randomized control study CR -GPS . A more detailed description of the experimental methods and the concept were published in the study protocol.13 The short-term results of the 12-week intervention were also reported.14 The data presented in this study were obtained one year after the end of the intervention. A brief description of the population and the design of the CR -GPS study is included.

## Design and intervention

The research was carried out as a prospective mono-centric randomized control study in cardiac patients at the Department of Cardiology in cooperation with the Rehabilitation Department of the University Hospital Brno, Czech Republic. The study corresponded to the Helsinki Declaration of Ethical Principles for Medical Research, in which human subjects participate, and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University Hospital Brno, Czech Republic. Patients signed an informed consent form before entering the study. According to the World Health Organization recommendations, the study was prospectively registered in July 2018 in the Register of Clinical Trials with International Operations under registration number ACTR N12618001170213.

Study patients were more than 18 years old and were diagnosed with CAD (angina pectoris, myocardial infarction in the last six months, with left ventricular ejection fraction >45%) with low to moderate cardiovascular risk.15 All patients were after cardiac revascularization (percutaneous coronary intervention or coronary artery bypass graft), and they were recommended pharmacological treatment.16 They were assessed by a cardiologist, whether their clinical condition allowed CP ET participation.

All patients had to own ICT equipment (personal computer, telephone or mobile connection, and internet access) and were able to operate them. Eligible patients meeting all prescribed conditions signed an informed consent form and were included in the study. All the baseline assessments were performed at the hospital clinic. Patients obtained a personal questionnaire (sex, age, diagnosis and pharmacological treatment), a list of study information, and an educational booklet (healthy diet advice, cardiovascular risk factors management, and smoking cessation). A 1:1 randomization followed initial examinations, and patients were assigned to the HBCT group or the CBCR group.

The intervention was based on the principles of II . phase of CR and consisted of regular physical exercise

in the patient’s home environment and teleconsultations. The 12-week physical intervention was aimed mainly at increasing CRF . Two mandatory training sessions initiated HBCT at the clinic under a physiotherapist’s guidance and a cardiologist’s supervision. During the pilot sessions, the patients were instructed how to exercise (load time, intensity) and were lent the HR Polar M430 wrist monitor (Kempele, Finland). Subsequently, they were explained how to operate the device and the possibilities of sharing training data, for which a web account was created in the PolarFlow application.

The HBCT program consisted of physical exercise 3 times a week, for 60 minutes at an intensity of 70-80% of HR reserve. Once a week, each patient received a telephone consultation (feedback, motivation, education) based on the telemonitoring. Using the Global Position System, the physiotherapist supervised patient’s training sections and gave telephone feedback once a week. Feedback was set for a specific day and time. The physiotherapist contacted the monitored patient, and the history of the training activity was consulted in terms of intensity, duration, and inquiries related to the training were addressed, and the patient was motivated for the next period.

Patients allocated to CBCR started the traditional program of the II . phase of CR under the direct supervision of a physiotherapist and cardiologist in the University Hospital Brno, Czech Republic. CBCR was an active control group in which patients trained three times a week for 60 minutes at an intensity of 70-80% HR reserve.

After completing the 12-week intervention, all patients were supported in their independent continuation and physical exercise adherence. No further contact was made during the following period of 1 year to ensure compliance after the intervention.

## Data collection

Study data were collected at baseline after the intervention and 12 months later. The primary outcome was the change in CRF expressed by the pVO parameter. The secondary outcomes were measurements of anthropometric parameters, HR QL, and the number of hospitalizations and deaths. One year after the intervention, patients were asked to perform a CP ET at the clinic and complete a personal questionnaire.

## Cardiopulmonary exercise test

Determination of pVO was performed using an automated metabolic system, Metalyzer 3b (Biophysics GmbH, Leipzig, Germany), and a progressive incremental CP ET

on an Ergoselect 100 bicycle ergometer (Ergoline Bitz, Germany) up to the symptoms limited maximum of each patient. CP ET was performed according to the guidelines of the European Society of Cardiology and the American Cardiology Association.17 A CP ET ramp protocol was used, consisting of 2-minute warming up to 10 watts, followed by a 15-watt increment every minute for men and a 10-watt increment for women with a final 2-minute recovery at 10 watts. Measuring HR with a standard 12-lead electrocardiogram, gas exchange, and blood pressure were continuously recorded and evaluated during CP ET.

Blood pressure was measured manually with a CA -MI tonometer (Parma, Italy) every 2 minutes. CP ET was terminated upon subjective exhaustion, symptoms, or changes in the electrocardiogram. A cardiologist was present during the testing for an immediate consultation or in case any possible complications occurred. After the test, the cardiologist examined the abnormalities on the electrocardiogram. The variables obtained were based on previously published CP ET studies.18

# Secondary outcomes
HR QL was evaluated using the Short-Form-36 (SF -36) questionnaire. The questionnaire version was translated into Czech.19 A normative representative study of validity, reliability, sensitivity, and difficulty of the European population tasks was demonstrated for this questionnaire.20 The questionnaire enables to quantify individual sub-areas of health conditions. It contains 36 items arranged into eight sub-areas of health status, physical activity limitations, limitations caused by emotional problems, vitality, general mental health, social activity, physical pain, a general perception of health, and a general change in health. All subareas together indicate the overall condition of the patient. Within each sub-area, individual items are coded, summed, and transformed to a scale from 0 to 100, with 100 indicating excellent health and 0 poor health.21 The SF-36 questionnaire was selected in our study as an evaluation tool for its high reliability and wide usability in studies focusing on CR programs.22 Anthropometric characteristics (Body Mass Index and waist circumference) were also measured. Input data on hospitalization were obtained subjectively through the patient’s report. Objective control of hospitalization was obtained using the database of the hospital information system. Inputs were divided into acute hospitalizations or contacts in emergency rooms. All-cause mortality was recorded using the register of the Institute of Health Information and Statistics of the Czech Republic one year after the end of the intervention of the last patient.

## Statistical analysis

The calculation of the study sample size was presented in a short-term study based on a power analysis. All statistical analyses were reported according to CO NSORT guidelines.23 Statistical assessments of CRF and HR QL levels among patient groups after 1-year follow-up were analyzed using a two-sample t-test. An effect size analysis was performed for the primary outcome – Cohens-d. Assessment of group differences over time was analyzed using a paired t-test. Data were expressed using mean and standard deviation. Statistically significant differences in time or between groups were reported to have a statistical significance P<0.05. All study data were processed and analyzed in Statistica 12 (TIBCO , Software Inc, Palo Alto, CA , USA ) statistical software.

# Results
At baseline, 56 patients were enrolled in the CR -GPS interventional study, mostly men (80%), with an average age of 57. Primary outcome expressed as \mathrm { \ p V O } _ { 2 }showed significant improvement (P<0.01) in both study groups without significant between-group differences after 12 weeks of intervention (HBCT group difference: 2.8 ml/ kg/min versus the CBCR group 2.5 mL/kg/min, P=0.59). The 12-week CR -GPS intervention completion rate was high, with 51 (91%) patients (42 men and 9 women) completing.

A total of 7 patients were lost during the 1-year followup after the CR -GPS intervention (CBCR group N.=5 and HBCT group N.=2; P=0.18). The loss of follow-up patients (4 cases) was caused by non-participation in the follow-up evaluation due to the CO VID -19 pandemic. In 1 case, the patient had a joint replacement; in 1 case, there was nonparticipation due to malignant illness, and in 1 case, the patient refused to participate. Finally, 44 (76%) patients participated in the 1-year follow-up analysis (CBCR group

N.=21 and HBCT group N.=23). Figure 1 shows the study development, randomization, and follow-up period. At the beginning of the study, the groups were similar concerning demographic and clinical characteristics (Table I). The

demographic characteristics of patients who dropped out were not statistically different.

CRF results were performed using CP ET, with no adverse events recorded. Fifteen months after baseline, the average CRF measured by pVO was higher in the HBCT patient group (25.5 mL/kg/min) than in the active CBCR control group (23.6 mL/kg/min). The difference between the groups was statistically significant at P<0.05 (Table II). In both groups, the improvement in CRF was maintained after long-term follow-up, CBCR by 2.7% vs. HBCT by 7.8% (Figure 2), only for HBCT the long-term effect was statistically significant (P=0.17 vs. P=0.01). The effect size showed a medium effect of treatment in the telerehabilitation group (d=0.52) and a low effect in the center-based group of patients (d=0.21).

Additional physical test results did not show statistically significant differences between groups (Table I), including peak workload (P=0.11), maximum HR (P=0.24), and peak respiratory exchange ratio (RER, P=0.22).

No statistically significant difference between groups was found in the HR QL assessment or any of the eight sub-areas. There was a statistically significant improvement in HR QL in the General health area in both groups after long-term follow-up of CBCR (53.4, P=0.01) and HBCT (60.1, P=0.01). Anthropometric parameters did not change significantly between groups even after long-term follow-up (Table II ).

No patient in the study died during the study period. A

total of 8 patients were hospitalized during the one-year follow-up (CBCR : N.=5, HBCT : N.=3; P=0.37) due to angina pectoris (N.=3), selective coronarography (N.=2), percutaneous intervention (N.=2), acute heart failure (N.=1). In addition, 10 emergency contacts were made (CBCR : N.=4, HBCT: N.=6; P=0.58) due to arterial fibrillation (N.=3), back pain (N.=2), treatment-related injury (N.=2), fracture (N.=1), burns (N.=1), difficulty breathing (N.=1).
