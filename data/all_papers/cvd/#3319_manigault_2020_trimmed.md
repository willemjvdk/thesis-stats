## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# 1 | METHODS
This was a prospective, multicenter, randomized, controlled trial conducted at three sites in Atlanta, Georgia. This study was reviewed and approved by Mercer University and associated health care systems' Institutional Review Boards (IRB). The study is registered on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT04066010).

Enrollment began in October 2016 and was completed in May 2018, with data collection completed in November 2018. Patients who received care at these ambulatory care clinics (one family medicine, one internal medicine, and one heart failure clinic) were eligible to participate if they were at least 18 years of age, diagnosed with hypertension as evidenced by International Statistical Classification of Diseases, 10th edition (ICD-10) codes or chart documentation, prescribed at least one antihypertensive medication for a minimum of 3 months prior to enrollment, had access to a compatible Android mobile device with data capabilities, and consented to using the app on their device. Patients were excluded if they did not read or speak English or were unable to consent due to literacy or cognitive impairment. Eligible patients were prescreened using institutional scheduling systems and recruited from the clinics' waiting rooms either before or after a visit. Patients were required to sign an informed consent and a Health Insurance Privacy and Accountability Act (HIPAA) waiver prior to study enrollment. The study outcomes included assessment of the change in medication adherence as defined by the cumulative medication gap (CMG) and the evaluation of change in BP.

The study timeline is shown in Figure 1. Upon study enrollment, a member of the research team collected patient demographics (age, gender, race, marital status, insurance status, annual income, and selected comorbidities). Additionally, clinical information was

collected relating to antihypertensive medications, BP measurement, and lifestyle habits (activity level, diet, smoking, and alcohol use). Blood pressure goals were programmed into the app at the time of study enrollment. Of note, hypertension guidelines were updated during the study enrollment period; therefore, a participant's BP goal reflected the most current guideline recommendations at the time of patient enrollment.11,20 The short-form Test of Functional Health Literacy in Adults (s-TOFHLA) was administered to assess health literacy.

The customized mobile app, BP-n-Me, was developed by the research team, which consisted of clinical pharmacists and pharmaceutical researchers with support from technology consultants. In order to ensure that the sample size was sufficient to assess the intervention, study participants were randomized to either the intervention or control group in a 2:1 ratio during the 3-month study period. Subjects in the intervention group received a full version of this app while subjects in the control group received a limited version. The World Health Organization (WHO) suggests several strategies that may be effective as adherence-enhancing interventions including self-monitoring, goal setting, corrective feedback, and reinforcement.4 The clinical pharmacists involved in this research were board-certified pharmacists practicing in ambulatory care, well aware of the adherence challenges that occur in patients with chronic disease states. Thus, the WHO recommendations were incorporated to develop targeted, individualized interventions (eg, patient education, feedback) in the full version of the app. The full version of the BP-n-Me app consisted of a series of features as depicted in the Supporting Information. Features included: (1) Calendar reminders of when to take medications and the patient's antihypertensive medication regimen with a log; (2) “Call your Pharmacy” button specific to a patient's dispensing pharmacy; (3) BP log in which the patient could enter BP values that were automatically compared with goal values; (4) Lifestyle survey; and (5) Patient education for lifestyle factors individually tailored to each patient. Education, in the form of “Tips of the Day,” was tailored based on patient responses to the lifestyle survey. The limited version of the BP-n-Me app only allowed participants to enter their BP values and did not provide any additional functionality or app engagement. Study personnel downloaded the applicable version of the BP-n-Me app onto study patients' phones, trained the patients on how to use the app, and gave all patients a copy of the pamphlet

“Blood Pressure and Your Health” developed by the American Society of Hypertension at the initial visit. Patients in the intervention group were asked to complete the survey of lifestyle habits in the BP-n-Me app at baseline, and at the end of months one and two of the study period.

All patients received standard of care throughout the study period; therefore, changes could be made to the patients' antihypertensive regimen. All patients completed the lifestyle habits and applicable medication adherence survey at the 3-month follow-up appointment. In addition, data from the patient's BP-n-Me app was downloaded and the patient's BP was reassessed during follow-up. During the follow-up appointment, patients received a nominal monetary stipend for their participation.

Refill history was assessed retrospectively for 3 months before study enrollment, during the 3-month study period, and prospectively for 3 months following the study period. The CMG was selected as the preferred adherence measure because adherence at the beginning of an assessment period is not influenced by premature refills, but early refills can fill gaps later.21 Therefore, adherence for medications without multiple refill dates was not included in the analysis. The CMG was assessed by contacting patients' community pharmacies and obtaining patients' antihypertensive medication refill histories or evaluating refill records available through the institution's electronic health record. The CMG is the number of days in which the medication was not available (gap) between each prescription fill, divided by the number of days between the first and last medication fill during the period. The CMG is expressed as either a continuous proportion or is dichotomized at the cutoff point of 0.2, and classified as either adherent (<0.20) or nonadherent (≥0.20).21 A total of three CMGs were calculated from pre-, during-, and postapp uses for each of the patients enrolled in the study.

## 1.1 | Statistical analysis

Statistical comparison of baseline demographics was conducted to assess the differences in medians and frequencies of covariates between groups postrandomization. Categorical covariates were summarized by frequency, and statistical comparison between groups was accomplished using Fisher's exact tests. Continuous covariates were summarized by median and interquartile range (IQR). Statistical comparison of continuous covariates between groups was conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05 for all analyses. Statistically relevant differences in baseline demographics postrandomization were addressed through propensity score matching (PSM). Briefly, a regression model was defined to predict the propensity (ie, probability) of treatment group membership based on baseline patient characteristics. Subjects were matched with replacements based on propensity score. Subjects with no match were not included in the adjusted dataset. Covariates with significant differences between groups at baseline were tested for inclusion in the regression model. Change in the distribution of propensity score after matching was used to confirm improvement in covariate balance

between groups (see Appendix 1). PSM was accomplished via the Pymatch package for Python (version 3.7). Missing data were accounted for using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE). The multiple imputation methodology and statistical analysis were conducted using the StatsModels (version 0.9.0) package. The MICE implementation in the StatsModels package defines a series of regression models that treats each variable with missing data as the dependent variable in a regression. The remaining variables are treated as the independent variables used to predict the missing variable. The algorithm then cycles through each model and the missing values are generated from random draws from the predictive distributions of the regression models.22

The primary outcomes were median CMG and systolic and diastolic BP at the 3-month follow-up visit. The secondary outcome was the proportion of subjects with uncontrolled blood pressure at the 3-month follow-up visit. Additional outcomes investigated included change in CMG over the 90-day study period (from 3 months prior to enrollment to the 3-month postfollow-up visit) and change in systolic and diastolic BP from enrollment to the 3-month follow-up visit. Primary outcomes were analyzed using Theil-Sen Siegel regression. Theil-Sen Siegel regression is a nonparametric, distribution-free method for linear regression. This regression procedure was chosen as a conservative test of medians which performs well in the presence of outliers and heteroscedasticity.23 The R implementation of this estimator calculates the medians of the n−1 slopes between observations in the control and intervention groups. The slope of the regressor is then taken as the median of the resulting n medians. Further information on Theil-Sen Siegel regression are referenced.24 The secondary outcome was analyzed using logistic regression. Confidence intervals (CIs) for the median change in CMG and systolic and diastolic BP over the study period were estimated using a bootstrap method with 1000 samples. In a subgroup analysis, we investigated the impact of the intervention on the primary and secondary outcomes in a subgroup of 32 patients from the intention to treat population who were nonadherent at baseline.

# 2 | RESULTS
A total of 413 patients were screened for inclusion; 55 met eligibility criteria and were enrolled (Figure 2). Thirty-nine patients were randomized to the intervention group and 16 to the control group. Thirty-five patients completed the study, 23 in the intervention group and 12 in the control group. Table 1 depicts the baseline characteristics of the study population after PSM. Regarding the total PSM study population, approximately half of patients were Caucasian. The majority of patients in both groups were between the ages of 31 and 50 years. Additionally, 41% of the total study population was female. Half the patients in the study had some form of governmentsponsored insurance (eg, Medicare, Medicaid, or insurance through the health insurance marketplace). Finally, approximately 47% of patients had an annual income of less than $30 000. After PSM to adjust for differences in CMG at baseline, statistically significant

differences between groups remained for ethnicity, insurance status, income, number of medications, and the incidence of dyslipidemia.

# 2.1 | Study outcomes (PSM population)
Three months before study enrollment, the median CMG for the control group was similar to the median CMG for the intervention group (0.21 vs 0.25; P = .92). This trend persisted during the study (0.20 vs 0.19; P = .61) and remained relatively unchanged after study completion (0.20 vs 0.20; P = .83). There were no statistically significant differences in CMG between time periods for either group. The bootstrap estimate for the median change in CMG for the control group was 0.00 (95% CI [−0.05, 0.00]) and 0.05 (95% CI [0.00 to 0.05]) from before the study to during the study and during the study to after study completion, respectively. The bootstrap estimate for the median change in CMG for the intervention group was −0.03

(95% CI [−0.13 to 0.03]) and 0.00 (95% CI [−0.02, 0.1]) from before the study to during the study and during the study to after study completion, respectively. Nonparametric regression analysis showed the intervention to be associated with a nonsignificant decrease of 0.02 in CMG favoring the intervention group at the 3-month follow-up visit, and a negligible change in CMG at the 3-month postfollow-up visit (Table 2).

Median systolic BP for the control and intervention groups were not significantly different at enrollment (132 vs 141 mmHg; P = .46) or at the 3-month follow-up visit (130 vs 128 mmHg; P = .11). Median diastolic BP at enrollment and the 3-month follow-up visit was also similar between groups (84 vs 85 mmHg; P = .92) and (79 vs 78 mmHg; P = .86). The intervention group had a significantly lower median systolic BP at the 3-month follow-up visit compared with BP at enrollment (141 vs 128 mmHg; P = .001). Likewise, both groups also demonstrated a significantly lower median diastolic BP at the 3-month follow-up visit compared with BP at enrollment (84 vs

79 mmHg; P = .0004) and (85 vs 78; P = .009), control and intervention groups, respectively. The bootstrap estimate for the median change in systolic BP from enrollment to the 3-month follow-up visit was −2.00 mmHg (95% CI [−24.00, 10.00]) and −11.00 mmHg (95% CI [−17.00, −4.0]) for the control and intervention groups, respectively. The bootstrap estimate for the median change in diastolic BP from enrollment to the 3-month follow-up visit was −4.00 mmHg (95% CI [−12.00, 0.00]) and −5.00 mmHg (95% CI [−16.00, 1.00]) for the control and intervention groups, respectively. Nonparametric regression estimates indicated that the intervention was associated with a nonsignificant decrease of 2 and 1 mmHg in median systolic and diastolic BPs, respectively.

As a secondary outcome, the impact of the intervention on the ability of patients to meet their target BP was investigated. Patient BP targets were assigned individually by their primary care physician according to current clinical guideline standards at study enrollment.11,20 There was an increase in the proportion of patients who met their guideline-driven BP goal by the 3-month follow-up visit for both groups, however, the increase was significant among patients in the intervention group (51.3% vs 46.2%; P = .63 and 61.5% vs 41.0%; P = .01) control and intervention groups, respectively. Odds ratios (ODs) suggest that patients in the intervention group were 36% less likely to have uncontrolled hypertension than patients in the control group at the 3-month follow-up visit (OR 0.64; P = 0.35).

In a subgroup analysis, the impact of the intervention was examined on 11 control and 22 intervention patients who had poor

medication adherence (defined by a CMG > 0.2) at enrollment. Over half (53.1%) of patients in this subgroup were between the ages of 31 and 50, 56.3% were female, 50% were African American, 62.5% had some form of government-sponsored insurance (eg, Medicare, Medicaid, or insurance through the health insurance marketplace), and 65.6% of patients had an annual income ≤ $30 000. Effect estimates for the intervention in the nonadherent subgroup are presented in Table 3. The intervention group demonstrated a significant decrease of 0.06 and 0.07 in CMG during the study and after study completion, respectively. The intervention group also showed a significant decrease of 12 mmHg in median systolic BP and a nonsignificant decrease of 1 mmHg in median diastolic BP at the 3-month postfollow-up visit compared with BP at enrollment. Overall, this translated to a 74% reduction in the risk of having a BP above goal in intervention patients compared with patients in the control group at the 3-month follow-up visit.
