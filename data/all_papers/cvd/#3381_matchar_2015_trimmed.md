# Methods
## Design overview

The Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) Cooperative Studies Program (CSP) #481, ‘‘The Home INR Study’’ (THINRS) was a prospective, unblinded RCT of the clinical impact of PST. The design of the THINRS trial has been described previously [16]. In brief, the study enrolled patients with a history of AF, MHV, or both, who required long-term AC with warfarin initiated by a clinical care provider for an indeterminate period.

The institutional review board for each participating VA site approved the substudy, which was conducted in accordance with the amended protocol for the parent study (available with the full text of [13]).

## Setting and patients

Data were collected at 28 VA medical centers with AC clinics that met guidelines for high quality anticoagulation management (HQACM), as defined in the Managing Anticoagulation Services Trial (MAST) [17] and that were treating at least 400 patients. PST testing was performed using FDA-approved ProTime Microcoagulation System (International Technidyne Corporation) INR meters.

## Randomization and interventions

In Part 1 of the trial, consenting patients were screened, trained and tested for competency in using the INR monitor over a 2–4 week period. In Part 2, individuals capable of performing PST and who gave a separate, written,

informed consent were randomized with equal allocation to either a PST arm with regularly scheduled testing or an AC clinic management arm (HQACM) with testing every 4 weeks. In the substudy sites, subjects assigned to PST in Part 2 were randomized to one of three test frequencies: once every 4 weeks, once a week, and twice a week. THINRS patients were followed for 2–4.75 years. The primary endpoint for the parent study was time to first event among major bleed, stroke, and death, whereas for the substudy, the primary endpoint was TTR at 1-year follow-up with major events and quality of life as secondary endpoints.

Substudy enrollment lasted 34 months (from August 2003 through May 2006), and patients were followed for a minimum of 24 months. To achieve 80 % power to identify a 10 % absolute difference in TTR (e.g., 50 vs. 60 %), with a standard deviation of 0.25, the minimal sample size was estimated to be 100 subjects per arm. Randomization to the three PST test frequencies was performed with equal probability until each group had enough subjects to adjust for an anticipated first year dropout rate of about 15 %. Thereafter, all PST subjects were assigned to test once weekly.

# Outcomes and follow-up
HQACM was defined as care that met the following criteria based on the deliberations of an expert panel [18]: a designated, trained staff responsible for patients’ visits and follow-up; the use of a standard, local procedure at each site for AC management; and the performance of regular INR testing approximately once per month. For self-testing, patients used an approved device at home and recorded the results by means of an interactive telephone voiceresponse reporting system with Web-based local monitoring. If the patient reported a measurement outside the assigned INR range or reported having been hospitalized, the reporting system instructed the patient to contact the study staff for dosing instructions or to provide additional information. The reporting system prevented the patient from reporting additional INR results until the out-of-range test result was addressed. Otherwise, the reporting system recorded the result and instructed the patient to continue testing at the assigned frequency.

Follow-up study visits were scheduled every 3 months after randomization to collect data on medical events, concomitant medications, adverse events, and changes in the target INR range in the interval since the previous visit. Patients in the self-testing group were checked quarterly for meter-use competency (see the Supplementary Appendix in [13]) and were given additional testing supplies.

The pre-specified primary endpoint of the substudy was the TTR at 1-year follow-up (this time point was chosen so

that even if the main study terminated early, there would be data to address the PST test frequency question), calculated on the basis of linear interpolation [19]. The pre-specified secondary endpoints were: (1) the components of a first major event (stroke, major bleeding episode or death) over the entire length of follow-up, as adjudicated by the Duke Clinical Research Institute without knowledge of treatment assignments; (2) quality of life at 1 year as measured by Health Utility Index Mark 3 (HUI) [20]; (3) AC quality of life at 1 year as measured by the Duke Anticoagulation Satisfaction Scale (DASS) [21].

## Statistical analysis

All analyses were based on intention to treat with a 0.05 level of significance. SAS version 9.1.3 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) was used for all analyses. Four-group comparisons of continuous variables (such as TTR and DASS) used the Kruskal–Wallis test while those for categorical variables used the Fisher exact test or Chi squared test, as appropriate.

A comparison of the distributions of time to earliest of death, major bleed, or stroke by treatment group assignment was performed via the log-rank test according to the Kaplan–Meier method. For each of the PST treatment groups, the treatment effect relative to HQACM was estimated using Cox proportional hazards with the Wald Chi squared test determining significance of the treatment effect. No other covariates were considered, nor were any subgroup analyses performed due to the substudy not being powered to detect such differences.

Pairwise comparisons of median 1-year TTR were performed using the Wilcoxon rank sum test.

# Results
## Characteristics of the patients

Study flow is shown in Fig. 1. Notably, the majority of patients who underwent training in Part 1 were assessed as competent and were randomized to Part 2 (787/841 patients, 76 %).

Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics of the patients; there were no statistically significant differences between treatment arms. The majority of subjects were men (98 %) and white (91 %). Overall mean age was 67.1 years; patients with a MHV were somewhat younger than those without a MHV (68.3 vs. 63.3 years). The most common indication for AC was AF only (602/787 patients, 77 %); 129 patients (16 %) had MHV without AF; and 56 (7 %) had both AF and MHV. Mean \mathrm { C H A D S } _ { 2 }score was 1.99, with the majority of subjects having hypertension (75 %). About one-third of subjects were on antiplatelet agents (aspirin and/or clopidogrel), and 8 % were taking amiodarone.

## Primary end point

During the period of the substudy, the TTR through the first test 1-year post-randomization was significantly different across the four groups ( p = 0 . 0 0 6 8 )and modestly higher for the groups that underwent PST more often (Table 2). Patients who self-tested twice weekly had the highest mean (±SD) TTR among the four groups ( 6 6 . 8 \pm 1 3 . 2 \% )), followed by patients who self-tested weekly (63.3 ± 14.3 %). Patients who self-tested every 4 weeks had the lowest mean TTR ( 5 9 . 9 \pm 1 6 . 7 \% ), whereas the mean TTR of those who underwent in-clinic testing via HQACM every 4 weeks was modestly higher ( 6 0 . 8 \pm 2 0 . 4 \% )(Fig. 2).

In a post hoc analysis, several additional measures of INR performance to target values were assessed, including percentage of patients whose INR values were within range less than half of the time (i.e., a TTR of less than 50 %), percentage of INR results in target range, and percent of INR results highly out of range ( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } \leq 1 . 5 \ \mathrm { ~ o r ~ } \geq 4 . 0 )(Table 2). In all cases, INR performance was statistically significantly better as testing frequency increased.
