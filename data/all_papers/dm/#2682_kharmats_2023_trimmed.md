# Methods
## Study objective and approvals

The Personal Diet Study was a randomized controlled trial to compare the effects of personalized and standardized diet interventions on weight loss in participants with prediabetes or moderately controlled T2D (clinicaltrials.gov identifier: NCT03336411). The trial was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in

2013) and was approved by the NYU Grossman School of Medicine Institutional Review Board (IRB #17-00741). Informed consent was obtained from all study participants. A detailed study design and intervention protocol have been published elsewhere and are briefly described here [22,23]. The goal of this report was to examine between-group differences in GV and HbA1c at 3-mo and 6-mo follow-ups.

## Participants

Participants were recruited primarily from NYU Langone Health (NYULH) between January 2018 and March 2021. Potentially eligible participants received an IRB approved message on their MyChart patient portals, where a brief overview of the study and an invitation to self-refer were provided. Participants were also recruited from clinicalt rials.gov and iConnect, which is a NYULH-hosted website outlining all active clinical trials conducted at NYULH and can serve as a platform for self-referral. English-speaking adults aged 18 to 80 y, with comorbid overweight or obesity (27 \mathrm { k g / m } ^ { 2 } \le \mathrm { B M I } \le 5 0 ~ \mathrm { k g / m } ^ { 2 } )and prediabetes or moderately controlled T2D ( 5 . 7 \% \leq \mathrm { H b A l c } \leq 8 \% )managed with lifestyle alone or lifestyle plus metformin, and with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) > 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 , were eligible for the study. Supplemental Table 1 summarizes the detailed eligibility criteria.

## Screening and data collection procedures

Prescreening to determine potential eligibility and interest was conducted over telephonic conversation. Before the COVID-19 pandemic, potential participants were scheduled for an in-person visit at the Clinical Research Center at the NYULH Clinical and Translational Science Institute (CRC-CTSI), to obtain consent and additional screening measurements (HbA1c and eGFR). Initially, height was measured to the nearest 1 cm using a SECA 213 Stadiometer (Seca GmBH), and weight measured to the nearest 0.1 kg in light clothing without shoes using a Stow-Weight scale (Scale-Tronix).

Owing to the COVID-19 pandemic, after March 15, 2020, inperson screening, consent, and measurement procedures were conducted through videoconferencing (Cisco Webex). Weight was obtained from an electronic scale shipped to potential participants (RENPHO; Joicom), and data were transferred through Bluetooth to a corresponding mobile application that study staff were able to access electronically. Height was self-reported. HbA1c and eGFR values were obtained from electronic medical records if performed within the last 6 mo. Otherwise, potential participants were guided through Webex to obtain point-of-care HbA1c using the study-provided A1cNow monitor (PTS Diagnostics). Kidney disease diagnosis was used in lieu of eGFR data if eGFR data were not available.

## Randomization

Randomization was performed in blocks of 4 to equally allocate participants to one of the 2 arms: standardized or personalized. Each study ID was preassigned a randomization arm. As will be discussed in the Intervention Strategy further, blood testing and stool samples were required by the PNP algorithm for predicting PPGRs in personalized arm participants. In addition, stool samples were shipped to Israel for processing and programming of the PNP application to provide PPGR feedback that was personalized to the individual participant. Because of limited resources and to minimize participant burden and delays in reprogramming of the PNP application, blood and stool samples were only collected from personalized arm participants at baseline. In other

words, randomization was performed before baseline assessment. However, randomization was performed by the study statistician, independent of measurements; the data reported in this study were derived from objective measures not subject to investigator bias, and participants remained blinded to randomization assignment until week 5 of the intervention.

## Intervention strategy

## Both groups

Participants in both groups received an intensive technology suppo rted behavioral intervention program targeting 7% weight loss and a calorie goal (deficit of 500 kcal/d). Originally, participants calorie goals were calculated using resting energy expenditure, measured using indirect calorimetry (Quark RMR; COSMED) with an activity factor of 1.4. In March 2020, owing to the COVID-19 pandemic, we discontinued the use of indirect calorimetry and calculated participants calorie goals using the Mifflin St-Jeor equation with an activity factor of 1.4 [24]. Participants engaged in registered dietitian-led, group-- based, behavioral counseling that was informed by Social Cognitive Theory [25] and the Diabetes Prevention Program [26]. Counseling was conducted using Webex weekly for 4 wk and then every other week for 20 wk. Sessions covered both educational content (e.g., obesity risks, benefits of weight loss, strategies for restricting calories, aerobic exercise and strength training, dealing with weight loss plateaus), and behavioral strategies (e.g., establishing relevance of behavior change, goal setting, self-reward, and problem solving around common barriers to weight loss success). To assure fidelity, each session was anchored by 2 brief (~5-minute) animated videos that were interspersed with open-ended questions designed to elicit discussion about experiences, values, and feelings; evaluate learning; and provide participants with the opportunity to practice applying new content (e.g., evaluating dietary intake, setting goals, and seeking solutions around barriers encountered). Each session lasted ~1 h. Participants were advised to gradually build to 150 min/wk of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity. They were also directed to self-monitor planned meals using the PNP mobile application for an ~5-mo period, starting from the first day of the intervention. The PNP application provided real-time feedback regarding dietary intake in relation to the intervention targets specific to their randomization assignment (see further). Participants were expected to enter their dietary intake data for ~5 mo.

For those who did not have a smartphone, a study phone was provided free of charge. Summary reports were sent to participants through e-mail every week for the first 4 wk that dietary selfmonitoring was expected and every other week for 20 wk [22,23]. These reports contained information about dietary self-monitoring adherence, weight loss trajectory, calorie targets, and dietary targets (Supplemental Figure 1A, B). Adherence to physical activity recommendations was not accessed, and feedback about physical activity was not provided to study participants.

## Standardized

Standardized arm participants were advised to consume <25% of their calories from total fat. The PNP mobile application provided realtime feedback regarding calorie intake and macronutrient distribution of meals and snacks logged by participants.

## Personalized

Personalized arm participants received the same PNP mobile application feedback as the standardized participants in addition to

meal scores reflecting their predicted PPGR derived from the PNP algorithm [19]. To generate meal scores, stool samples were collected from participants at baseline in the personalized arm only using an OMNIgene Gut stool collection kit (OMR-200; DNA Genotek). Stool samples were shipped to the Weizmann Institute for a microbiome analysis. Features of the gut microbiome were combined with participant-level data (HbA1c, anthropometrics, sociodemographics, and health variables) to generate personalized meal scores, which were displayed by the PNP application when planned meals or snacks were logged into the application. Meal scores were color-coded using a traffic-light motif with green scores indicating an excellent or very good PPGR, yellow scores indicating a good PPGR, and red scores indicating a bad or very bad PPGR. Participants were trained to experiment with different food choices (e.g., smaller serving sizes, different ingredients, and the addition of healthy fats) to improve their score while remaining under their calorie targets.

# Outcomes assessments
GV and HbA1c measurements were collected at baseline, 3 mo, and 6 mo.

## Glycemic variability

The primary objective of this manuscript was to examine the differences in the mean amplitude of glycemic excursions (MAGE) at 3 mo and 6 mo compared with those at baseline. Secondary end points included other measures of GV: mean of CGM glucose readings, SD, CV, and continuous overall net glycemic action.

Participants wore the Abbott FreeStyle Libre Pro CGM, which measured interstitial glucose values every 15 min for up to 14 d. Participants were blinded to the CGM data. As per manufacturer s information, data collected in the first 12 h of CGM placement may produce inaccurate readings related to calibration; therefore, these data were removed from the analysis [27]. Every 24-h period during which CGM failed to record 2 or more consecutive hours of glucose readings was excluded from analysis because this may result in unreliable MAGE estimates [28]. After missing glucose values were interpolated, GV measures were calculated for every 24-h period using EasyGV version 9.0.R2 (Nathan R. Hill, University of Oxford, United Kingdom) and the mean was determined across CGM collection days for baseline, 3-mo, and 6-mo assessments. Participants with at least 1 d of CGM data were included in the analytic sample.

## Hemoglobin A1c

Initially, HbA1c values were obtained at each assessment period at the CRC-CTSI through venipuncture and analyzed using high-pressure liquid chromatography (Variant II) Turbo analyzer. After the start of the COVID-19 pandemic, HbA1c values were obtained from patients electronic medical records or using A1cNow monitors that were shipped to participants homes at baseline only.

## Adverse event monitoring

All potential adverse events reported by participants were assessed by the principal investigator and research physician to determine severity and relatedness to study protocol. The institutional review board was informed of all reportable information. No severe adverse events were identified.

## Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistical analyses were conducted using STATA version 15.1 software (StataCorp). Using Statistical Analysis Software

version 9.4 (SAS Institute), we conducted intention-to-treat analyses. Initially, we used piecewise linear mixed models to test on whether GV s changing trend was different between 2 periods (0 3 mo compared with 3 6 mo) in each arm [29]. We divided the study period into 2 segments according to the study design: baseline to 3 mo and 3 mo to 6 mo, and then fitted the data using linear spline mixed models assuming different slopes within each segment but joined at 3 mo. Fixed effects were included for the study arm, 2 time periods (0 3 and 3 6 mo), and interactions of study arm and time period. A random effect was included for the participants. Covariates of age and sex were included for adjustment. The differences of the temporal treatment effects between the 2 periods (0 3 mo compared with 3 6 mo) in both arms were not significant. Therefore, we used linear mixed models to analyze GV, in which time period (0 6 mo), study arm, and the arm period interactions were modeled as fixed effects, and the participants were included as a random effect, while adjusting for covariates age and sex. With these models, we estimated the GV group means at 3 and 6 mo and GV s changing trend in each treatment group, and performed the hypothesis tests to compare the group difference in GV means at 3 and 6 mo and GV s changing trend from baseline to 6 mo. In all analyses, results were considered statistically significant if P values were -0.05 using a 2-sided test. We conducted post hoc subgroup analyses by sex. There was an insufficient number of Hispanic participants to conduct subgroup analyses by self-reported ethnicity.

## Sample size and power calculations

This study examined tertiary outcomes of the parent study, the Personal Diet Study. Therefore, the sample size was not calculated for the purpose of this study. The Personal Diet Study was designed to detect a between-group difference as small as 2% in weight loss (%) at the 6-mo assessment, with the type I error set at 0.05, and a power of 80%. A sample of at least 164 participants (82 per group) was needed. Because we expected a 20% attrition, the parent study aimed to complete 200 baseline assessments.

# Results
## Study participants

Of the 269 participants who completed the study screening, 239 met eligibility criteria, 20 declined to participate, 15 were lost to follow-up, and 204 were randomly assigned and completed the baseline assessment. Of those participants, 200 had HbA1c data (standardized 97, personalized 103) and 156 had least 1 d of CGM data (standardized 75, personalized  81) at baseline and were included in analyses. At baseline, the mean number of usable CGM days was 5.8 (SD 2.1) for the standardized diet arm and 5.4 d (SD 2.0) for the personalized diet arm. At 3 mo, it was 5.4 d (SD 1.5) and 5.6 d (SD 1.7) and, at 6 mo, 5.9 d (SD 1.7) and 5.5 d (SD 1.9) for the standardized diet and personalized diet arms, respectively. There was no statistically significant difference in CGM observation days between the groups. Figure 1 shows the CONSORT diagram with more details. Study enrollment ended owing to meeting enrollment requirements for the assessment of weight change outcome (primary outcome of the Personal Diet Study).

The final analysis sample consisted of 156 adults, most were female (66.5%, n  105), had a Bachelor degree or higher (69.5%, n 110), and had an annual household income of at least $75,000 (54.4%, n  86). ## Engagement with the intervention for participants included in the analytic sample

The mean attendance rate for participants in the standardized diet arm was 71.1% (SD 28.3%) of the 14 group counseling sessions, and 74.5% for participants in the personalized diet arm (SD 26.6%), with no between-group differences (P ¼ 0.95) (Supplemental Figure 2). By contrast, the mean percentage of days participants logged 50% of their daily caloric goal in the PNP mobile application was 33.1% (SD 34.2%) for the standardized diet arm, and 43.3% (SD  33.8%) for the personalized diet arm (Supplemental Figure 3). This difference was statistically significant ( P = 0 . 0 4 5 )). For both study arms, dietary selfmonitoring adherence decreased substantially over time and dropped to just >20% during the final week of self-monitoring.
