# A Randomized Controlled Trial to Improve Unmet Social Needs and Clinical Outcomes Among Adults with Diabetes
Minal R. Patel, PhD, MPH1 , Guanghao Zhang, MS2 , Michele Heisler, MD, MPA1,3,4, John D. Piette, PhD1,4, Kenneth Resnicow, PhD1 , Hae‑Mi Choe, PharmD5,6, Xu Shi, PhD2 , and Peter Song, PhD2

## ABSTRACT

BACKGROUND: Adults with type 1 or type 2 diabetes often face fnancial challenges and other unmet social needs to efective diabetes self-management.

OBJECTIVE: Whether a digital intervention focused on addressing socioeconomic determinants of health improves diabetes clinical outcomes more than usual care.

DESIGN: Randomized trial from 2019 to 2023.

PARTICIPANTS: A total of 600 adults with diabetes, HbA1c ≥ 7.5%, and self-reported unmet social needs or fnancial burden from a health system and randomized to the intervention or standard care.

INTERVENTION: CareAvenue is an automated, e-health intervention with eight videos that address unmet social needs contributing to poor outcomes.

MEASURES: Primary outcome was HbA1c, measured at baseline, and 6 and 12 months after randomization. Secondary outcomes included systolic blood pressure and reported met social needs, cost-related nonadherence (CRN), and fnancial burden. We examined main efects and variation in efects across predefned subgroups.

RESULTS: Seventy-eight percent of CareAvenue participants completed one or more modules of the website. At 12-month follow-up, there were no signifcant diferences in HbA1c changes between CareAvenue and control group (p = 0.24). There were also no signifcant between-group diferences in systolic blood pressure (p=0.29), met social needs (p=0.25), CRN (p=0.18), and perceived fnancial burden (p = 0.31). In subgroup analyses, participants with household incomes 100–400% FPL (1.93 (SE = 0.76), p < 0.01), 201–400% FPL (1.30 (SE=0.62), p<0.04), and>400% FPL (1.27 (SE=0.64), p < 0.05) had signifcantly less A1c decreases compared to the control group.

CONCLUSIONS: On average, CareAvenue participants did not achieve better A1c lowering, met needs, CRN, or perceived fnancial burden compared to control participants. CareAvenue participants with higher incomes achieved signifcantly less A1c reductions than control. Further research is needed on social needs

interventions that consider tailored approaches to population subgroups.

# METHODS
## Study Design

This was a two-arm randomized controlled trial comparing usual care with CareAvenue. All study procedures were reviewed and approved by the University of Michigan Institutional Review Board (IRB-MED HUM00149794). A study protocol is available elsewhere and describes inclusion criteria, recruitment, enrollment consent procedures, and intervention components.19

## Treatment Conditions

Treatment conditions included CareAvenue (intervention) and Guest Assistance Program (GAP) Services (usual care). Details of the interventions are described in the study protocol article.19 Briefy, CareAvenue is an interactive website designed to enhance participants’ capacity to address fnancial strain and other social needs while managing diabetes.19 Participants went through a series of eight videos lasting roughly 3 min each addressing themes including low-cost medicine and food insecurity. Participants also received weekly interactive voice response calls and text messages to encourage use of resources and diabetes self-management such as diet and physical activity recommendations for 1 year. Participants randomized to the control group were given contact information for the Guest Assistance Program (GAP). GAP social workers provide assistance and resources for medical and non-medical needs to patients receiving care within the local health system. Participants were responsible for reaching out to GAP. All participants received ongoing text messages to support their diabetes self-management, but only received GAP contact information once. Before March 2020 when COVID-19 restrictions began, participants had their initial session at the study clinic site, and then by phone when the pandemic began and remainder of the study.

## Study Setting and Participants

Between 2019 and 2023, potential participants were identifed via the University of Michigan’s Diabetes Research Registry and the electronic health record through Michigan Medicine. A sample size of 600 was estimated based on (1) 2-tailed test at an alpha level of 0.05, (2) a power of

0.80, (3) a mean diference of 0.42-unit change in HbA1c at 12 months between groups, and (4) a 15% attrition rate. Recruitment was stopped when adequate sample size was achieved. Study participants met the following criteria: (1) 18–75 years of age, (2) diagnosis of type 1 or type 2 diabetes with prescribed anti-hyperglycemic medication, (3) most recent (within 6 months) recorded HbA1c level of ≥ 7.5% for individuals ≤ 70 years and > 8.0% for individuals 70–75 years in age, (4) positive report of fnancial burden or CRN20,21, and (5) access to a mobile phone. Exclusion criteria included signifcant cognitive impairment as evidenced by inability to complete study intake procedures, and participation in another diabetes-related research study.

Recruitment staf made initial contact with potential participants via telephone and screened them for inclusion/ exclusion criteria. The consent was completed prior to baseline and described the voluntary and confdential nature of the study, and those who agree to participate would be randomly assigned to one of two groups who receive resources for better managing diabetes. They would participate in the trial for 1 year and complete study assessments at baseline, 6-month follow-up, and 12-month follow-up.

## Randomization

A block randomization schedule was created using SAS PROC PLAN to generate three blocks stratifed by income categories: very low, low, and middle/high.22,23 People with middle/high income were included in the study due to known fnancial burden and CRN observed across the socioeconomic spectrum.24 Participants were randomly assigned with a 1:1 allocation to treatment or control conditions within these pre-identified blocks based on their self-reported income. Study team members were blinded to treatment assignment, and randomized participants into their intervention assignment at the end of the baseline appointment by recording participants’ study IDs in the appropriate income category table of the randomization chart. The data manager incorporated the intervention assignments from the randomization chart into a crosswalk linking participants’ study IDs with their intervention assignments and randomization IDs.

Over the 4-year recruitment period (2019–2022), a total of 6055 potential participants were initially contacted, and 997 were confrmed eligible (Fig. 1). Of those, 667 (66%) consented to participate, and 600 provided completed baseline assessments and were randomized to the treatment or control group. Of 600 participants randomized, 464 (77%) completed 6-month follow-up surveys (follow-up 1), and 435 (72.5%) completed 12-month follow-up surveys (follow-up 2). There were 426 participants included in the analysis. There were 100 participants lost-to follow-up (54 intervention, 46 control, and 74 who withdrew from the study (54 intervention, 20 control). Those who withdrew were more likely to be younger (p < 0.01), and taking fewer medications (p<0.01) (Supplemental Table 1). Attrition was signifcantly

diferent between the 2 study arms (treatment [n = 86], control [n = 43]).

## Measures

Except during COVID-19 restrictions in 2019, those who consented to participate were scheduled for a visit with a study team member to complete baseline assessments. Inperson research appointments at our institution were prohibited from mid-March 2020 to August 2020. The protocol was changed to allow for remote administration of study assessments, and continued for the remainder of the study.

Standard demographic characteristics were collected. Using income and household size information, participants were classifed using percentiles relative to the US poverty level for 2019.22 Other data collected included the length of time since diagnosis with diabetes, number of chronic conditions, and type of diabetes (type 1 or type 2), and monthly out-of-pocket expenses for managing diabetes. Additional clinical measures included the 2-item validated Diabetes Distress Scale.25 Higher scores indicated greater distress.

Depressive symptoms were measured using the PHQ-4, with higher scores indicating greater severity of symptoms.26

# Primary Outcome
The main outcome of interest was HbA1c measured at baseline and 6- and 12-month follow-up using the DCA Vantage Analyzer prior to the start of the COVID-19 pandemic.27 For participants surveyed via telephone once the pandemic began, HbA1c was collected by the A1c Now home test kit.28 HbA1c was calibrated to ensure that the diferent modes of collection followed a similar empirical distribution to remove potential measurement bias.

# Secondary Outcomes
Secondary outcomes included systolic blood pressure, unmet social needs, cost-related non-adherence behaviors, and perceived fnancial burden. Systolic blood pressure and diastolic blood pressure was measured in millimeters of mercury (e.g., 120/80 mmHg) with an automated blood pressure machine.29

Unmet social and diabetes-related needs were measured using items adapted from several well-established social risk screeners.30–32 Domains included food, housing, energy/ utilities, income, employment, medical bills, and diabetes management needs. We measured social and diabetes-related needs that were met at follow-up.

Cost-related non-adherence (CRN) behaviors for diabetes were measured by asking participants if they engaged in any of the following behaviors during the last 12 months due to fnancial burden from diabetes: took less medications, skipped medication doses, delayed or decided not to fll a prescription, and delayed or decided not to see a healthcare provider (4-point Likert scale: never–often).20,32 Behaviors were analyzed as dichotomous variables, with “never” and “rarely” indicating “no” CRN, and “sometimes” and “often” indicating “yes” to CRN. We created a summary variable indicating CRN reported for any of the six items.

Perceived fnancial burden was assessed with the 11-item COST-FACIT measure.33,34 Each item was measured on a 5-point scale (0, not at all–4, very much), with recall based on the past 7 days related to their diabetes management. The COST-FACIT is scored by frst reverse coding six items, then taking the sum of all items, multiplying the sum by 11, and then dividing that number by the number of items answered.33,34 Lower scores indicated higher perceptions of fnancial burden.

## Data Analysis

Six-hundred participants completed baseline. At 12-month follow-up, there were 426 (71%) participants who had data for our primary outcome, HbA1c: 193 were in the CareAvenue arm and 233 were in the control arm. With 80% power at an alpha level of 0.05, this allowed us to detect a diference of 0.42 units in HbA1c at 12-month follow-up between arms.

Descriptive analyses (chi-square exact test and Student’s t-test) were conducted to examine baseline data by randomization status, and to compare diferences by treatment arm in outcomes by the three time points (baseline, 6 months, and 12 months) for our primary outcome, HbA1c.

Linear mixed models were used to examine diferences in primary and secondary outcomes between treatment and control over 6 and 12 months post-baseline. We started with a crude model adjusting for time (baseline, 6 months, and 12 months), intervention, and an interaction term between time and intervention in fxed efects. We included a random intercept and random slopes of time to account for participant-level heterogeneous trajectories. We then ft a fully adjusted model, further adjusting for age, sex, race/ethnicity, marital status, poverty level, education, employment status, insurance, monthly out-of-pocket spending, type of diabetes, years living with diabetes, number of chronic conditions, number of medications, depressive symptoms, diabetes distress score, and social support. Statistical signifcance was based on an alpha level of 0.05.

We also conducted an analysis to investigate the hypothesis that the diference between baseline to 12-month change scores across randomization groups would vary across unknown latent subgroups, which might be associated with key baseline characteristics of the participants. Key baseline characteristics of interest included age, sex, race/ethnicity, marital status, federal poverty level, monthly out-of-pocket spending, number of medications, COST-FACIT score, confdence requesting lower-cost resources, perception on amount of low-cost resources available to manage diabetes, type of diabetes, and unmet social needs. We ftted latentclass linear mixed models (LCLMMs) that implement two models simultaneously: a linear mixed model that allows for diferent subgroup-level treatment efects, and a latent subgroup membership model with similar interpretation to the ordinary multinomial logistic regression model. We implemented LCLMM using the R package lcmm. This approach was confrmed by an alternative EM-algorithm-based subgroup analysis approach.35

Specifcally, we ftted an unadjusted LCLMM with HbA1c measure at 12 months as the outcome, and we included treatment and HbA1c at baseline as covariates. With the crude LCLMM, we identifed variables that predict group membership. We then ftted a crude and a fully adjusted LCLMM with the identifed subgroup membership predictors, where the fully adjusted LCLMM further adjusted for age, sex, race/ethnicity, marital status, federal poverty level, education, employment status, insurance, monthly out-of-pocket spending, type of diabetes, years living with diabetes, number of chronic conditions, number of medications, depressive symptoms, diabetes distress scale, and social support in fxed efects of the linear mixed model.

We calculated the AICs for the LCLMM and subgroup treatment effects, which provided indication of distinct classes, to determine the number of latent classes. While the AIC for 2 latent classes was marginally diferent from that of 3 classes, we ended up choosing 3 latent classes because of the signifcant subgroup treatment efects observed.

The data sets generated during this study are not publicly available because it was not required at the time of the initial grant award, and as such, participants did not consent to future use of their data. Sharing the data publicly would require re-consenting and increase the risk of breach of confdentiality.

# RESULTS
Diferences between those who completed 12-month data collection and lost to follow-up were observed in terms of age, education, and type of diabetes, with those lost to follow-up being younger (p < 0.01), having less educational attainment ( p = 0 . 0 2 ), and higher prevalence of type 1 diabetes (p = 0.02) (Supplemental Table 2). Signifcant diferences were evident between those who remained in the study

and those who willingly dropped out, specifcally by age (p < 0.01) and number of medications (p < 0.01). Those who were younger and had fewer medications were more likely to drop out of the study.

Among those randomized to the intervention group, 20% completed the intervention (defned as completing all the modules of the website, and 75% of text messages and IVR calls), 78% partially completed the intervention, and 2% did not engage with the intervention defned as no log in or did not complete one module.
