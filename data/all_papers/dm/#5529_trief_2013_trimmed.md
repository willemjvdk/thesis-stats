# Adherence to diabetes self care for white, African-American and Hispanic American telemedicine participants: 5 year results from the IDEATel project
Paula M. Triefa,b*, Roberto Izquierdob , Joseph P. Eimickec , Jeanne A. Teresic,d,e, Robin Golandf , Walter Palmasf , Steven Sheag and Ruth S. Weinstockb,h

(Received 26 October 2010; final version received 4 June 2012)

Objectives. Adherence to diabetes self care is poor for Hispanic American and African-American patients. This study examined the change in adherence over time and in response to a telemedicine intervention for elderly diabetes patients in these groups compared to white diabetes patients. We also examined whether adherence mediated the effect of the intervention on glycemic control (A1c).

Design. The Informatics for Diabetes Education and Telemedicine project randomized medically underserved Medicare patients (n1665) to telemedicine case management (televideo educator visits, individualized goal-setting/problem solving) or usual care. Hispanic and African-American educators delivered the intervention in Spanish if needed.

Main outcome measures. Annual assessment included A1c and self-reported adherence (Summary of Diabetes Self-Care Activities scale). A simple model (only time and group terms) and a model with covariates (e.g., age) were examined for baseline and 5 years of follow-up. SAS PROC Mixed was used with non-linear terms to examine mediating effects of adherence on A1c, by performing tests of the mediating path coefficients.

Results. Over time, self-reported adherence improved for the treatment group compared to usual care (pB0.001). There was no significant interaction with racial/ethnic group membership, i.e., all groups improved. However, minority subjects were consistently less adherent than whites. Also, greater comorbidity and diabetes symptoms predicted poorer adherence, greater duration of diabetes and more years of education predicted better adherence. Adherence was a significant mediator of A1c (pB0.001).

Conclusions. A unique, tailored telemedicine intervention was effective in achieving improved adherence to diabetes self care. However, African-American and Hispanic American participants were less adherent than white participants at all time points despite an individualized and accessible intervention. The finding that adherence did mediate glycemic control suggests that unique interventions for minority groups may be needed to overcome this disparity.

Keywords: diabetes; adherence; minorities; glycemic control; telemedicine

# Methods
## Participants

Participants ( n = 1 6 6 5 )were recruited through primary care providers (PCPs) and included if they were receiving Medicare benefits, were \geq 5 5years of age, and diagnosed with diabetes. Individuals were excluded if they had moderate/severe physical, cognitive, or visual impairments, or severe comorbid disease. Participants were recruited from very different communities. SUNY Upstate Medical University, Syracuse, New York served as the recruitment and intervention center for rural upstate NY which enrolled mostly underserved non-Hispanic white participants, while recruitment and management for urban participants, who were mostly Hispanic and African-American, was centered at Columbia University in New York City (NYC). The sample consisted of 1665 subjects, 821 randomized to usual care, and 844 randomized to the telemedicine intervention. (See ‘Description of the Sample’ section) The two groups did not differ at baseline in terms of demographic or clinical characteristics (See Table 1 for baseline characteristics of participants). There were significant differences on many of these variables for the three ethnic/ racial groups (See Table 2 for baseline demographic characteristics by race/ethnic group). For example, whites were significantly older than Hispanics \left( { p = 0 . 0 0 6 } \right)and had significantly higher education levels than both Hispanics and blacks ( p < 0 . 0 0 0 1

for both). In terms of medical variables, whites had significantly better blood glucose control than blacks and Hispanics ( p < 0 . 0 0 1for both), lower diastolic blood pressure than blacks ( p = 0 . 0 0 3 ), and higher total cholesterol than Hispanics ( p = 0 . 0 1 7 ), while Hispanics had significantly lower Body mass indexes (BMIs) than whites and blacks ( p < 0 . 0 0 1for both).

## Theoretical basis of the intervention

The basic principles of Social Cognitive Theory, first detailed by Albert Bandura in the 1960s (Bandura and Walters 1963, Bandura 1997) underlie the behavior change techniques that form the basis of most health behavior change interventions, and of the IDEATel intervention. These include: (1) behavioral contracting, including reinforcements; (2) self-monitoring; (3) realistic, incremental goal-setting; (4) knowledge development; and (5) provision of social support for change. These strategies are generally accepted as the foundations of behavior change interventions (Shumaker et al. 1998). While studies have shown that patients can change behavior when they use these strategies, longer term maintenance of change is less successful (Delamater et al. 2001), therefore, the IDEATel intervention was developed as a longer term collaboration between patient and educator.

## Design and intervention

A detailed description of IDEATel study design has been reported (Shea et al. 2002, Starren et al. 2002). Subjects were randomized within primary care practices to telemedicine case management or usual care. Trained research nurses blinded to group assignment conducted physical and psychosocial assessments at baseline and annual follow-up visits. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards at Columbia University, SUNY Upstate Medical University, and other participating organizations.

The intervention involved regular (every 4-6 weeks throughout the 5 years of the project) tele-visits with nurse case managers and dietitians via a home telemedicine unit (HTU). The HTU consisted of a web-enabled computer with a camera for the video visits, as well as the capability to upload blood glucose and blood pressure measurements, and access diabetes education and patient-specific data to assist in education and management. Hemoglobin A1c (A1c), blood pressure and cholesterol goals were established (consistent with established guidelines), and the educators worked with participants toward achieving their goals. The intervention was supervised by study endocrinologists, who made recommendations to PCPs for changes in medical management when appropriate, however, full control over patients’ care rested with the PCPs.

At each tele-visit the educator reviewed the blood glucose and blood pressure readings of the participant to assess their progress in achieving control and look for any patterns of difficulty. The educator and participant set a behavioral goal to work toward improved adherence to good diabetes self care. They discussed barriers to goal attainment and developed action plans to help to achieve the goals. They reviewed the goal and behavior changes at subsequent tele-visits, and a new goal was set. For example, after being educated about the importance of eating a low fat diet in order to lose weight, one participant might set her goal to eat smaller portions of ice cream, while another might decide to eliminate ice cream completely from her diet. They would discuss what might interfere with this plan, and ways to address this barrier. If they need support from their partner, the first participant might choose to talk to him to ask him to remind her to take a smaller portion, while the second might ask her partner to give up ice cream too so that it is not in the house. The educator and participant develop the plan and the participant makes a verbal commitment to try it. At the next tele-visit, the educator will ask her how it went. If she was able to achieve her goal, the educator will praise her, and together they will develop a new behavioral goal. If it did not go well, they will discuss why not and how to change the plan to make it more likely that she would succeed.

The interventionists in NYC were African-American or Hispanic American individuals who were bi-lingual and able to converse with Spanish-speaking participants in their own language. As members of these minority groups themselves, they were encouraged to tailor their recommendations and goal-setting to the unique cultures. For example, they encouraged different food choices, or activity regimens, that were likely to be appealing to the individual based on cultural norms. Educators in upstate New York were non-Hispanic whites who similarly tailored the intervention to the needs of the participants. For example, since most lived in rural communities, they addressed activity regimens and goals with the knowledge of what options were available in their environment.

## Description of the sample

More than 9500 patients were assessed for eligibility, 7396 were excluded (e.g., not in CMS system, too sick, refused). Complete telephone screens were done for 2201 patients, 534 were excluded at screen (e.g., vision, cognitive impairment, refused). N1665 subjects were enrolled, completed baseline assessment and randomized. Randomization began in December 2000 and was completed in October 2002. At 1- year, the intervention group had 160 subjects who dropped out of the intervention, 57 of whom returned for follow-up assessment, and 41 subjects who continued the intervention but did not complete the follow-up assessment. Thus, the intervention group had 144 subjects lost-to-follow-up. The control group had 31 subjects who dropped out, 1 who returned for 1-year assessment, and 74 who did not complete the 1-year assessment. Thus, the control group had 104 subjects lost-to-follow-up. This is an overall drop-out rate of 14.9% (i.e., 248 of the 1665 randomized subjects). (See Shea et al (2006) for CONSORT diagram). Comparing baseline values for subjects who were lost-to-follow-up to subjects who remained in the study showed that the two groups did not differ in terms of age, gender, race/ethnicity, or on medical or psychosocial measures.

A comparison of baseline demographic and clinical variables showed no significant differences between the intervention and control groups, except that intervention subjects reported greater diabetes symptom severity (p0.015; Table 1).

## Statistical techniques

Using an intention-to-treat design, all IDEATel participants randomized (n1665) were included in the analyses. (Among the 1665, 1443 contributed at least two waves of data to these analyses, and 222 were lost-to-follow-up.) A simple model (only time and group terms) and a model with covariates were examined. Covariates were selected based on theoretical reasons; a relationship of these variables to adherence was posited. Covariates included age, gender, race/ethnicity (split into dummy variables with White as reference group), years of education, duration of diabetes, comorbidity, and diabetes symptom severity. Comorbidity and diabetes symptom severity were treated as time-varying covariates.

SAS Proc Mixed with an adjustment for clustering within PCP was used for all analyses. A compound symmetry covariance structure was used. Up to six waves of data were included (baseline plus 5 years of follow-up). Log and quadratic terms were included to model the non-linear distribution of overall adherence over time. To reduce colinearity, time was centered and log time was computed using log (time 0.01). The Expectation Maximization (EM) algorithm was used to fill in missing data in covariates. Colinearity diagnostics were examined in order to test for too highly correlated covariates. Only minor violations were observed (variance inflation factors were below 2.0 for all covariates, excluding group and time terms). Respondents with baseline and up to five waves of data collected annually were included in the analyses if they had at least one measure of the outcome over time (n1665).

For examination of mediating effects of overall adherence on A1c, SAS PROC Mixed was used with non-linear terms and adjustment for clustering within PCP. Up to six waves of data (baseline plus five follow-ups) were included in the analyses. Non-linear models with exponential (e-time) and quadratic terms to model nonlinearity with a first order auto-regressive covariance structure were used for the A1c analyses. Overall adherence was treated as a time-varying covariate. Mediating effects of overall adherence on glycemic control were examined by performing tests of the mediating path coefficients (MacKinnon et al. 2002).
