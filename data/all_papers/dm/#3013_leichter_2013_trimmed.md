# Research Design and Methods
In this 12-month, randomized, controlled, non-inferiority study we assessed the clinical efficacy of computer/ telephone-based patient consultations compared with traditional clinic-based patient visits in the management of type 1 and type 2 diabetes. The goal was to determine whether use of ‘‘telemedicine’’ interventions could provide the same level of care but with greater staff time efficiency.

## Study population

The study recruited 100 subjects with known diabetes who were currently treated at the Center for Diabetes and Metabolism, Columbus, GA. Inclusion criteria were as follows: type 1 or type 2 diabetes, 19–65 years of age, computer literate, and competent in use of SMBG. Exclusion criteria were as follows: creatinine clearance < 30 mL/min, severe diabetic retinopathy, known or suspected gastroparesis, cardiovascular disease, hepatic disease, severe neuropathy, recreational drug or substantial alcohol use, use of medications that affect blood sugar and blood pressure, pregnancy or capable of becoming pregnant, and use of an insulin pump other than the Roche Diagnostics (Indianapolis, IN) Spirit insulin pump. The protocol was approved by the Columbus Regional Institutional Review Board and is in compliance with the Helsinki Declaration.13 Written informed consent was obtained from each subject.

## Procedures

At the baseline visit, we collected relevant medical history, performed physical examinations, collected laboratory samples (e.g., HbA1c, lipids), and documented current medications. Subjects were identified at regularly scheduled clinic visits and assessed for eligibility based on inclusion/exclusion criteria. Eligible subjects signed informed consent forms. Subjects were then scheduled to attend a baseline visit for clinical assessment. Following the baseline assessment, the investigator stratified subjects into subgroups according to their current diabetes treatment regimen: treated with insulin alone, oral agents alone, insulin pump alone, or combination of insulin and oral agents. The investigator then randomized subjects within each treatment subgroup (1:1 ratio) on an alternating basis to the control (CG) or study (SG) group.

Subjects in both arms received a free blood glucose meter and test strips (ACCU-CHEK- Aviva meter system; Roche Diagnostics) and were instructed in their use. SG subjects received data management software (ACCU-CHEK 360 software) to be used with their blood glucose meter along with training in using the software. They were also given a body weight scale and a digital blood pressure cuff.

Following the baseline visit, CG subjects were required to attend quarterly clinic visits during the 12-month study period. Measurements of body mass index (BMI), blood pressure, HbA1c, and lipids were taken at each visit. At these visits, subjects’ SMBG data were reviewed, and therapy adjustments were made based, in part, on the glucose data. SG subjects attended office visits at 6 and 12 months; 3- and 9-month visits were conducted via the Internet and telephone. Laboratory values for lipids were obtained prior to the Internet interactions. Body weight and blood pressure were measured and reported via the Internet, along with SMBG data, using the software program. Patients were seen in the

clinic and managed via remote computer and telephone by an endocrinologist. The treating endocrinologist assessed the transmitted data and provided advice to SG subjects through e-mail and telephone confirmation. The endocrinologist required no time for data review or preparation prior to the telemedicine visits because patient information could be viewed in coordination with the electronic medical chart.

As with CG patients, SG patients were encouraged to transmit their SMBG data as often as they felt appropriate but were required to transmit their data at least quarterly prior to the telemedicine visit. All patients were able to call in between scheduled visits with any questions or concerns.

## Efficacy assessments

The study was designed to determine whether the clinical end points of care for the SG subjects were similar to those of the CG subjects. The clinical end points selected for observation and analysis included HbA1c, BMI, systolic blood pressure, and lipid fractions. Between-group comparisons of these data were based on accepted clinical criteria.11 Secondary end points included comparison of the dropout rate in each group and average time spent by clinicians for clinic visits and Internet consultations.

Sample size was estimated based on the end point of HbA1c level. With 50 patients in each group, the study had 80% power to detect a mean (SD) difference of 0.5% (0.9) between groups. For each of the end points of care a linear mixed model for repeated measures (PROC MIXED) was used to analyze the difference between SG and CG. Control variables included diabetes treatment (insulin or non-insulin), baseline values of clinical end points measured (e.g., blood pressure, BMI), and visit and group-by-visit interaction as fixed effects and subject as random effect. Least-squares mean values and the SD between the two groups were reported at each postbaseline visit. Data were analyzed by SAS version 9.3 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

# Results
The study was conducted from October 27, 2009 (first patient recruited) to June 2, 2011 (last patient evaluated). Of the 100 enrolled subjects, 98 were randomized, and 70 completed three of the four clinic visits/telemedicine contacts, including the final clinic visit (CG, n = 37; SG, n = 33). Among these, 26 CG subjects completed all four follow-up clinic visits, and 24 SG subjects completed their two follow-up clinic visits and two telemedicine interactions. Our analysis includes data from the 70 patients who met the completion requirements.

Baseline characteristics of each study group were similar with respect to race, ethnicity, type of diabetes, HbA1c, lipid profiles, weight, and BMI; however, SG subjects were significantly younger than CG subjects (Table 1). SG subjects also had a higher diastolic blood pressure than the CG subjects, but neither group exhibited a mean blood pressure above established guidelines.11 The SG had a significantly higher percentage of men than the CG (P = 0.02). The groups were also similar in the number of subjects on insulin, insulin pumps, oral hypoglycemic agents, or combinations of insulin and oral agents (Table 1). Patient dropout rates were similar between the two groups (P = 0.371) with no bias in dropout rate based on the type of diabetes treatment; however, noncompleters were more likely to be younger than completers. The study results

are reported based on the completers (i.e., subjects who completed both baseline and month 12 visits). The analysis results, based on the full analysis set, are similar to those for the completers and do not change any of the conclusions.

At 12 months, no significant between-group differences were observed in HbA1c, blood pressure, lipids, or BMI; however, SG subjects experienced significantly more weight loss compared with CG subjects (P = 0.04) (Table 2). Although BMI in SG subjects at 12 months reflected this change, the between-group difference in BMI did not achieve statistical significance (P = 0.06), nor did the decrease in body weight correlate with any change in HbA1c level. There were no adverse episodes for any subjects during the study, including hospitalizations for hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia.

Clinicians reported spending an average of 27 min per subject for each clinic visit (coded as Level 3 follow-up visits) and approximately 5 min per telemedicine consultation. For CG subjects (n = 26) who attended all four clinic visits, the total clinician time requirement was 108 min per CG subject, compared with SG subjects (n = 24) who required a total of approximately 64 min per SG subject for two clinic visits and two telemedicine consultations, a total time savings of greater than 40%.
