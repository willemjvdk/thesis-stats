# Material and Methods
## Study Design and Study Site

This randomized controlled study was conducted between December 2019 and May 2020 in an outpatient department of medicine at HAHC Hospital, Hamdard Institute of Medical Sciences & Research (HIMSR), Jamia Hamdard, New Delhi, India. The study was approved by the Jamia Hamdard Institutional Ethics Committee, New Delhi, India (Registration no. ECR/48/Inst/DL/2013/RR-19) and carried out in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki [11] and written following the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) [12] (Supplementary fle). Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants.

## Study Participants

Participants were recruited based on predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The study included individuals who met the following criteria: a) a confrmed diagnosis of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) with an HbA1c level of

7% (53 mmol/mol) or higher, b) aged over 18 years, regardless of gender, and c) ownership of a mobile phone and suffcient literacy to comprehend the messages.

The study excluded individuals who met any of the following criteria: a) unwillingness to provide signed informed consent, b) inability to read or understand the messages, and c) prior participation in any other study involving lifestyle modifcation or mHealth. Demographic characteristics such as age, gender, education level, lifestyle factors (smoking, tobacco use, and drinking), and family history were recorded in the case report form.

To evaluate the socioeconomic status of patients, a modifed version of Kuppuswamy's socioeconomic scale was employed. This scale categorized socioeconomic status into fve subgroups (upper, upper-middle, lower-middle, upperlower, and lower) based on factors such as education, occupation of the head of the family, and monthly income. For the purpose of this study, patients belonging to the upper-middle class were classifed as the upper class, while those falling into the lower-middle or upper-lower class were categorized as the middle class. Thus, socioeconomic status was classifed into three subgroups: upper, middle, and lower [13].

## Randomization and Allocation

In this study, eligible participants were randomized into intervention and control groups in a 1:1 ratio. Randomization was performed using an online portal called "RAN-DOMIZER," and each participant was assigned a unique number. The allocation information was concealed from the recruiting investigator until after the participant was enrolled. This was achieved by sealing individual slips with participant numbers and their group assignments in envelopes, which were opened only after recruitment. A staf member (Physician) was involved in the study who randomized participants and assigned them to their group after baseline measures had been completed. Participants were then enrolled in the study by the trained researchers.

## Intervention

In the intervention group, participants received tailored text messages and telephonic calls as part of the intervention. These messages and calls were designed based on various standard guidelines provided by the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) [14]. The script of the text messages and telephone calls is present in the supplementary fle. The content of the text messages and calls aimed to enhance medication adherence, promote physical activity, encourage healthy eating habits, support smoking cessation, limit alcohol intake, and provide counseling on overall health. During the registration process, participants in the intervention group had the fexibility to choose the timing

of their text messages and telephonic calls, as well as the language of the messages (English or Hindi). Participants had the option to discontinue receiving messages and calls at any time by simply texting the word 'STOP' or by contacting the research investigator via phone call. These text messages and telephonic calls were provided by a trained pharmacist (Research scholar) who ensured individualized delivery of intervention.

## Procedures

Throughout the study, all participants maintained their regular diabetes care, including medical visits, tests, and participation in diabetes support programs. In the intervention group, patients received 3 to 5 text messages per week and had a weekly telephonic consultation. The mode of the intervention was through mobile phones at their residence (according to participant mobility) for a duration of 3 months. On the other hand, the control group received usual care without any additional intervention. After a period of 3  months, all participants underwent a follow-up interview. The primary outcome was to measure HbA1c levels (Blood test was performed as a routine checkup under the guidance of physician) at baseline and three months. Secondary outcomes were medication adherence, healthy eating habits, physical activity, and HRQoL were assessed through self-reported measures at baseline and three months.

## Health‑related Quality of Life

The European Quality of Life-5 Dimensions questionnaire (EQ-5D) consists of two parts, the first part is a health status categorised in five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/ depression. Each perspective has five alternatives i.e., “no problems”, slight problems”, “moderate problems”, “severe problems”, and “extreme problems” or unable to (mobility, self-care and usual activities), which scores from 1 (no problem) to 5 (extreme problems).

Responses to the frst part of the EQ-5D can be noted separately for each individual item in terms of a profle (EQ-5D profle) or converted into a weighted index (EQ-5D index) using EQ-5D-5L Crosswalk Index Value Calculator’ downloaded from the EuroQol website [15].

The score of EQ-5D index ranges from -0.111 to 1, where 1 represents preferred health. The second part of the EQ-5D is visual analogue scale (VAS), determines the overall health status. It consists of a 20 cm scale to measure the participant’s perception of his or her quality of life or good or bad health on the day of the interview. This scale ranged from 0 to 100, 0 represents worst health you can imagine while 100 represents best health.

## Sample Size Calculation

Based on the previous study's fndings, which indicated a mean HbA1C level of 9.9% and a standard deviation (SD) of 1.69 in subjects akin to our study's target group, we pursued an appropriate sample size calculation. To efectively detect a treatment efect of a 1% absolute decrease in HbA1C across treatment groups, while maintaining an 80% statistical power and a signifcance level of 2-tailed α = 0.05, an initial sample size estimation of 90 patients (45 per group) was established. To accommodate a projected 10% loss to follow-up, we enrolled a total of 100 subjects, evenly distributed as 50 participants in each group.

## Statistical Analysis

The data was analysed using descriptive statistics: All Continuous variables were expressed as mean standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables are expressed as percentages.

Baseline characteristics were compared in two groups using a chi-square test for discontinuous variables. Analyses of primary and secondary outcomes were performed with the use of paired t-test within the groups. A two-tailed p-value with equal variances assumed < 0.05 was considered statistically signifcant.

All the statistical analysis was performed by using R Programming (R Studio) version 4.2.1.

# Results
A total of 159 individuals were assessed for eligibility and enrolled in the study between December 2019 and January 2020. Among them, 100 participants were randomly assigned to either the intervention or control groups, with 50 participants in each group (see Fig. 1). The fnal three-month follow-up assessments were completed in April 2020, and the overall loss to follow-up, where no data on any outcome was available, was low in both groups (3/100 = 3%). Three participants (1 from the intervention group and 2 from the control group) were excluded from the analysis of the primary and secondary outcomes due to a lack of follow-up for HbA1c results.

## Participants' Engagement and Feedback on the Intervention

A total number of 1968 messages were delivered manually by mobile phone to participants (median number of messages per participant 39) in the intervention group. Additionally, 600 telephonic calls were provided to the intervention group within 3 months of study duration. Communication

between the intervention group participants and the investigator amassed around 3264 min, with an average of approximately 65 min per participant.

During the telephonic calls, participants were inquired about their adherence to the prescribed text messages and any challenges they encountered. They were also provided with guidance on how to efectively implement the recommended steps. These telephonic interactions were conducted by a qualifed pharmacist who was also a Research Scholar.

Among the participants in the intervention group, a total of 49 individuals (100%) completed the follow-up questionnaire regarding their feedback on the intervention (refer to supplementary fle). The participants expressed high levels of satisfaction with the intervention. Among the fve questions asked, only 1 participant (5%) reported having briefy considered withdrawing from the study, while 2 participants (10%) mentioned fnding the intervention slightly challenging to follow. Importantly, all participants (100%) reported that the intervention did not disrupt their daily lives, provided valuable information, and improved their knowledge and/or lifestyle related to diabetes.
