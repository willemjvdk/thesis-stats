## a b s t r a c t

Background and purpose: To investigate the effects of adding virtual activity groups to a multicomponent ambulatory activity monitoring intervention in managing chronic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes.

Methods: We randomized 120 subjects with type 2 diabetes to receive an activity monitor with or without placement into virtual activity groups. We monitored subjects over six months and collected HbA1c, weight, step count, blood pressure, and SF-36 questionnaire data.

Results: All subjects lost significant weight over the course of the study (p = 0.005); however, there was no statistically significant difference in mean weight loss between two groups (p = 0.520). HbA1c decreased in both groups, but was not statistically significant (p = 0.084). Daily step counts were similar between groups (p = 0.633), but both groups did decrease significantly over time (p = 0.004). There was no linear correlation between daily step count and HbA1c (p = 0.609), but there was between daily step count and weight (p = 0.016) although this only accounted for 5.6% of weight loss. There were no significant differences found for blood pressure and Rand SF36 measures between groups or times, or group-time interactions with two exceptions. The SF36 Role functioning/emotional measure showed an interaction between group and time (p = 0.039) and the SF36 Health Change measure had a significant difference due to time irrespective of group (p < 0.0001). Compliance remained high, with 93.8% of subjects actively participating at the conclusion of the study.

Conclusions: Activity monitoring with in-person goal-setting and scripted feedback over six months was associated with significant weight loss with or without virtual support groups.

# Material and methods
## Participants

Our investigation was approved by Wilford Hall Ambulatory Surgical Center Institutional Review Board. We obtained informed consent from all individual subjects included in the study. All recruitment, assessment, randomization, and communication with subjects was administered by the research coordinators who were unblinded to the specific task.

We recruited 120 subjects with a diagnosis of type 2 diabetes at a single Department of Defense military medical facility. Subjects were at least 18 years old and able to link their activity monitor – Fitbit® – to a personal computer or cell phone. Subjects were excluded if they could not achieve at least 4 metabolic equivalents of exercise, walk without assistive devices, or had a medical condition which would make walking unsafe. We did not recruit special populations such as children, pregnant women, or prisoners. We unenrolled subjects from the study if they had any significant changes to their ambulatory or medical status, failed to adhere to research parameters, or chose to end participation for any reason.

# Materials
The device used to measure ambulatory activity was a black Fitbit Charge® worn around either wrist during ambulation. The device itself displayed daily steps and would buzz when subjects reached their inputted step goals. The device also functioned as a wristwatch. The Fitbit® app provided subjects an interface to track their own step counts, see the step-counts of linked accounts, and it provided continuous, automatic uploading of data. The WhatsApp® application provided an end-to-end encrypted messaging service for communication within the study. Subjects used their own smartphones to run the applications. Biometric information was gathered using certified clinical scales and blood pressure devices in the Mike O’Callaghan Military Medical Center Family Medicine Residency Clinic. HbA1c values were obtained at the Mike O’Callaghan Military Medical Center laboratory using the certified Roche Hitachi Cobas c 501 analyzer. The Rand Health Care 36 item short form survey (SF-36) was given to all participants pre-post and included questions about activity levels, physical functioning, physical role functioning, emotional functioning, fatigue, emotional well-being, social functioning, pain, and general health.

# Methods: initial evaluation
All subjects participated in an in-person session with a research coordinator to obtain biometric data and receive instruction on activity monitor use. We obtained baseline weight, height, blood pressure, and HbA1c levels. A medical provider associated with

the study reviewed baseline medical history to ensure the subject met inclusion and exclusion criteria. All subjects downloaded the Fitbit® and WhatsApp® applications to their smartphones. We taught subjects how to use their activity monitors and link it with their smartphones to track steps. We gave each subject a de-identified name (i.e. Fitbit001, Fitbit002, etc.) to protect identities and limit their outside associates from identifying them. All subjects filled out the SF36 to assess overall baseline health status. These measures are: vitality, physical functioning, bodily pain, health perceptions, physical role functioning, emotional role functioning, social role functioning, and mental health.

Each participant stated step count, weight, and HbA1c goals. If the participant did not have a specific step-count goal in mind the coordinator suggested 10,000 steps per day. The research coordinators recorded their stated goals but did not attempt any motivational interviewing or specific counseling or guidance.

# Methods: intervention
We randomized subjects into one of two groups using block randomization, Fig. 1. Blocks of 20 subjects were divided equally as recruitment progressed using a random number generator into two groups, heretofore known as “SoloFitbit” and “Fitbit + Friends” groups. The SoloFitbit group served as the control group, while the Fitbit + Friends group served as the intervention group.

Subjects in the Fitbit + Friends group were assigned to a virtual fitness group with 9 other individuals from their randomized block. Every subject in the Fitbit + Friends group was a virtual “friend” with the rest of the group and was able to see a “leaderboard” which tracked individual step counts and ranked them in order. Within their smartphone applications, Fitbit + Friends subjects were able to see in real time their daily step counts in comparison with the rest of their group on a ranking board. Subjects were able to use the WhatsApp® messenger to communicate with each other on a messaging forum and the research coordinator. We did not allow subjects to share personally identifying data or use crude, vulgar, or offensive language. The research coordinator monitored each group to ensure privacy and professionalism.

We set up SoloFitbit subjects with the same secure Fitbit® and WhatsApp® platforms but did not assign them to any virtual support groups. Their step counts were visible only to themselves and the research coordinator who gave identical scripted feedback to both groups. They did not interact with any other members of the study. We allowed the subjects to use the normal functions of their

Fitbit® applications but did not allow them to make other virtual friends on the Fitbit® app.

We gave all subjects identical monthly scripted individual feedback on WhatsApp® based on his or her step goals. For instance, a subject would get a congratulatory message if they achieved their personal step-count goal. Subjects also received standard automated messages of encouragement from the Fitbit app® itself. For example, their Fitbit Charge® would buzz when they reached their daily step-count goals.

# Methods: follow-up evaluations
Subjects had in-person follow up sessions at 3 and 6 months with a research coordinator. Height, weight, HbA1c, and SF36 item questionnaire data were gathered during these sessions. Average daily step counts were collected monthly from month 1 to month 6. Research coordinators answered any questions but did not provide any motivational counseling or comment on the subjects’ attainment of goals or relative step-counts compared to their peers. Our research coordinator monitored their profile names and had complete access to their profiles. We incentivized compliance and rewarded participation by permitting subjects to keep their devices (Fitbit Charge® valued at $100) if they successfully completed the study as instructed. After study completion, we deleted subjects’ profiles and the virtual groups.

Subjects continued receiving routine management of their chronic diseases through their primary care provider. We asked

all subjects to update the research team with any changes in their medical management. Research coordinators had access to the subjects’ ambulatory care notes and could review their medications and clinic visits to monitor for any changes.

# Methods: statistical analysis
The study is organized as a mixed effects, randomized complete block design with repeated measures. Means, standard deviations and ranges for interval variables and frequency distributions for nominal variables were calculated for the entire sample and for treatment groups. A priori power was assessed using for an effect size the minimal clinically important difference of 0.4% percent in HbA1c. Using this as an effect size, a priori power for rANOVA indicated a total number of 100 subjects with 3 repeated measures would have a power of 0.80 at - = 0.05 to detect a 0.4% difference in HbA1c in a sample having a mean HbA1c of 7.3% 0.9% 1SD. Statistical analysis was performed with R Version 3.5.1 [25].

We tested the null hypotheses of there being no differences in means for physiological, HbA1c and SF36 outcome measures by a mixed effects repeated measures analysis of variance (rANOVA) using measurements taken at baseline (month 0), 3 and 6 months. The null hypothesis of there being no difference in means for 30-day average daily step count was also tested by rANOVA using measurements taken monthly from month 1 to month 6. We used contrasts to investigate effects and differences in the event of null hypothe-

ses rejection. The significance level of multiple comparison tests was corrected to \alpha = 0 . 0 5by the Holm method [26].

# Results
Table 1 shows the statistical analysis of subject characteristics and baseline outcome measures for both groups. There were no statistical differences between groups for any of the variables (p ≥ 0.632) inferring subjects were appropriately randomized between groups. However, 12 subjects did not complete the study, leaving 108 subjects for analysis, Fig. 1. Four subjects voluntarily withdrew for personal reasons, one developed a medical condition that precluded participation, and seven patients stopped using their devices and were lost to follow-up. We statistically compared 15 variables (subject characteristics and outcome variables at baseline) of those who were lost to the study and those who completed the study. These groups did not statistically differ for all 15 variables ( \mathbf { p } > 0 . 2 0 9 )allowing us to conclude both groups had been drawn from the same population and were appropriately randomized. We further assessed whether the proportions of those in the intervention and nonintervention groups differed as to whether they were lost to the study or completed the study. They did not (p = 0.671) allowing us to conclude that the 12 subjects lost from the study did not impact our randomization, and did not increase our risk of bias as they were prognostically identical to the patients who remained.

All subjects lost a significant amount of weight (p = 0.005; 95% CI for estimated difference of 3 = 9 to 15). The mean (95%CI) weight at baseline, 210 (202–218) pounds decreased to 207 (199–215) pounds by 6 months. However, there was no statistically significant difference in mean weight loss over time between the two groups (p = 0.520; 95% CI for estimated difference of 9 = -.4 to 19). The Fitbit + Friends group reduced mean weight from 214 (202–226) to 212 (200–224) pounds, and the SoloFitbit group reduced mean weight from 206 (195–216) to 202 (191–212) pounds, Table 2, Fig. 2.

Mean (95%CI) HbA1c values decreased over the intervention period in both groups, 7.2% (7.0–7.5) to 7.1% (6.9–7.3), but was not statistically significant over time (p = 0.084; 95% CI for estimated difference of 0.1 = -0.2 to 0.4).
