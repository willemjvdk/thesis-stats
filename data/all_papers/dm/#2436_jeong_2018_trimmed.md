# Research Design and Methods
## STUDY PARTICIPANTS

Volunteers with previously diagnosed type 2 diabetes and HbA1c ranging from 7% to 11% were recruited from the outpatient clinic of four university hospitals in South Korea from March 2011 to April 2013. Among those who required insulin treatment, only patients on basal insulin or premixed insulin twice daily or less than a day were eligible. Patients able to use a personal computer for this study and to access the Internet at home were included. The study excluded patients with acute illness, liver dysfunction, renal dysfunction, or

chronic lung disease, or any other medical conditions that could affect glycemic level.

Of the 417 patients screened, 79 were excluded. The remaining 338 patients, constituting the intention-to-treat population, were randomized to three groups: a conventional care group (n = 113), a telemonitoring group (n = 113), and a telemedicine group (n = 112).

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of four different hospitals: Kyungpook National University Hospital, Yeungnam University Medical Center, Yonsei Severance Hospital, and Gangnam Severance Hospital. All subjects provided written informed consent.

## STUDY DESIGN AND INTERVENTION

This 24-week, randomized, multi-center, parallel, interventional, open-label study involved patients at four university hospitals in South Korea. All subjects were provided with diabetes self-care education, including information on self-monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG), dietary recommendation, and physical activity, as recommended by the American Diabetes Association.13 Subjects receiving oral medication were asked to perform at least three SMBG tests per week, whereas those taking insulin were asked to perform at least six SMBG tests per week.

Device. Participants randomized to the intervention groups received an SCU invented specifically for this study (LG Eelectronics, Inc., Seoul, South Korea). The SCU consisted of a Web-enabled computer with a camera and specific software, a glucometer (One Touch; LifeScan, Inc., Milpitas, CA), and a body composition analyzer (InBody IH-U070B). Overall, this unit provided several functions, including video conferencing, allowing patients to communicate with endocrinologists in the hospital; remote monitoring of blood glucose and body weight transmitted automatically; records of diet and exercise; automated short message feedback based on glucose monitoring; and access to education provided by the Smart Care Center in Daegu, Korea. The physicians and diabetes specialist nurses at this Smart Care Center were responsible for responses to patients’ telephone calls and management of remote glucose monitoring and feedback. All communications between patients and nurses at the Smart Care Center were recorded and transmitted to diabetes specialists in the respective hospitals.

Conventional care group. After being educated on standard diabetes self-care, as recommended by the American Diabetes Association,13 patients were advised to report for all scheduled outpatient appointments with their doctors (endocrinologists),

at baseline and after 8, 16, and 24 weeks. All patients were provided a glucometer (Care Sens- N, i-SENS, Republic of Korea) and encouraged to self-monitor their blood glucose level detected with the glucometer. Each patient was examined by an endocrinologist in the hospital, who prescribed the medication, and reported any adverse events.

Telemonitoring group. Patients in this group were advised to report for scheduled outpatient appointment with the endocrinologists after 8, 16, and 24 weeks. All patients were instructed to perform SMBG and measure body composition and to transmit these data to the Smart Care Center by using the provided SCU. In response, each patient received a short message generated by the automated clinical decision support system algorithm. Medications were prescribed by endocrinologists based on these data. These patients also received general information about diabetes self-management once a week from the Smart Care Center.

Telemedicine group. Similar to patients in the telemonitoring group, patients in this group were provided remote monitoring equipment. Instead of visiting the hospital at weeks 8 and 16, patients in this group contacted physicians from their homes via the SCU equipment with a camera. Based on these communications and data transmitted on SMBG and body weight, as in the telemonitoring group, physicians assessed patients, with prescribed medicines delivered to each individual’s home. The third consultation, at 24 weeks, was a faceto-face outpatient visit in the hospital.

# OUTCOMES
The primary outcome was change in HbA1c concentration from baseline to 24 weeks. Secondary outcomes included changes in fasting blood glucose (FBG) concentration, lipid profiles, body weight, and body mass index (BMI); the percentage of subjects who achieved <7% HbA1c <7%; compliance with SMBG and medications; and frequency of hypoglycemia. Safety outcomes include rates of hospitalization and number of emergency room visits due to complications of diabetes; adverse events; and laboratory test results, including serum concentrations of aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and creatinine. Effects of intervention were analyzed by comparing changes within each group and differences among the three groups.

## STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

All results are expressed as means – standard deviation. Baseline characteristics in the three groups were compared by

analysis of variance or the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. Differences in adjusted HbA1c changes and other outcomes were assessed by using analysis of covariance. All statistical analyses were performed by using SAS 9.1 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC), with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

# Results
## BASELINE CHARACTERISTICS OF PARTICIPANTS

The baseline characteristics of the 338 patients in the three groups are shown in Table 1. The mean age of participants was *53 years, and most were male. Most participants had been treated with oral hypoglycemic agents or insulin. Only 13 patients (3.85%) were without any medications and had been continuing life style modifications. Clinical and sociodemographic characteristics, including age, body weight, and glucose control methods, were similar in the three groups, as were the proportions of patients who dropped out prematurely. These parameters were statistically not different among three groups, as indicated in Table 1 \textstyle { \big ( } p > 0 . 0 5 { \big ) }. Of the 338 patients, 307 completed (Fig. 1); the main reason for dropout was withdrawal of consent.

## GLUCOSE-LOWERING EFFECTS OF INTERVENTION

All three groups showed a significant reduction in HbA1c levels from baseline to the end of the study (-0.66% – 1.03% in the control group, -0.66% – 1.09% in the telemonitoring group, and -0.81% – 1.05% in the telemedicine group, p < 0.001 each). However, when the degree of reduction during the 24-week follow-up was compared in the three groups, no significant differences were observed, although there was a trend toward greater decline in the telemedicine group (Table 2).

SMBG compliance was defined as the proportion of number of records in SMBG device (control group) or transmitted number (telemonitoring and telemedicine groups) per recommended number of records. To assess the efficacy of intervention in patients who showed high compliance with SMBG, sub-group analysis was performed in patients with SMBG compliance >90%. Based on this criterion, 42, 44, and 46 patients in the conventional, telemonitoring, and telemedicine groups, respectively, showed good SMBG compliance. Changes in HbA1c concentrations in these three subgroups were -0.81% – 1.07%, -0.93% – 0.99%, and -1.08% – 0.96%, respectively, with reductions being significantly greater for patients in the telemonitoring and telemedicine groups than those in the control group ( p < 0.05 each) with high SMBG compliance (Table 2).

Sub-group analysis showed that the reduction in HbA1c concentration was greater in men than in women during the 24-week study period. However, there were no significant differences among the three groups in either men or women (Table 2). When the participants were categorized by their educational status, the effect of intervention was minimal in college graduates, but the reduction in HbA1c differed significantly between subjects in the telemedicine and control groups

who had graduated high school or less (-0.94% – 1.11% vs. -0.48% – 0.95%, p = 0.0281). Division of subjects into those aged <55 and ‡55 years showed no significant differences among groups.

# EFFECT OF INTERVENTION ON SECONDARY OUTCOMES
Table 3 summarizes the effects of intervention on secondary outcomes. The change in mean FBG concentration from

baseline was significantly greater in the telemonitoring (-7.62 – 42.92 mg/dL) and telemedicine (-12.28 – 41.20 mg/ dL) groups than in the control group (-4.97 – 41.14 mg/dL) (Table 3). Two-hour postprandial glucose level was significantly lower in the telemedicine than in the control group. At the end of the 24-week period, 33.9% (37/113) and 38.9% (42/ 112) of patients in the telemonitoring and telemedicine groups, respectively, had achieved target HbA1c <7.0%, compared with 28.7% (31/113) in the control group, but none of the between-group differences was statistically significant.

The net change in cholesterol concentrations over 24 weeks was similar in the three groups. However, within-group analyses showed that triglyceride concentrations were significantly reduced in the telemonitoring and telemedicine groups and high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol levels were significantly increased in all three groups. Body weight and BMI were significantly decreased in both the telemonitoring and telemedicine groups, but not in the control group, but the difference among the three groups was not meaningful. During the 24-week study period, subjects in the telemedicine group reported 18 hypoglycemic events, significantly lower than the 34 and 37 events reported in the control and telemonitoring groups, respectively ( p < 0.05 each). SMBG compliance during the study tended to be higher in the telemonitoring

(87.66% – 63.14%) and telemedicine (89.90% – 60.74%) than in the control (81.87% – 56.29%) group, but the differences were not statistically significant. Medication compliance was above 90% in all three groups, but was higher in the telemonitoring (95.31% – 9.79%) and telemedicine (96.68% – 9.65%) groups than in the control group (92.59% – 13.06%) ( p < 0.05 for each pairwise comparison). There were no meaningful changes in blood pressure in any of the three groups.

## SAFETY AND ADVERSE EFFECTS OF INTERVENTION

Only one patient in the telemonitoring group was hospitalized, due to a complication of diabetes, and none of the patients in any group visited the emergency room for a diabetes-related complication. Adverse events were reported in 33 (29.2%), 30 (26.55%), and 23 (20.54%) patients in the control, telemonitoring, and telemedicine groups, respectively, and adverse events related to complications of diabetes in seven (6.19%), seven (6.19%), and three (2.68%) patients, respectively (Supplementary Table S1; Supplementary Data are available online at www.liebertpub.com/tmj). All of these events were well controlled without serious morbidity. Five serious adverse events were reported, including one patient each with angina pectoris and rotator cuff syndrome in the control group; one patient each with a malignant hepatic neoplasm and skin ulcer

in the telemonitoring group; and one patient with hematuria in the telemedicine group. No patient died of these adverse events during the trial. Serum ALT, AST, and creatinine levels were measured at baseline and at 24 weeks to detect any asymptomatic biochemical abnormality (Supplementary Table S2). Each biochemical parameter was categorized as normal or abnormal and their relative proportions at baseline and 24 weeks were compared. In the telemedicine group, all 86 subjects with initially normal serum ALT level had normal serum ALT level after 24 weeks, whereas nine subjects with initially abnormal serum ALT level had normal ALT 24 weeks later ( p = 0.027). Similarly, in this group, only 4 of 84 subjects with initially normal serum creatinine showed abnormal levels at follow-up, whereas 15 subjects with initial abnormal serum creatinine had normal concentrations after 24 weeks ( p = 0.0116). To detect subclinical adverse effects such as biochemical abnormalities, the latter were compared among the three study arms. In comparing the relative percentage of patients showing worsened biochemical parameters, from normal at baseline to abnormal at follow-up, only ALT

differed significantly in the telemonitoring (0, 0.0%) and telemedicine (7, 6.7%) groups ( p = 0.014); in addition, five patients (4.8%) in the control group went from normal to abnormal ALT.
