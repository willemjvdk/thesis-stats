# Materials and Methods
## Trial design and oversight

The RCT was supported by the Pennsylvania Department of Health. Institutional Review Board approval was obtained, participants provided written informed consent, and a Data and Safety Monitoring Board oversaw the study. Inclusion criteria were (1) Black race (self-identified), (2) age 35 years and older, (3) type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and (4) patient in the ED for a diabetes-related cause or had a hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) level ‡7.0%. Exclusion criteria were (1) dementia, (2) life expectancy <1 year, (3) Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition–defined psychiatric disorders other than anxiety or depression, and (4) institutional residence.

Two previous publications reported results on subjects from this study.16,17

## Recruitment and enrollment procedures

Participants were recruited from the EDs of Thomas Jefferson University Hospital and Einstein Medical Center, 2 urban academic medical centers in Philadelphia, PA. Research staff obtained preliminary consent in the ED or hospital and written informed consent in participants’ homes soon after discharge.

## Study treatments

Diabetes Interprofessional Team to Enhance Adherence to Medical Care. A previous publication describes the intervention and its cultural adaptations in detail.18 In brief, the intervention included race-concordant community health workers (CHWs), a clinical pharmacist, a diabetes nurse educator, and primary care physicians (PCPs). CHWs delivered six 90-minute in-home initial treatment sessions over 3 months followed by 3 booster sessions over the next 8 months (16.2% of all sessions were delivered remotely due to COVID). The CHWs delivered diabetes self-care education and behavioral activation (eg, goal-setting, action

plans) to increase participant adherence to recommended diets, physical activity, and medications to improve glycemic control.

The CHWs used laptops to facilitate 1 telehealth visit with each participant’s PCP (session 2) and 2 telehealth visits with the diabetes nurse educator (sessions 3 and 5) to increase access to care, discuss current treatment, and build trust. At baseline, a clinical pharmacist reviewed participants’ medications, identified potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs) (Beers criteria),19 and made suggestions (eg, reduce dose, discontinue, safer alternatives) to PCPs through Epic (electronic medical record) or fax.

Usual medical care. UMC was usual nonprotocol-driven primary care practice in which PCPs delivered care based on clinical experience and available research and guidelines. Comparing DM I-TEAM to UMC identifies the added value of DM I-TEAM over the current standard of care.

Treatment fidelity. A previous publication describes staff training, supervision, and procedures to assess treatment fidelity, which was high (data not shown).18

## Study measures

Baseline measures. A masked outcome assessor obtained the following data: age, sex, education, literacy (Literacy Assessment for Diabetes),20 marital status, cognition (Short-Montreal Cognitive Assessment),21 insurance type, number of medications, chronic medical conditions, and Area Deprivation Index scores (which provide census block indicators of relative neighborhood disadvantage).22

# Outcome measures (baseline and 12 months)
Return ED visits and/or hospitalizations (‘‘events’’) [primary outcome]. Epic and the HealthShare Exchange database were reviewed to detect incident all-cause events over 12 months after randomization. This information was supplemented by participant self-report of events, which required medical record confirmation to be counted.

Glycemic control. The glycemic outcome was the proportion of participants achieving a HbA1c level £7.0% measured with a point-of-care device.

Diabetes self-care. The Diabetes Self-Care Inventory-Revised was used to assess adherence to 12 diabetes selfcare behaviors (eg, medications, glucose monitoring, exercise, diet) over the past month.23 Items are rated from 1 (‘‘never do this’’) to 5 (‘‘always do this’’).

Diabetes self-efficacy. The 8-item Self-Efficacy for Diabetes was used to assess confidence to manage diabetes (eg, judge when to seek medical care, choose appropriate foods). Items are rated from 1 (‘‘not at all confident’’) to 10 (‘‘totally confident’’).24

Potentially inappropriate medications. Beers criteria were used to characterize PIMs, which are medications with high risk/safety ratios (eg, potent anticholinergics, longacting benzodiazepines) in older adults.19 The study pharmacist reviewed medical records and participant reports to identify PIMs.

Medical trust. The 17-item Multidimensional Trust in Health Care Systems was used to assess trust in physicians (eg, ‘‘I trust my doctor’s judgment’’); health payers (eg, ‘‘Healthcare payers are good at what they do’’); and health care institutions (eg, ‘‘Healthcare institutions provide high quality care’’). Responses range from 5 (strongly agree) to 1 (strongly disagree).25

# Results
From November 2017 to February 2020, 447 ED patients were screened for eligibility, and 247 of them were ineligible for the following reasons: declined to participate (n = 103); unable to contact (n = 72); met exclusion criteria (cognitive impairment, n = 24; excluded psychiatric diagnoses, n = 3; and no diabetes-related visit and low HbA1c; n = 20); other (n = 23); and died (n = 2). Two hundred patients (139 [69.5%] recruited from Jefferson and 61 [30.5%] recruited from Einstein) were randomized to DM I-TEAM (n = 98) or UMC (n = 102).

Randomized and nonrandomized patients were similar in age (66.1 [10.5] vs. 64.9 [9.5], respectively) but nonrandomized patients included more males (20.1% vs. 13.3%, respectively; X2 = 8.645; df 1; P = 0.003). The CONSORT

figure (Fig. 1) shows participant completion rates. Follow-up data on ED visits/hospitalizations at 12 months were available for all participants. The 2 treatment groups had similar baseline demographic and clinical characteristics (Table 1). Of importance, 68% of all participants resided in impoverished neighborhoods (ie, Area Deprivation Index scores ‡8/10).

DM I-TEAM participants received a mean of 5.1 (1.9) initial CHW treatment sessions (78 participants [79.6%] received all 6 initial sessions), and a mean of 2.3 (1.3) booster sessions (70 participants [71.4%] received all 3 booster sessions). Seventy-nine participants (80.1%) had at least 1 diabetes nurse telehealth visit and 74 participants (75.5%) had both visits. Twenty-three participants (23.5%) had a PCP telehealth visit. The most common reason for no PCP telehealth visit was that many PCPs did not do telehealth visits (before COVID). At baseline, the clinical pharmacist made suggestions to PCPs about PIMs use for the 43 DM I-TEAM participants (43.9%) who were taking a PIM. Sixteen participants (16.1%) discontinued DM I-TEAM treatment because of loss of interest.

Sixty-eight (69.4%) DM I-TEAM participants and 69 (67.6%) UMC participants had at least 1 incident ED visit or hospitalization over 12 months. The unadjusted rates by treatment group were 2.31 events per person year (95% confidence interval [CI] 1.81–2.94) and 2.09 events per person year (95% CI 1.65–2.65), respectively. The distribution of events appeared similar in both groups (Table 2). The IRR for DM I-TEAM versus UMC, adjusted for the stratification variables was 1.11 (95% CI 0.79–1.56; P = 0.54) (Table 2). Adjustment for number of medications, number of health conditions, and insurance type yielded a similar result.

There were no treatment group differences in rates of ED visits nor hospitalizations when these events were considered separately. To assess COVID’s impact, authors note that 93% of participants were randomized before March

2020, and no treatment group differences in incident events were evident before versus after March 2020 (data not shown). Over 12 months, DM I-TEAM participants achieved significantly higher (better) diabetes selfmanagement, diabetes self-efficacy, and institutional trust scores than UMC participants. There were no treatment group differences in rates of HbA1c levels £7.0%, PIMs use, nor depressive symptoms (Table 2).
