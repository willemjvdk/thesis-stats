# Research Design and Methodology
## Participants

This study was approved by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board (PRO13040441). All participants provided written informed consent. Potential participants were referred by internal medicine clinic staff located in a large mid-Atlantic US city (population 300,000). Preliminary eligibility was determined via a phone screening, and potentially eligible individuals were further evaluated at baseline for A1C and diabetes distress criteria (see Figure 1, Consort Diagram).

Inclusion criteria were (1) diagnosis of T2DM, (2) mean score of 3.0 or greater on the Diabetes Distress Scale (DDS)20 (moderate distress), (3) A1C level of 8.0 or greater, (4) self-reported problems following T2DM treatment plans, (5) taking at least 1 oral antihyperglycemic medication at the time of study enrollment, (6) receiving T2DM treatment in a primary care center, and (7) age 30 to 65 years. Potential participants were excluded if they exhibited (1) serious mental illness (eg, bipolar disorder or schizophrenia), (2) serious cognitive impairment, or (3) serious suicide risk.

## Procedures

This pilot study used a randomized controlled trial design to investigate the preliminary feasibility and acceptability of the intervention. Randomization allowed for investigation of differences in feasibility/acceptability based on different durations of the intervention. The small pilot randomized controlled trial study design used is consistent with those commonly used in the diabetes–mental health literature.38 All participants completed baseline measures assessing demographic and medical history (including diabetes and medication), A1C, body mass index, level of distress, problems with diabetes management, diabetes self-care, and self-reported medication taking. All participants also used an electronic medication event monitoring system (MEMS) to measure consistency in taking oral diabetes medication for the duration of the study. Following the baseline assessment,

participants were randomly assigned to 1 of 4 arms: treatment as usual (TAU) or the 6, 8, or 12 weekly 30-minute phone CBT sessions. Randomization assignments were computer-generated and placed in sealed security envelopes. Following randomization, intervention patients were instructed in the use of the CBT MobileWork-DM© smartphone app.39 All participants, both CBT and TAU, were scheduled to complete follow-up assessments at about 16 weeks, which included having A1C measured and MEMS cap bottles returned. Figure 2 depicts a flow chart for the study.

## Interventions

The app treatment arm consisted of (1) CBT by telephone (once weekly for 6, 8, or 12weeks), (2) CBT MobileWork-DM© adjunctive app, and (3) ongoing TAU T2DM care. Figure 3 depicts screenshots of the CBT app.

CBT therapists were trained on the basics of T2DM self-management and the impact of diabetes distress on completing required self-care activities. The CBT intervention featured the general CBT agenda25 and the following specific problem-solving structure (see Table 1), which employs a logical-deductive model.40,41 The telephone-delivered CBT intervention worked in concert with the app to promote the development and practice of skills to challenge negative cognitions (eg, “I can’t follow this diet, exercise is too difficult”) and address barriers related to T2DM self-management including diabetes distress (see Table 2). CBT skills covered included

challenging negative thinking, promoting engagement in activities, and improving interpersonal and problemsolving skills.

The CBT MobileWork-DM© app used in this study was a modified version of the original CBT MobileWork©, 39 a smartphone app that was developed to support CBT skills practice and address typical cognitive distortions and behavioral issues related to major depression. The original app demonstrated high patient and therapist usability and satisfaction.39 The CBT MobileWork-DM© version was modified to target cognitive distortions, behavioral issues, and functional impairment associated with diabetes distress and insufficient T2DM self-management. For example, text was changed to target diabetes distress rather than depression, and additions of T2DM-related content were made to the library (eg, psychoeducation on nutrition, websites and apps for diabetes, and strategies for exercise and health). In one-on-one instruction sessions, participants were provided the rationale for the app and instructions to use each section of the app.

Treatment as usual care was continued by all participants in this study and consisted of all care that was recommended by their primary care physicians. These may include appointments with diabetes care and education specialists and dieticians and/or other diabetes-related education as recommended by their physicians. Participants in the TAU treatment arm received only TAU care, whereas participants in the CBT arm received CBT (by telephone with app) in addition to TAU care.

## Measures

Intervention usability and acceptability. Intervention usability and acceptability was evaluated using the (1) Acceptability Questionnaire42 assessing satisfaction with the CBT intervention, (2) Assignment Compliance Rating Scale (ACRS)43 assessing adherence to CBT homework, and (3) Computer Systems Usability Questionnaire (CSUQ)42 assessing usability of CBT MobileWork-DM© app.

The Acceptability Questionnaire is a 14-item self-report measure created for this study based in part on the Post-Study System Usability Questionnaire.42 Items inquired about whether the CBT interventions were feasible, helpful, efficient, and effective and were rated on a 1 (strongly agree) to 7 (strongly disagree) scale, with lower scores indicating better acceptability.

The \mathrm { A C R } S ^ { 4 3 }is a therapist-rated assessment of patient homework completion. Therapists rated on the ACRS the degree to which the patient completed the assigned task from 1 (did not complete the assigned homework) to 8 (did more of the assignment homework than was requested). Higher scores indicated greater homework completion.

The CSUQ42 is a 19-item self-report evaluation of the usability of the CBT MobileWork-DM© app system. Usability characteristics assessed by the items included ease of use, ease of learning, simplicity, effectiveness, and user-interface. Items were rated on a scale from 1 (strongly agree) to 7 (strongly disagree), with higher scores indicating greater usability of the CBT MobileWork-DM© app system.

Diabetes outcomes. Diabetes outcomes were evaluated using (1) DDS20 assessing distress regarding diabetes management, (2) \mathrm { M E M S ^ { 4 4 } }assessing objective medication taking, (3) \mathrm { A } 1 \mathrm { C } , { } ^ { 4 5 }(4) Morisky Green Levine Medication Adherence Scale (MGL)46,47 assessing subjective medication taking, (5) Problem Areas in Diabetes Questionnaire (PAID)48 assessing problems regarding diabetes management, and (6) Summary of Diabetes Self-Care Activities (SDSCA)49 assessing frequency of diabetes management behaviors. All participants in both CBT and TAU groups completed the DDS, PAID, MMAS, and SDSCA at baseline and 16 weeks. A1C was measured at baseline and 16 weeks, and MEMS bottles were returned at 16 weeks.

The DDS20 is a 17-item self-report questionnaire in which patients rate various problems on a scale from 1 (not a problem) to 6 (serious problem). The questionnaire has 4 subscales consisting of problems relating to (a) emotional burden, (b) physical-related distress, (c) regimen-related distress, and (d) interpersonal distress. Higher scores indicate greater distress, with mean item scores of 3 or greater indicating moderate or higher distress.

The MEMS44 is a type of medication vial cap fitted with a microprocessor that records the date and time that the cap is removed, with cap removal serving as a proxy for medication taking. Medication taking via MEMS was calculated using total number of doses taken over the expected prescribed doses for the duration of the study. MEMS data are reported as percentage of doses measured.

A1C levels were collected using A1cNow,45 a readily available, portable point-of-care test device to measure blood glucose level. A1C levels were measured at baseline and at final follow-up (ie, week 16). The A1cNow device provides %A1C, which measures what percentage of hemoglobin proteins in your blood are glycated providing an estimate of blood sugar control over 3 months. As defined by the American Diabetes Association, a level of less than 7% is in the acceptable range.

The MMAS46,47 is a 4-item self-report measure of medication taking using a dichotomous yes (0) or no (1) response format. Higher total scores indicated better medication-taking.

The PAID48 is a 20-item self-report instrument that measures a wide range of diabetes-specific feelings related to living with diabetes and its treatment. Items are rated on a scale of 0 (not a problem) to 4 (serious problem), with higher scores indicating greater problems with negative feelings relating to living with diabetes and managing diabetes.

The SDSCA49 is a 23-item self-report instrument that measures typical diabetes self-care activities over the past week. The respondent is asked to report how many of the last 7 days appropriate regimens were followed for such care activities as diet, exercise, blood sugar testing, foot checks, foot care, and medication taking. Items are rated by how many days in the past week these activities occurred (ie, 0 to 7 days), with higher scores indicating greater engagement in diabetes self-care activities.

## Statistical Analyses

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS (version 27, IBM Corp, Armonk, NY). For Aim 1, feasibility parameters (eg, recruitment and retention of study participants; acceptability of treatment) were evaluated using means for continuous feasibility indicators and frequency counts and percentages for categorical feasibility indicators. For Aim 2 and Aim 3, examining changes over time for each outcome variable (ie, diabetes distress, medication taking, and A1C for each treatment group), individual change scores were computed to describe change over time from baseline to end of treatment. Outcome variables were presented for individual participants and summarized by treatment group using descriptive statistics.

# Results
## Study Sample

Participants were 12 patients who were middle-aged, mostly female, and college educated (see Table 3). The racial profile of the sample was evenly divided between White and African American. The 12-week phone CBT with app and the TAU groups possessed higher levels of education than the 6- and 8-week CBT groups. Unlike all other groups, the 12-week CBT group exhibited 100% employment.

Most participants (75%) were taking Metformin and/or insulin (80%) to manage their T2DM (see Table 3). Additionally, the sample also exhibited a high duration of having T2DM (12.58 years; SD = 3.61), which illustrates the level of chronicity and self-management needs of this sample.

## Aim 1: Aceptability of the CBT Intervention Protocol

The CBT group exhibited an attrition rate of 11.1% (1/9 participants), whereas the TAU-only control group exhibited an attrition rate of 33.3% (1/3 participants).

Responses on the Acceptability Questionnaire indicated high ratings for the feasibility, ease of learning, and relevance of the CBT intervention to diabetes management. CSUQ ratings indicated that the app was moderately usable (M = 3.0; SD = 2.1; scale, 1-7; lower = more usable; sample range = 1.16-5.58; n = 6). Acceptability

Questionnaire ratings further indicated that participants not only found the app to be highly acceptable but also would continue to use the app after they completed the study (M = 1.8; SD = 0.6; scale 1-7; lower = more acceptable; sample range = 1-2.64; n = 7). The app data indicate that participants used it regularly (daily actions: M = 33.3; SD = 19.7; sample range = 15.9-82.0).

# Aim 2: Effect of Intervention on Clinical Outcomes
On average, participants in the intervention groups exhibited improvements on the clinical measures of A1C, DDS, and PAID related to T2DM management (see Table 4). A1C changed by –0.40 to –2.80 (M = −1.69, SD=−0.88), and DDS changed by 12 to −49points (M=−18.43, SD=22.99) among the CBT participants. With respect to medication taking, the self-report MMAS-4 had changes that varied between a –200% decrease to a 100% increase in medication taking among the intervention participants, and the MEMS measure of overall medication adherence during the study varied from 16% to 96%. The ACRS scores ranged from 3 to 7, indicating moderate to high CBT homework completion. The SDSCA (see Table 5) showed variability but indicated that most participants improved in diet and exercise. Areas of self-management on the SDSCA with limited change were specific diet (red meats), medications, glucose checks, foot checks, and foot care.

Participants in the TAU group (ie, 5016 and 5017) also exhibited high MEMS adherence rates (78% to 90%), reductions in DDS (from −19 to −23 points or −32% to −39%), reductions in PAID (from 22 to 40 points or 58% to 62%), and moderate decreases in A1C (−1.00 to −3.30).

# Aim 3: Treatment Outcomes Relative to the Number of CBT Sessions
Among the CBT groups, participants who received 12 sessions of CBT experienced the most improvements in diabetes-related outcomes, and those who received only 6 sessions exhibited the least improvement. A1C levels decreased by up to −2.80 points in the 12-session group versus by up to −0.90 points in the 6-session group. Similarly, DDS decreased by up to −49 points in the 12-session group, compared to only up to −12 points in the 6-session group. The MEMS adherence also was higher in the 12-session group (up to 89%) compared to the 6-session group (up to 36%). Overall, measures of clinical outcomes exhibited the greatest improvements for patients who received 12 weeks of CBT with TAU (see Table 6).

## Conclusion

This small, randomized pilot study investigated a remote telehealth intervention protocol for improving T2DM distress and management consisting of phone CBT augmented by the smartphone app CBT MobileWork-DM©. Participants received TAU plus either 6, 8, or 12 sessions of weekly phone CBT with app in addition to TAU. Participants who received CBT in addition to TAU care exhibited greater medication taking and greater decreases in A1C and diabetes distress than participants in the TAU only group. Moreover, attrition in the intervention was low (89% of participants completed all CBT sessions). The CBT intervention received high ratings for feasibility and relevance to T2DM, and the adjunctive CBT MobileWork-DM© app received high ratings for usability and acceptability. The use of the CBT MobileWork-DM© app among T2DM patients to complete CBT homework activities may have helped to reduce diabetes distress and improve self-management of T2DM. The results of this pilot study indicate that this may be a feasible CBT telehealth model to improve diabetes self-management and clinical outcomes. Subsequent research is needed to expand on these preliminary results and generate more empirical data to guide recommendations for clinical use.

Given the surge in smartphone ownership among all demographic groups,50 this phone CBT with app approach may serve as an important and readily available intervention model. Phone-delivered CBT with a therapeutic focus

on unhelpful cognitive appraisals that impact diabetes distress and medication taking may be an effective method for improving diabetes distress, diabetes-related management behaviors, and diabetes disease outcomes. This telehealth approach addresses practical barriers to CBT treatment engagement and accessibility (eg, distance, stigma, and time) and has the potential to improve access to psychological supportive services in an environment of shortages. Research has shown that diabetes education specialists have been successfully trained to deliver CBT interventions.51 Adding CBT to the diabetes educator’s skill set may be beneficial to improving T2DM clinical outcomes. Further research is needed to determine whether this telehealth model can be utilized effectively by diabetes care and education specialists to improve T2DM clinical outcomes.

We must acknowledge a few limitations of our study. The use of a small-scale pilot randomized controlled trial design with less than 5 participants in each of the 4 groups substantially limited the interpretations of treatment efficacy and generalizability of the treatment findings and increased the likelihood of variability in the outcomes. The patient sample, although diverse with respect to gender and race, lacked diversity with respect to employment status and education. These limitations may have exaggerated the positive study outcomes given the high level of participant education—especially in the CBT group with 12 sessions.

Nonetheless, this CBT intervention is unique in that it targets diabetes distress and incorporates a smartphone app, CBT MobileWork-DM©, to complement phone CBT sessions. Patients receiving 12 weekly CBT phone sessions and the CBT app, in combination with T2DM standard care, experienced the best improvements in diabetes-related outcomes. These findings suggest that the use of CBT phone sessions augmented with a CBT smartphone application has the potential to improve physical and mental health outcomes, increase diabetes selfmanagement, and reduce diabetes distress among T2DM patients.
