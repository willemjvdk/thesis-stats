# Methods
## Design, Recruitment, and Procedures

We employed a 3 × 2 randomized controlled factorial trial using three parallel groups (SM-HL, TEL-HL, and usual care) and two health levels of health literacy (high and low). The SM-HL group was provided with smartphone-based technology for the social media intervention, while the TEL-HL was supported with traditional phone calls, although both groups utilized health literacy–sensitive diabetes management practices. Participants were recruited from endocrinology outpatient units at two general hospitals, which were equipped with 940 and 603 beds, respectively, located in the southeast region of South Korea. The eligibility criteria for patients were as follows: (a) diagnosed with type 2 diabetes, (b) poor diabetes control (HbA1c > 7.0%), (c) can read Korean, (d) and owns a smartphone. We recruited potential subjects from referrals by nurses in the hospitals’ endocrinology outpatient units. The nurses approached potential participants waiting for a meeting with them and asked if they would be willing to participate in the study. After explaining the purpose, procedure, privacy, and confidentiality of the study and the participants’ rights—including withdrawal from the study at any time—written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The institutional

review boards of the hospitals approved this study in advance (2016-07-025001).

All participants who agreed to participate in the study were randomly assigned in a 1:1:1 ratio to each treatment group using a web-based randomization tool (www.randomizer.org). Each participant’s allocation assignment was sealed in an envelope and kept from the participants. Then, the data collector—who did not participate in the intervention—collected data at baseline and measured the health literacy level. Following baseline data collection, two nurse diabetes educators delivered the initial diabetes education for all patients using different strategies according to the treatment condition. Nurse diabetes educators used short, easyto-read educational materials and the teach-back method during diabetes education sessions (Table S1). Over 8 weeks, a study nurse encouraged weekly action plans for the SM-HL via social media services and for the TEL-HL via phone calls. Data collection occurred at baseline and at 9 weeks for patient activation, diabetes self-care behaviors, and HbA1c, then additionally just for HbA1c at 12 weeks. The data collector, who assessed all of the outcomes but did not participate in the intervention, was blinded to the study. We collected data from September 2016 to December 2017.

Based on the G-power software program (Faul, Erdfelder, Buchner, & Lang, 2009) with a 3 × 2 factorial design, two-tailed test, alpha  =  .05, and the effect size of health literacy–based diabetes intervention on HbA1c of 0.48 (Rothman et al., 2004), the required sample size was 22 per cell to achieve a statistical power of .80. With the expectation of a 15% dropout rate, we planned to recruit at least 152 subjects. We recruited 155 subjects but excluded 4 who did not fit the eligibility criteria. In total, 151 subjects and 120 subjects were assessed at 9-week and 12-week follow-ups, respectively (Figure S1).

## Description of the Interventions

Table S1 displays the components of the SM-HL and TEL-HL interventions and the usual care for the control group. For all patients, nurse diabetes educators provided initial diabetes self-management education in which the content consisted of general information on diabetes mellitus, complications, blood glucose monitoring, nutritional management, exercise, and medication utilization, using different strategies. For the TEL-HL and SM-HL groups, the educators distributed an easy-to-read diabetes education brochure that was developed for this study using simple words, short sentences of fewer than 50 words, pictures rather than text, and emphasizing key points with bullet points; in addition, they used the

teach-back method during the initial education session (DeWalt et al., 2011). For the control group, nurses delivered the usual education using a conventional diabetes education brochure. The nurse diabetes educators had more than 5 years of clinical experience in diabetes education and were certified diabetes educators by the Korean Diabetes Association. Before the intervention, we trained these educators in the teach-back method and the action-planning strategy.

After the initial diabetes self-management education sessions, the study nurses delivered either social media– based or telephone-based self-management support to each group for 8 weeks. The study interventionist encouraged patients in the SM-HL or TEL-HL groups to make an action plan for each week and provide answers to their questions whenever needed via social media or a phone call, respectively. For the patients in the SM-HL group, the study interventionist showed each patient in the office how to gain access to and use the social media service via a smartphone and provided step-by-step written instructions about accessing and navigating the social media service. The social media–based self-management support, called Mastering Diabetes, was developed in Naver Band, one of the most popular social media services in Korea, which has group announcement, instant messaging, and member feedback features (http://band.us/ko). The social media–based self-management support was operated as a closed social media service, which allowed the registered study participants to access, share, and view posts. The navigation menu for action planning in the social media service was developed in the form of passive use function to relieve the burden of typing on a smartphone among patients with low health literacy (Sarkar et al., 2010). The study interventionist also uploaded diabetes self-management information in both video and short text formats, including information regarding diet, exercise, foot care, and complications every week and encouraged patients to share their diabetes management experiences on the discussion board. The study interventionist had more than 5 years’ diabetes education experience as a nurse in a clinical setting and was trained about the intervention with the intervention protocol prior to the study.

## Measurement

As outcome variables, we assessed patient activation level and diabetes self-care behaviors at baseline and 9 weeks after enrollment and the HbA1c level (%) at baseline, 9 weeks, and 12 weeks. All outcomes were assessed by a data collector who was unaware of the patients’ group allocation.

Patient activation level was assessed with the Korean version of the Patient Activation Measure 13 (PAM13-K), which is a unidimensional 13-item scale for assessing patients’ self-reported knowledge, skills, and confidence related to the self-management of health condition (Ahn, Yi, Ham, & Kim, 2015; Hibbard, Mahoney, Stockard, & Tusler, 2005). Examples of items include “When all is said and done, I am the person responsible for taking care of my health,” and “I am confident that I can help prevent or reduce problems associated with my health.” Each item was scored on a 4-point Likert scale that ranges from “strongly disagree” (1 point) to “strongly agree” (4 points). Responses of “not applicable” were scored as missing. The total score was calculated by summing each score and transforming them into a 0–100 metric in which higher scores indicate higher activation. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the PAM13-K was .88 in the study by Ahn et al. (2014) and .90 in this study. Rasch analysis and factor analysis of the PAM13-K demonstrated the scale’s validity (Ahn et al., 2014; Hibbard et al., 2005).

Diabetes self-care behaviors were assessed using a validated 17-item revised Korean version of the Summary of Diabetes Self-care Activities measure (SDSCA), assessing how many days per week patients followed the diabetes regimen of diet, exercise, bloodglucose testing, foot care, and medication (Chang & Song, 2009; Toobert, Hampson, & Glasgow, 2000). The total score for self-care behaviors was calculated by summing the scores of each subscale—general diet, specific diet, exercise, blood-glucose testing, foot care, and medications—and transforming them into a 0–7 scale. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the SDSCA was .77 among Korean older adults (Chang & Song, 2009) and .67 in this study. Constructed validity was supported using factor analysis among older Korean adults with type 2 diabetes (Chang & Song, 2009).

Health literacy was measured at baseline using the Short Form of the Korean Functional Health Literacy Test (Kim, 2017). The cut-off value of ?6, classified as low health literacy, showed a sensitivity of 0.77 and specificity of 0.65 for predicting low health literacy, and the validity and internal consistency were supported by a previous study (Kim, 2017). Demographic (age, sex, income, and education) and illness-related characteristics (duration of diabetes, diabetes complications, insulin use, and treatment regimen) were assessed using questionnaires.

## Statistical Analysis

We compared patients’ characteristics at baseline by intervention status using analysis of variance tests for

normally distributed variables, the Kruskal-Wallis test for non-normally distributed variables, and the \chi ^ { 2 }or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. To assess the correlation between study variables, we utilized Spearman’s rho correlation analysis because the variables were non-normally distributed. To compare the outcome variables, we conducted a multivariate general linear model analysis with adjustments for the covariates identified as being significantly different among the groups at baseline (age and HbA1c). We did not adjust for education level because it was highly correlated with health literacy level (Bailey et al., 2014). In addition, we examined the effect of the interventions by health literacy level by analyzing the interaction effects between the treatment condition and health literacy level in the model. We performed all analyses using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The significance level was set at p \ > \ . 0 5.

# Results
Baseline patient characteristics were similar with regard to sex, income, duration of illness, presence of diabetes complications, use of insulin upon enrollment, and use of an oral glycemic agent among each of the groups (Table S2). However, age, education, HbA1c, and health literacy level at baseline differed among the groups. Patients in the SM-HL group were younger and more educated, and had poorer glycemic control and higher health literacy. There were no significant differences in the baseline characteristics between patients who dropped out and those who did not, except in the control group, as those not treated with an oral glycemic agent were more likely to have dropped out at the 12-week follow-up ( \chi ^ { 2 } ~ = ~ 9 . 2 5, p  =  .008).

## Patient Activation

In the analysis of treatment by health literacy level interaction, there was a significant interaction effect for patient activation level [F(2, 140) = 3.55, p =  .031, observed power =  .65] (Table S3). As shown in Figure  S2, patients with low health literacy had the highest patient activation level at the 9-week followup when provided with the SM-HL (adjusted mean 78.01; 95% confidence interval [CI] 70.70–85.30) compared to the TEL-HL group (adjusted mean 73.72; 95% CI 68.05–79.38) and the usual care control group (adjusted mean 70.27; 95% CI 64.55–75.98), but these differences were not significant. However, patients with high health literacy had a significantly higher patient activation level when provided with the usual care

control (adjusted mean 81.93; 95% CI 76.26–87.59) than the TEL-HL (adjusted mean 78.29; 95% CI 72.24–84.34) and the SM-HL (adjusted mean 73.69; 95% CI 68.54–78.84).

Follow-up analysis of all pairwise comparisons for the three treatment conditions by the two health literacy levels indicated that—in the usual care control group—patients with high health literacy had a significantly higher patient activation level than those with low health literacy (adjusted mean difference 11.67; 95% CI 3.70–19.65). However, in the SM-HL and TEL-HL groups, patients with high health literacy had no significant difference in patient activation level compared to those with low health literacy (adjusted mean difference 4.57; 95% CI −3.80–12.94; and adjusted mean difference 4.32; 95% CI −4.57–13.21, respectively).

## Self-Care Behaviors

A multivariate general linear model analysis demonstrated no significant interaction effects of treatment condition and health literacy on self-care behaviors (see Table S3). Analysis for the overall treatment effects of the treatment conditions showed a significant difference among the treatment groups for self-care behaviors at the nine-week follow-up [F(2, 137) = 3.45, p = .035]. Patients in the TEL-HL group had a significantly higher score for self-care behaviors than the usual care control group at follow-up (adjusted difference 0.48; 95% CI 0.11–0.86; observed power = 0.64). However, no difference was observed in selfcare behaviors between the SM-HL group and the usual care control group (adjusted difference 0.15; 95% CI –0.26–0.56).

## HbA1c at 9 and 12 Weeks

In general, all three groups showed improvements in levels of HbA1c from baseline to follow-up (see Table S3). A multivariate general linear model analysis showed no significant interaction effects for HbA1c at the 9- and 12-week follow-ups. Analysis of the overall treatment effects of the treatment conditions showed no significant difference among the treatment groups for HbA1c at 9 and 12 weeks’ follow-up [F(2,  137) = \ 1 . 6 7 , p \ = \ . 1 9 3 ; F ( 2 , \ 1 1 4 ) \ = \ 2 . 0 1 , \ p \ = \ . 1 3 9 ].
