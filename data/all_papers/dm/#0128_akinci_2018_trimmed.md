## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# Method
Subjects with type 2 diabetes aged between 40 and 65 years, who were followed up routinely in the Department of Endocrine and Metabolism Diseases, were included in this study from March 2015 to August 2016 (Figure 1). This study was approved by the University Hospital Ethical Board with file number 2014/1977/1977 and conducted based on the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided informed consent. The protocol of the study was prospectively registered in

the clinicaltrials.gov portal, with the number NCT02655627.

Subjects were included in the study if they were diagnosed with type 2 diabetes for at least one year and had HbA1c values between 6.5% to 11%; could walk for 420 m in a six-minute walking test; had a high-speed Internet connection at home, in the workplace or via cell phone; and if they could communicate in the Turkish language. The exclusion criteria for subjects were as follows: participation in an exercise programme in the last six months, uncontrolled hypertension, uncontrolled arrhythmia, and inability to participate in an exercise programme due to neurologic and respiratory system disorders. In addition, patients were excluded if they had undergone percutaneous transluminal coronary angiography. Moreover, excluded were patients with cardiac pacemakers and those with a history of stroke, myocardial infarction, amputation, severe musculoskeletal disorder, kidney failure (estimated glomerular filtration rate <30 mL/minute.), chronic liver disease (alanine aminotransferase or aspartate aminotransferase <3× the normal upper limit), diabetic ulcer or peripheral neuropathy, haemolytic anaemia and renal anaemia, as well as patients who were pregnant or breastfeeding.

Subjects were randomized into one of three groups, Group A (the control group), Group B (supervised exercise group), or Group C (Internetbased exercise group), using a numbered series of prefilled envelopes specifying group assignment created by a computer-based (GraphPad Software) random number sequence.17 Randomization was performed by a researcher (I.S.) who was not involved in the assessments. To ensure group concealment, the subjects were unaware of the allocation sequence.

# Outcome measurements
All measurements were performed baseline and after the intervention by the same researcher (I.Y.), who was blind to the group allocation. The sociodemographic and clinical features of subjects were collected. Primary outcomes including HbA1c, fasting blood glucose, and blood lipids (high-density

lipoprotein, low-density lipoprotein, triglyceride, and total cholesterol) were measured via the blood sample. The laboratory tests were carried out at the same laboratory in the University Hospital. The secondary outcome measures were body mass index, waist and hip circumference, functional capacity, physical activity, and quality of life.

Body mass index was calculated as weight (in kg) divided by height (in m2). Waist circumference was taken from the umbilicus level, and hip circumference was taken from the femur trochanter major. Both measurements were done at exhalation phase using a tape measure while the patient was standing on both feet with equal weight on each (the values of hip and waist measurements were recorded in centimetres). Waist/hip ratios were also calculated. Functional capacity was evaluated with the six-minute walking test in accordance with the American Thoracic Society’s criteria.18 Subjects were instructed to walk in a 30-m-long corridor. Blood pressure (Erka D sphygmomanometer), heart rate, and oxygen saturation and dyspnoea severity (Baseline® fingertip pulse oximeter) were measured before and after the test, and the six-minute walking distance (m) was noted. The physical activity level of the subjects was assessed with a pedometer. Subjects were asked to wear a pedometer (Omron HJ 321E Walking Style) on their waist or their pockets every day for the period of a week. The pedometer was used to record the number of steps for seven days, and this was used to calculate the average number of steps. Quality of life was assessed by the European Quality of Life 5 Dimensions ranked along three levels ranging from −0.53 to 1.00, with 1.00 indicating perfect health.19 The scale was validated in a previous study.20

## Intervention

Group A: control group (n = 22). The brochure was designed by the investigators and provided to the subjects. In the brochure, subjects were educated about the importance and benefits of physical activity and exercise for people with diabetes, with some practical tips for lifestyle included as well.

Group B: supervised exercise group (n = 22). Subjects participated in aerobic and resistance group exercises under the supervision of a physical therapist (B.A.) three days a week for eight weeks (Online Appendix A). Each group comprised four to six subjects. The training programme was applied according to frequency, intensity, time, type, principles, and progression of the training, as shown in Online Appendix A. Subjects were asked to attend 24 exercise sessions with a duration of 50–60 minutes, including rest periods.

Group C: Internet-based exercise group (n=21). A website was created (www.diyabetvehareket.com), and subjects in this group subscribed to the website. Subjects were provided with exercise videos containing aerobic and resistance exercises (Online Appendix A), three days a week for eight weeks. The training programme was conducted according to similar principles. B.A. provided verbal instructions in the recorded videos to the subjects in order to ensure that the exercises were done correctly and modified where necessary. See also Online Appendix B for details of website design and content and the Internet-based exercise reporting system. The subjects in the Internet-based exercise group submitted their reports using the Internet-based reporting system. Subjects in Group C were also reminded to complete three exercise sessions each week via a short phone message every Monday, and subjects were encouraged to contact the physical therapist if they had any questions. The system was used to remind subjects who had not completed their previous weekly three exercise sessions. All subjects in Group B and Group C were expected to attend to 24 exercise sessions in total over the course of the study. Subjects who failed to complete three-session training for three consecutive weeks without any reason (although they were invited to visit the website by reminder phone calls) were dropped from Group B and Group C. Based on the subject Internet reports, subjects who reported hypoglycaemia were instructed to adhere to their routine medication time, activity, and dietary regimen.

For both exercise groups, subjects were informed about the hypoglycaemia symptoms and advised to stop exercise if the symptoms occurred. For each

session, fasting blood glucose levels before and after exercise, perceived difficulty level during the training (according to the BORG scale21), the presence of hypoglycaemia, and any additional pertinent notes were reported. Subjects in Group C used an Internet-based exercise reporting system for reporting these parameters. In subjects whose blood glucose level before exercise was <100 mg/dL, measurements were repeated after an intake of 15 g of carbohydrate (six small sugar cubes). Subjects whose blood glucose level was not at least 100 mg/ dL or experienced dizziness, fatigue, or palpitation were not included in the training season. During the treatment, subjects continued their medication or insulin therapy.

## Data analysis

Using the G*power sample size calculator, the sample size was calculated to be 20 for each group with an 80% power (α = 0.05), considering the minimal clinically significant change of 1% in HbA1c.22 Estimating a 10% drop out rate among the participants, the sample size was determined as 66 (22 in each group).

The descriptive statistics were presented as the mean, standard deviation, and percentage. Normal distribution of the group’s variables was analysed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov Z test. An oneway analysis of variance (ANOVA) test was used in order to determine the difference between groups for baseline values. Tukey’s post hoc multiple comparison tests were performed to detect the differences among the groups. A paired t-test was used to determine the differences within a group after the treatment. Chi-square or Fisher exact tests were used to compare categorical data. Postpretreatment change in the groups was compared using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA, repeated measures delta change) with baseline measurements as a covariate. Multiple comparison tests were performed to detect the differences among the groups using Bonferroni corrected significance. We computed this method independently for all primary (HbA1c, fasting blood glucose, high-density lipoprotein, low-density lipoprotein, triglyceride, and cholesterol) and secondary (waist and hip

circumference, six-minute walking distance, number of steps, and Euro-Quality of Life-5 Dimension score) measures. This analysis was assessed by calculating the intention-to-treat effect in the data with dropout cases. The mean values for each parameter which were obtained from the patients who were finished the study were used as second assessment result for the dropped patients in the intention-to-treat analysis. The SPSS 21.0 program was used for the statistical analysis. The two-tailed significance level was accepted as P < 0.05 for all tests.

# Results
A total of 133 subjects were screened for eligibility according to the inclusion criteria. In total, 66 (47 female, 19 male) subjects were assigned to group allocation, but 1 patient did not attend all baseline assessments. In all, 65 subjects were included in the study, and 50 subjects (40 women, 10 men) completed the study. None of the subjects in the study reported adverse events that could be associated with the intervention during the study.

Demographic and clinical data of the subjects in each group and comparisons between groups are shown in Table 1. Age, gender, smoking status, duration of disease, medical treatment, comorbid diseases, and baseline outcome measurements were all similar in the groups (P > 0.05).

A comparison of differences for primary and secondary outcome measurements between groups is shown in Table 2. Analysing the data on an intention-to-treat basis with the data for missing cases being replaced with the mean values for each parameter which were obtained from the participants who were finished the study values gave similar results (P > 0.05). After the treatment, changes in HbA1c (95% confidence interval (CI) = −1.508 to −0.230, P = 0.004), fasting blood glucose (95% CI = −65.404 to −7.459, P = 0.009), waist circumference (95% CI = −8.845 to −0.301, P = 0.032), hip circumference (95% CI = −6.049 to −0.634, P = 0.010), and Euro-Quality of Life (95% CI = 0.014 to 0.282, P = 0.025) were found to be superior in Group B compared to Group A. Changes in HbA1c (95% CI = −1.393 to −0.095,

P = 0.019), cholesterol (95% CI = −43.813 to −2.393, P = 0.024), average amount of steps (95% CI = 110.198 to 2021.926, P = 0.024), six-minute walking distance (95% CI = 6.059 to 55.230, P = 0.010), waist circumference (95% CI = −9.640 to −1.016, P = 0.010), and Euro-Quality of Life (95% CI = 0.002 to 0.272, P = 0.046) were found to be superior in Group C compared to Group A. Group B and Group C changed with equal magnitude (P > 0.05).

All outcomes for the groups and intra-group changes are shown in Table 3. There were no significant differences in any parameter over time in Group A. In Group B, HbA1c, fasting blood glucose, number of steps, six-minute walking distance, waist circumference, hip circumference, and Euro-Quality of Life showed significant improvement after the treatment. For Group B, lipid profile did not show significance. In Group C, HbA1c, fasting blood glucose, cholesterol, number of steps, six-minute walking distance, waist circumferences, hip circumferences, and Euro-Quality of Life all showed a significant improvement after the treatment.
