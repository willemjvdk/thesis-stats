# Methods
## Study design

NICHE was a pilot controlled trial of a biometric/cell phone system using Confidant technology [18]. The study was conducted in collaboration with a primary care network in Connecticut (Community Health Centers – CHC). Two of the seven CHC clinics with similar demographic characteristics in the network elected to participate. Clinics were then randomly allocated to intervention and control. Patients meeting the following inclusion criteria were included in the study: (i) age ≥18 years; (ii) type 2 diabetes diagnosed by a health professional at least 1 year prior and confirmed by other clinical laboratory data (Fasting Plasma Glucose >126 mg dL−1 and/or 2-hour 75-g oral glucose tolerance test OGTT > 200 mg dL−1 ); (iii) controlled by either diet or oral medications for at least 3 months; (iv) BMI > 25; (v) no exogenous insulin use; (vi) a glycosylated haemoglobin (HbA1c) < 8% reflecting fair to good glycemic control; and (vi) serum creatinine <1.5 mg dL−1 . Of the 52 patients meeting the inclusion criteria 30 patient were included in the study.

## Intervention

Two CHC nurse practitioners attended comprehensive training sessions in the NICHE system. Intervention patients then attended a 1-day training workshop facilitated by the nurses, and were taught how to utilize NICHE technology for type 2 diabetes management. The technology is an interactive informational feedback system that uses wireless remote technology to provide tailored feedback and reminders, based on patient-specific data, to patients and providers via messages on cellular phones.

Fifteen subjects from the intervention clinic received training in NICHE technology. After receiving the training intervention patients participated in a 3-month intervention and were required to test their glucose once daily (upon awakening) and wear their pedometers during the day. Subjects were required to upload data onto the NICHE server once daily. They received tailored messages via mobile phone based on the uploaded data. Both patient and provider were able to view readings from uploaded data. Conversely, control subjects continued with standard diabetes self-management and tracked their step count using a pedometer.

# Outcome measures
Outcome measurements were feasibility and utility of the NICHE System in enhancing diabetes management.

## Statistical analysis

Exploratory data analysis was carried out to assess descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, range and normality) for each pertinent outcome measure at baseline. The Student’s t-test was used to evaluate baseline differences in BMI, weight, HbA1C level, glucose level and blood pressure between the intervention and control group. The Fisher’s exact test evaluated baseline differences in gender and smoking status of patients between the two groups. Baseline data for total and specific subscales of DSES, SDSCA and YPAS were not normally distributed; therefore, the non-parametric Wilcoxon Sum Rank test was used to assess between-group differences for these outcome measures. Postintervention changes in the clinical outcomes, surveys scores and self-reported step counts were assessed using the Wilcoxon Sign Rank test for within-group analyses, and the Wilcoxon Sum Rank test for between-group analyses. For all statistical assessments, a two-sided P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All endpoint data were analysed using the intention-totreat principle. Data were analysed using SAS version 9.1 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

# Results
## Baseline characteristics of patients

Thirty patients were randomized by site to either intervention (n = 15) or control (n = 15). As depicted in Table 1, the two groups were comparable at baseline in demographic characteristics, clinical measures, total and subscale survey scores, as well as selfreported step counts (P > 0.05 for all measures). The majority of subjects were female (60.0% – intervention and 66.7% – control) with a mean age of 55.3 (SD = 8.7) years in the intervention group and 56.7 (SD = 10.6) years in the control group. Subjects were overweight, with mean BMI values well over 25 kg m−2 in both groups (intervention: 34.3, SD = 7.4 kg m−2 ; control: 36.9, SD = 12.5 kg m−2 ). The HbA1c levels were less than 8 in each group (Intervention: 6.4, SD = 0.6%; Control: 6.5, SD = 0.7%), and blood pressure values were below 135 mmHg systolic and less than 85 mmHg for diastolic values.

## Self-efficacy at baseline

Individual diabetes self-efficacy scores ranged from 1 ‘Strongly Agree’ to 6 ‘Strongly Disagree’. Mean total self-efficacy score for the intervention group was 2.3 (SD = 0.9), and 2.4 (SD = 0.9) for the control group. Baseline scores for self-care activities, as measured by SDSCA, revealed that, on average, patients in both groups were able to perform SDSCA on 3–6 days of any given week. In both groups, physical activity scores showed that patients in the study were fairly active: leisurely walks and vigorous activity had the highest scores, compared with lower scores for standing and sitting (Table 1).

## Uptake, utilization and focus groups

The level of adherence to intervention varied considerably within the intervention group. Only two of the 15 intervention patients were completely adherent, transmitting their glucose readings and step counts on a daily basis. Four patients utilized the technology for 1–2 months out of the 3-month intervention. Four patients had electronic readings for 1 week, and five did not transmit information altogether.

Focus groups conducted with intervention patients illuminated the difficulties encountered while attempting to utilize the NICHE system. The barriers were primarily technical and inhibited the ease of use of the cellphone and pedometer. Patients reported that there were too many menus to navigate on the cellphone when uploading data, buttons were too small, and that commands changed too frequently. Pedometers were considered ‘uncomfortable and inconvenient’; ‘the batteries keep wearing out and it’s just too complicated to re-program each time’; and ‘this machine (pedometer) keeps chafing on my leg . . . it’s really uncomfortable’. Most patients preferred to use their usual means of diabetic self-management over the NICHE system. However, all reported that if the system was improved, they would prefer using it over usual care.

## Intent-to-treat analysis: intervention group versus control group

Table 2 summarizes within-group differences from baseline in the outcome measures for each group of participants. A trend towards improvement in HbA1C in the intervention group was found (−0.1, SD = 0.3; P = 0.1534), compared with a deterioration in the control group (0.3, SD = 1.0; P = 0.3813), yet without statistical significance. A statistically significant improvement in total selfefficacy scores for the intervention group patients was observed (−0.5, SD = 0.6; P = 0.0080) compared with no improvement in the control group (0.0, SD = 1.0; P = 0.8340). Additionally intervention patient’s self-treatment efficacy score significantly improved (−0.6, SD = 0.9; P = 0.0360) compared with no change in the control (0.0, SD = 1.5; P = 0.9060). An improvement in the YPAS Moving Around score (−1.4, SD = 2.5; P = 0.0960) was observed in the intervention group compared with a decline in the control (1.3, SD = 4.4; P = 0.3010).

Following the intervention, scores remained relatively constant for the self-care activities related to diabetes management, with the exception of patients in the control group significantly improving scores in foot care activities (P = 0.0406).
