# 2 | MATERIALS AND METHODS
## 2.1 | Participants

This clinical study adopted a randomised control design. The study protocol was approved by the responsible ethics committee, and all participants provided informed consent. Type 2 diabetes patients recruited from April to September 2017 and admitted to the department of endocrinology of our hospital between October 2017 and March 2018 were approached and asked to participate in the study. After a clear explanation of the study was provided, those who expressed their willingness to participate were asked to sign a consent form. The inclusion criteria included the following: (a) aged 30 to 60 years old and owning a cell phone, (b) HbA1c level greater than 7.0%, (c) no acute complications or other serious chronic diseases, (d) no mental disorders, (e) effective communication skills, (f) able to perform self-monitoring of blood glucose, and (g) provided informed consent. The exclusion criteria included the following: (a) contact lost with the patient for various reasons, (b) incomplete personal information, and (c) serious diabetic complications.

## 2.2 | Randomisation, allocation concealment, and blinding

Simple randomisation was used to allocate each participant to test or control group. Randomisation was performed by a research team whose members were independent of recruiting participants and providing interventions, so as to reduce the potential selection bias. Generating random numbers with a computer and form a random number table, which were contained in a sequentially numbered opaque, sealed envelope. This study was a single blind design, because participants were unaware of their group assignment as provided either the continuous care or the conventional intervention. It was also impossible to blind the outcome assessor, because the questionnaires were completed by participants.

# 2.3 | Methods
Continuous care was provided to the test group with a mobile phone health application. The platform, jointly developed by our research group and Hangzhou Kangsheng Health Consulting Company, offered seven services, as described below.

The physician made discharge plans for diet, exercise, and blood sugar monitoring according to patients' condition and adjust patients' prescriptions. Nurse practitioners gave blood sugar monitoring, insulin injection reminder, and personalised diet and exercise schedule, which were prescribed by physicians according to the daily disease monitoring status of patients. In the meanwhile, nurse practitioners followed up once a week after discharge and once a month after discharge through health application for half a year.

# 3 | RESULTS
## 3.1 | Data comparisons between the two groups

In total, 120 patients were enrolled. For the total sample, the mean age was 45.4 years, and the majority was male (53.3%). No significant differences were observed between groups in terms of age, gender, educational background, and disease course (P > .05; Table 1).

## 3.2 | Disease cognition and self-management

Before the intervention, there were no statistically significant differences in disease awareness and self-management between the two groups (P > .05). After the 6-month intervention, both abilities were found to be higher in the test group than in the control group. This difference was statistically significant (P < .05; Table 2).

## 3.3 | Blood glucose indicators

After the intervention, levels of FPG, 2-hour postprandial blood glucose, and HbA1c were lower in the test group than in the control group; the differences were statistically significant (P < .05; Table 3).

## 3.4 | Rehospitalisation frequency

Before the intervention, no statistically significant differences were observed in terms of rehospitalisation frequency between the two groups (P > .05). After the intervention, the rate of rehospitalisation was significantly lower in the test group than in the control (P < .05; Table 4).
