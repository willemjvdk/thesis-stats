# METHODS
A 2-group, repeated measures experimental design was used in this pilot study. Patients who were eligible to participate were randomly assigned to either the intervention group (received early recovery management intervention and routine care) or the routine care group (received routine care only) using a previously generated randomization schedule. Routine care for all post-CABG patients at the study institutions consisted of in-hospital teaching related to discharge orders, such as activity, medications, wound care, follow-up with physicians. All patients received a hospital-specific discharge booklet containing information about the recovery process and general information on postsurgical self-care. Inclusion criteria for the study were (1) 21 years old with first-time CABG surgery; (2) diagnosed with diabetes (either type 1 or type 2) before CABG surgery; (3) oriented to person, place, and time; (4) able to read text on the screen of the intervention device, able to hear, and able to speak and read English; (5) possession of nonrotary phone; (6) discharged within 7 days after surgery; and (7) no presurgical physical impairments that would limit physical functioning after surgery. To verify vision and hearing inclusion criteria, patients were asked to read from a printed example of the intervention device screen to evaluate their ability to read text on the actual screen, and they were asked if they had any difficulty hearing, especially on the telephone.

The early recovery management intervention was delivered to the patients using a device called the Health Buddy (Health Hero Network, Redwood City, CA). It is a small device that attaches to the tele-

phone and delivers “daily sessions” or scripts and was used for 6 weeks. Daily sessions provided the subjects with assessment of symptoms (eg, fatigue or incision pain) and strategies to manage reported symptoms, education on CABG recovery (eg, wound management, incentive spirometer, walking), coronary artery disease (CAD) risk-factor modification education and strategies, and positive reinforcement to increase self-efficacy related to symptom management, functioning, and CAD risk-factor modification adherence. Diabetes management education regarding blood glucose testing, meal planning, foot care, and exercise was also included29 (Table I). The daily session was displayed on the screen of the Health Buddy, and the patient responded to questions by pushing 1 of the 4 buttons on the box. The content received by the patients was tailored based on responses. For example, a patient who rated his or her incision pain as a 10 on a 1-to-10 scale would receive more management strategies in that particular session compared with a patient who rated incision pain as a 1. Patients were asked if they were using any of the strategies for symptom management or risk-factor modification or following recommendations for incentive spirometer use, wound management, exercise (Table II). Responses were downloaded to a secure Web site during the middle of the night and reviewed daily by the research nurse. The Health Buddy device is easy to use, and usually -10 minutes/d is required to complete the “daily session” depending on how the patient responds to questions. The device has been field-tested in home settings with patients of all ages and has been especially designed to monitor patients with chronic illnesses.

## Measures

Physical functioning was measured three different ways: (1) MOS SF-36 subscales (physical, role physical, and vitality) at baseline, 6 weeks, and 3 months after surgery; (2) RT3 accelerometer (average daily vector magnitude and average kcal/kg/d) at 2 weeks, 6 weeks, and 3 months after surgery; and (3) activity diary (average daily minutes spent in moderate, hard, or very hard activity) at 2 weeks, 6 weeks, and 3 months after surgery. Data from the Modified 7-Day Activity Tool, administered only at baseline, were used to calculate the variables of values for baseline average kcal/kg/d and average daily minutes spent in moderate, hard, or very hard activity. Psychosocial functioning was measured by (1) MOS SF-36 subscales (role emotional, mental, and social) at baseline, 6 weeks, and 3 months after

surgery; and (2) the HADS at baseline, 2 weeks, 6 weeks, and 3 months after surgery.

## Physical functioning

MOS SF-36 subscales: physical， role physical, vitality. The MOS SF-36 is a multidimensional scale measuring 8 health concepts: physical functioning (10 items), role physical (role limitations caused by physical problems; 4 items), social functioning (2 items), bodily pain (2 items), general mental health (5 items), role emotional (role limitations caused by emotional problems; 3 items), vitality (4 items), general health perceptions (5 items), and change in health status (1 item).30 Standardized response choices are used. Each of the concepts is scored on a scale of 0 to 100, with a higher score indicating better health. Missing scores of each item are estimated using the average score across other items of the scale for that individual subject. Reliability has

been satisfactorily estimated by Cronbach’s alpha in diverse populations.31,32 Cronbach’s alphas in this study sample were: physical subscale .87 to .92; role physical subscale .97 to .99; and vitality subscale .37 to .72.

The Modified 7-Day Activity Tool33 was administered only at baseline to measure average daily minutes spent in moderate, hard, or very hard activity levels and average kcal/kg/d immediately before surgery because there was no activity monitor data or self-report 3-day diary data at baseline. This self-report tool obtains information about the number of minutes spent in sleep on a daily basis as well as minutes spent in light (eg, reading, cooking), moderate (eg, cleaning, raking, walking -3 miles/h), hard (eg, golf, scrubbing), and very hard (eg, swimming) activities. Responses are then converted to a mean number of kilocalories per kilogram expended on a daily basis. Concurrent validity was established

The RT3 accelerometer (by Stayhealthy) was used to obtain a physiologic measure of activity (vector magnitude) and estimated energy expenditure (average kcal/kg/d) on the same days that subjects were completing the activity diary. The RT3 is a triaxial accelerometer that uses advanced microcomputers to register body motion in 3-dimensional space (anteroposterior axis, mediallateral axis, and vertical axis). Counts of accelerations in 3-dimensional axes are sampled every second, stored in a buffer, and then the composite measure for all 3 axes are stored as vector magnitude, 1 activity variable used in this study.35 A subject’s physical characteristics (ie, sex, age, height, and weight) were entered on initializing the RT3 monitor. This information was used by the RT3 to calculate the subject’s resting estimated energy expenditure in kcal/min based on an established predictive equation for men and women. These data were collected for 3 consecutive days (2 weekdays and 1 weekend day), and a variable of average (mean) kcal/kg/d expended was calculated. Accelerometers, such as the RT3, are reported to be 80% reliable in measuring activity when worn during a 3-day period.36 The RT3 is approximately the size and weight of a pager, which made it possible to send the device, called the Health Buddy (Health Hero Network) to subjects by way of expedited mail deliver. The device is unobtrusive, and is comfortably and securely clipped and worn on the waistband.

## Psychosocial functioning

MOS SF-36 subscales: role emotional, mental, and social. See earlier discussion of MOS SF-36 psychometrics. Cronbach’s alphas in this study sample were role emotional subscale .93 to 1.0 and mental subscale .69 to .71. The social functioning subscale contains only 2 items, so reliability testing was not performed on that subscale.

The HADS37 was used to measure states of anxiety and depression. The scale consists of subscales

for anxiety and depression (7 items each) and has a score range of 0 to 21 on each subscale, with higher scores indicating greater levels of anxiety and depression. Factor analyses have verified the 2-factor scale, and reliability has been estimated by Cronbach’s alpha (range from .68 to .93 for the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale-Anxiety Subscale (HADS-A) and from .67 to .90 for the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale-Depression Subscale (HADS-D) in numerous populations).38 Cronbach’s alphas in this CABG sample with diabetes ranged from .76 to .79 for the anxiety subscale and .75 to .77 for the depression subscale.

## Procedure

Institutional Review Committee approval was obtained at the study site and sponsoring institutions before data collection. A power analysis was conducted with a medium effect size of 14, power of .80, and alpha of .5, resulting in a sample size of 100 patients/group, for a total of 200 subjects. For this pilot study, a 25% sample, or 25 patients/group, was used. Eligible patients were contacted before discharge and invited to participate. After we obtained informed consent, they were randomly assigned to either the early recovery management intervention group or the control group. Demographic data were obtained from the medical record, and the MOS SF-36, the HADS, and modified 7-Day Activity Tool were administered in person by the researcher. The RT3 accelerometer and the Self-Report Activity Diary were shown and explained to each patient. Each patient was given a booklet containing written instructions for completion of the diary, examples of a completed diary, instructions for wearing the RT3, and pictures showing an RT3 clipped to a person’s waistband. Use of the Health Buddy was explained, and written instructions were provided. Subjects were asked to read a sample of the intervention text and push the appropriate buttons on the intervention device to ensure their ability to use the Health Buddy correctly. Follow-up MOS SF-36 and HADS data were collected by way of telephone calls. The RT3 and activity diaries were sent by way of expedited mail delivery during the follow-up period. Patients were not compensated for participation in the study.

## Sample

During the study period, 76 patients met inclusion criteria. Sixty-four patients were enrolled in the study; however, 15 patients withdrew within the first

week after hospital discharge (8 from the intervention group and 7 from the control group). Patients who withdrew were similar to the study sample on all baseline variables. Reasons given by patients for withdrawing from the study were that they did not feel well enough to participate or they did not want to connect the telehealth device to their telephones after hospital discharge.

The final sample of 49 CABG patients with diabetes was comprised primarily of white (98%) patients, and the subjects were predominantly men (73.5%). Subjects had a mean age of 61.6 (7.2) years, with the majority of subjects being married (77.6%) and having worked outside of the home (59.2%) before CABG surgery. All patients had been diagnosed with diabetes at least 12 months before being enrolled in the study, with 90% having type 2 diabetes. The majority had multiple CAD risk factors, including a history of hypertension (84%) and hypercholesterolemia (84%) and family history of CAD (76%). Regarding the impact of their diabetes on their pre-existing health status, none of the subjects had documented nephropathy, but 14% of the sample had peripheral vascular disease, and 51% had peripheral neuropathy. Mean hemoglobin ALC value at admission was 7.4 (SD 1.4), and mean discharge fasting glucose was 137 (SD 44.8). Participants had a mean body mass index of 32.68 (SD 7.6), and 53% of the sample had preoperative ejection fractions 50%, with a mean of 47.6 (SD 2.3). Seventy-two percent (n  35) of the sample was either class I or II on the New York Heart Classification, leaving 22% (n  11) in class III, and six % (n  3) in class IV group; comparisons are listed in Table III. At 2 weeks after discharge, 47% of the sample was participating in phase II cardiac rehabilitation. Ultimately, 88% of the intervention group and 84% of the control group participated in phase II cardiac rehabilitation. Descriptive statistics (means, SDs, SEMs, and/or frequency distributions and percentages) and Student t tests revealed no significant differences between groups regarding personal characteristics or clinical profile variables.

## Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (release 11.5; Chicago, IL), and P - .05 was considered significant. Traditional repeated measures analysis of covariance (RM-ANCOVA) were used to analyze MOS SF-36 subscale (physical, role physical, vitality, role emotional, and social) and HADS data. An underlying assumption, homogeneity of regression, of RM-ANCOVA was violated for

the MOS SF-36 mental subscale and the HADS depression scale, so repeated measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA) were used on these variables. There were no baseline data for the RT3 accelerometer and both groups differed by number and group membership at each time point, so analysis of variance ANOVA was used for each time point. The modified 7-Day Activity Tool data were obtained only as a baseline measure of average kcal/kg/d and average number of minutes engaged in moderate, hard, and very hard activity each day. These variables were then used as the covariate for the following outcome variables: average kcal/kg/d (RT3 accelerometer data) and average number of minutes engaged in moderate, hard, and very hard activity/ day (3-Day Activity Diary Data). Because both groups differed by number and group membership at each time point ANCOVA was used at 2 weeks, 6 weeks, and 3 months for both of these outcome

variables. Study aim 1 was to determine if there were differences between the early recovery management intervention group versus the routine care group on physical functioning.
