# 2  |  METHODS
## 2.1  |  Study design and participants

This study was conducted in a district hospital between August 2021 and April 2022. Participants were randomised into two groups. A randomised controlled trial design was used in the study and it was reported using the Consolidated Standard of Reporting Trials-CONSORT 2010-guidelines (Schulz et al., 2010). Sample size was estimated by using the G*POWER software, based on 80% power and 95% confidence interval. Thirty-five individuals in each group were needed to determine the difference between the means of the measurements. An extra 10% of the sample size was calculated in case there would be a sample loss. The flow chart of the study is given in Figure 1.

Inclusion criteria for the study were having a diagnosis of Type 2 diabetes mellitus for at least 6 months, being aged ≥65 years, receiving oral antidiabetic and/or insulin therapy, having a smartphone or a computer, volunteering to participate in the study, having no sensory losses such as hearing and speaking that prevent communication, and having no psychiatric disorders. According to the exclusion criteria, individuals who had Type 2 DM, but were illiterate, had vision and hearing loss and did not volunteer to participate in the study were not included.

Regarding ethical issues, the project was permitted by the Endocrinology Department and University Clinical Research Ethical Committee of the Faculty of Medical Sciences (GO 2021/293- 07.07.2021). Written and verbal informed consent was produced from the participants. The objective of the research was clarified to the patients with diabetes. All stages of our study were carried out according to the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT04626531).

## 2.2  |  Randomisation and blinding

The study was carried out on the internet by using the online video conferencing method. In the first meeting, all participants were informed about the training program and data collection forms were applied. Fasting blood glucose values were obtained from the hospital information system by the education nurse. All older patients with diabetes who met the sampling criteria were assigned to onlinesupervised and unsupervised exercise training groups by block randomisation (1:1). The researchers created a randomisation list on computer software (https://www.randomizer.org/). The assignment of older patients with diabetes to groups was conducted by an independent education nurse who was not involved in the distance education application to be given to both groups, according to the randomisation list. The placement blinding principle was applied, and the researcher applying the therapy learned the randomisation information after the application of the pretest. As both groups were

given the same education, patients were blinded. Also, data analysts were blinded to study groups.

## 2.3  |  The training program

In the first stage of the study, an exercise program suitable for older people with diabetes was prepared by a physiotherapist based on national and international guidelines (Izquierdo et al., 2021; Kushkestani et al., 2022; Mañas et al., 2021). The DISCERN scale was used to evaluate expert opinions on the content of the training. The content of the training program was evaluated by a group of six experts, including two endocrine physicians, two nursing faculty members and two diabetes education nurses. After expert opinions were received, a pilot study was conducted with five different older patients with diabetes who met the sampling criteria to test the comprehensibility and usability of the training program. Patients with diabetes who were included in the first phase of the study were not included in the second phase. The content of the training program included mobility improvement exercises, aerobic exercises, muscle strengthening, balance exercises and flexibility development exercises.

Participants in the online-supervised exercise group were provided with an online-supervised exercise program by using the video conferencing method three times a week for 6 weeks on the Zoom application. The exercise program was carried out synchronously with the help of the expert physiotherapist, who took part in the research, with audio-visual support. Participants did the same exercises together with the physiotherapist. Each session consisted of the following five parts: (1) 10-min warm-up with slow walks, easy movements of progressive intensity and dynamic stretching; (2) 15 min of aerobic exercises at 60%–65% of maximum heart rate with movements involving large muscle groups and carrying the patient's own body in space; (3) 15 min of strength exercises, light weight loads and soft rubber bands targeting specific muscle groups in the lower and upper extremities, using the patient's own resistance; (4) An adaptation of the classical yoga position, sitting upright with the arms at the sides and the heels together and standing on one leg for 30 s–1 min. In addition, walking exercises on a single line; (5) 10 min of general mobility and cool-down with very limited intensity exercises and passive stretching exercises. The patients monitored their heartbeats with a heart rate monitor.

The unsupervised-exercise training group was informed about how to do the same exercises by another researcher, a nurse academician, on the online platform by using the lecturing method. Each training intervention took approximately 30 min.

At the end of the 6th week, the data collection forms were applied to both groups face-to-face again by the training nurse. Current fasting blood glucose values were obtained from the hospital information system by the same training nurse. As the education nurse collected study data and fasting blood glucose values of participants, the researchers were blinded.

## 2.4  |  Data collection tools

## 2.4.1  |  Fasting blood glucose

The fasting blood glucose level was analysed using a hand-held glucometer from a finger blood drop. The test was done by the nurse. Surgical spirit was applied to the fingertip of the fourth toe, allowed to dry and then punctured with a disposable sterile. The first drop of blood was discarded and the second drop was used. The device was held until the screen gave a beep showing the blood glucose measurements in mg/dL and the fasting blood glucose value was recorded.

## 2.4.2  |  Body mass index

Each participant's weight and height was measured to calculate BMI. The BMI categories were defined a priori by using the World Health Organization criteria: underweight ( < 1 8 . 5 \mathrm { k g } / \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } ), normal weight ( 1 8 . 5 - 2 4 . 9 \mathsf { k g } / \mathsf { m } ^ { 2 } ), overweight ( 2 5 . 0 - 2 9 . 9 \mathrm { k g / m ^ { 2 } } ), class 1 obesity ( 3 0 - 3 4 . 9 \mathrm { k g } / \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } ) ., class 2 obesity ( 3 5 - 3 9 . 9 \mathrm { k g } / \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } )) and class 3 obesity (≥40 kg/m2 ) (Verma & Bhat, 2004).

## 2.4.3  |  Personal information form

This form was created by the researchers following a review of the literature (George et al., 2020; Kafkas et al., 2017; Öz & Bahadır, 2009). It consists of six questions about participants' descriptive characteristics and five questions about their disease characteristics.

## 2.4.4  |  The Brief Resilience Scale

The scale was developed by Smith et al. to measure individuals' resilience levels and was adapted to Turkish by Doğan (Doğan et al., 2015; Smith et al., 2008). It is a one-dimensional six-item measurement tool. High scores obtained from the scale indicate that the individual's psychological resilience is good. Half of the items are reversed scored to avoid social desi liability response bias. Items 2, 4 and 6 were reversed in all analyses, as proposed by Smith et al. (2008) to avoid desirability response bias. Cronbach's alpha internal consistency coefficient of the scale is 0.83. In our study, the alpha value of the scale was found to be 0.89.

## 2.4.5  |  Quality of Life Scale in Older People (CASP-19)

The CASP-19 was developed by Hyde et al. (2003) to measure the quality of life of the older people (Hyde et al., 2003). The scale consists of 19 items. Each item of the original scale is scored as ‘never’ (0 points) and ‘always’ (3 points), and items 1, 2, 4, 6, 8 and 9 are reverse coded.

The higher the total score, the higher the quality of life. Cronbach's alpha values for each sub-dimension of the original scale were found between 0.59 and 0.77. It was adapted to Turkish society in 2014 by Türkoğlu and Adıbelli. In the internal consistency analysis of the scale, the item-total score correlation was found to be between 0.32 and 0.78. Six of the items had a correlation value below 0.30 and were excluded from the scale. The explanatory factor analysis of the scale revealed a two-factor structure (autonomy-satisfaction perception and disability perception), and the factor loads were in an appropriate range. The Cronbach alpha of the scale was 0.91, and the coefficients of the sub-dimensions were 0.93 and 0.75, respectively. Cronbach's alpha value of the original scale is 0.91 (Türkoğlu & Adıbelli, 2014) The alpha value was found as 0.92 in our study.

## 2.5  |  Statistical analysis

The data obtained in the study were analysed by using descriptive statistics (arithmetic mean, median, standard deviation and percentage distributions). Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests were used to check compliance with normal distribution before the inter-group comparison of mean scores was performed. In the comparison of baseline data between the two groups, Student's ttest and Mann–Whitney U test were used for continuous variables, and Fisher exact test and Pearson chi-squared test were used for categorical variables according to the compliance with normal distribution. The mean scores of more than two independent groups were compared by using the ANOVA and Kruskal–Wallis test. The dependent groups t-test was used for repeated measurements of dependent groups when parametric assumptions were met, and the Wilcoxon test was used otherwise. In addition, intention to treat analysis was conducted to include missing data in the study and to prevent bias. The SPSS 21 software package was used in the analysis of data, and a p < .05 level was considered significant. In addition, correlation analysis was performed to test the relationship between the scales according to the follow-up periods.

# 3  |  RESULTS
There was no statistically significant difference between the groups in terms of all descriptive characteristics of the participants (p > .05). This result showed that the participants were assigned to supervised and unsupervised-exercise groups homogeneously (Table 1).

The difference between the mean fasting blood glucose scores of the online-supervised and unsupervised exercise groups was not found to be significant both in the pre-training and post-training periods. However, when the time-dependent intra-group differences were evaluated, a significant improvement was observed in the online-supervised exercise group (p < .05), while no significant relationship was found in the online-unsupervised exercise group (p> .05). This result showed us that the group that was given online-supervised exercise training achieved a more significant

improvement (Table 2). In addition, the difference between the mean BMI scores of the online-supervised and unsupervised exercise groups was found to be significant both in the post-training periods. When the time-dependent intra-group differences were evaluated, a significant improvement was observed in the onlinesupervised exercise group (p < .05) (Table 2).

According to the results of our study, while there was no significant difference between the pre-and post-training scores of the supervised and unsupervised groups on the Psychological Resilience Scale, it was observed that there was a significant difference in both groups according to time (p< .05). In addition, when the difference between the scores of the groups before and after the training was analysed according to time, a significant difference was observed between the online-supervised and unsupervised exercise groups (p < .05).

When the participants' mean scores on the Quality of Life Scale were compared, it was seen that while there was a difference between the groups in terms of their pre-training scores on the perceived disability sub-dimension, there was no difference between their post-training scores. The analysis of time-dependent intragroup differences indicated that there was a significant difference in both groups. When we compared the time-dependent intra-group mean score differences to assess the effectiveness of the application, it was seen that there was a statistically significant difference. When the mean scores on the perceived autonomy and satisfaction sub-dimension of the scale were evaluated, while there was no difference between the groups in terms of their pre-and post-training scores, there were significant intra-group time-dependent differences. Finally, the evaluation of mean scores on the total Quality of Life Scale indicated that there was no significant difference between the groups in terms of their pre-and post-training scores, but that mean scores increased significantly within groups according to time. In addition, when the time-dependent differences in the mean scores were evaluated, it was seen that the increase in the onlinesupervised exercise group was significantly higher (p < .05) (Table 2).

According to the results of the correlation analysis, there was a statistically significant correlation between participants' pre-and post-training mean scores on the total Psychological Resilience Scale and their scores on the total Quality of Life Scale and its subdimensions (p< .05) (Table 3).
