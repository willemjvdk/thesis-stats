# SUBJECTS AND METHODS
## Subjects

Subjects were screened from rural patients who presented to the Department of Endocrinology and Metabolic Diseases, First Hospital of Jilin University, had access to the Internet at home and were able to participate in this randomized and controlled study. Inclusion criteria were type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), diabetes confirmed for more than 1 year and HbA1c 7-10.0%. Exclusion criteria were severe internal diseases, severe diabetic complications, several renal diseases with serum creatinine 4132.6 umol/L (men) or 4123.8 umol/L (women), severe hepatic diseases or elevation of AST or ALT Z 2.5 ULN, failure to take medications as scheduled within the 3 months prior to screening, receiving treatment with insulin pump,

currently participating in or planning to participate in another clinical trial, pregnant women and illiteracy. A total of 212 patients met inclusion criteria in the screening and were randomized into the intervention group (106 patients) and control group (106 patients). All patients included provide consent.

# Methods
Clinical study protocol for this study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Hospital of Jilin University. And the study was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

In the intervention group, a glucometer that could automatically upload blood glucose data via a cable was dispensed to each patient for free. Patients were required to take their blood glucose at least 2 times 2-3 days per week. Data were transmitted to the database by connecting the glucometer to a computer, and information about diet, exercise, blood pressure and body weight was submitted by the patient on the U-Healthcare website. The medical team at the medical center logged onto the website every 2 weeks to analyze the patients’ information including blood glucose, calorie intake and consumption, and to deliver the medical team’s advice (based on 2010 China Guideline for Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus) to the patient by leaving messages on the website or via telephone to prompt and supervise the patients on blood glucose self-monitoring and compliance to a diet and exercise

regimen. Patients who missed data upload for 1 week would be reminded by website message or telephone call. In the control group, patients received free glucometers without any other requirements. This study was conducted for a period of 6 months, during which both groups regularly visited the clinic every 3 months for physical and blood biochemical examination and received follow-up and ambulatory treatment by the same medical team.

## Statistical Analyses

Data were processed with SPSS17.0. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean  SD, and skewed variables were presented as median (quartile range). In intergroup comparisons, data of normal distribution were tested by independent-sample t-test and skewed data were tested by Mann-Whitney U-test. In intragroup comparison, normally distributed variables were tested by paired t-test, and skewed variables were tested by Wilcoxon rank sum test. P < 0 . 0 5indicated a statistically significant difference.

# RESULTS
No statistically significant differences were observed between the intervention group and control group in baseline data including age, diabetes course, physical examination or biochemistry ( P ~ > ~ 0 . 0 5 )except for triglyceride (TG) levels ( P = 0 . 0 0 1 )(Table 1).

At the 3-month follow-up, HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose levels of the intervention group were significantly lower than those at the baseline ( P = 0 . 0 0 0and P = 0 . 0 0 1, respectively) (Figure A and B) as well as those of the control group ( P = 0 . 0 0 3and P = 0 . 0 3 8respectively) (Table 2). TG levels of the intervention group significantly decreased ( P ~ = ~ 0 . 0 0 0 )(Figure C), and the mean change in the levels was also much greater than that of the control group ([from 1.04-0.36%], P = 0 . 0 1 )).

By the 6-month follow-up, HbA1c levels significantly improved compared to those at the baseline in both groups. Although there was no significant difference between HbA1c levels at the 6- and 3-month followups, the levels in the intervention group gradually decreased, whereas an upward trend was observed in

the control group (Figure A). Furthermore, the mean change in HbA1c levels from the baseline in the intervention group was significantly higher than that in the control group ([from 1.27-0.68%], P = 0 . 0 0 4 )(Figure D). Moreover, fasting and 2-hour postprandial plasma glucose levels of the intervention group significantly decreased compared with those at the baseline (P 0.016 and P = 0 . 0 0 0, respectively) (Figure B). Furthermore, these levels were much lower than those of the control group ( P = 0 . 0 4 2and P = 0 . 0 3 4, respectively) (Table 2). Although TG levels in the intervention group did not improve compared to those at the 3-month follow-up, they were significantly lower than those at the baseline (P  0.016) (Figure C).

When the trial ended, 13 (11.8%) and 21 (19%) patients in the intervention and control groups dropped out, respectively. More than 80% patients in the intervention group had been compliant with blood glucose monitoring 2-3 days/week, compliance rate was 72%. At 3-month follow-up, regimens of all patients had not been changed except for 4 patients in the intervention group and 7 in the control group because of their abnormal

blood glucose elevation. No severe hypoglycemic event was reported during the study.
