# 2. Methods
## 2.1. Trial design

A stratified, randomized, assessor-blind, controlled, pragmatic [22], trial with three parallel groups was carried out in accordance with the CONSORT. The trial was registered with clinicaltrials.gov (identifier: NCT03766802).

## 2.2. Participants

The study was conducted at Fatih Sultan Mehmet Education and Research Hospital’s Diabetes and Obesity Center in Istanbul, Turkey. The Center admits people between 30 to 65 years of age and therefore this population was accessible for the current study. People who were diagnosed by an endocrinologist and who possessed a smart phone were invited to participate in the study. Excluded were people who: (i) had participated in an exercise program before, (ii) were unable to use a smart phone or a wearable smartwatch independently, (iii) had a health condition other than T2D associated with disordered glucose metabolism; (v) had a diagnosed cognitive, mental health, neurological, or orthopedic condition.

## 2.3. Study settings

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from Marmara University Clinical Research Ethics Committee, Istanbul, Turkey. All participants were referred to the study by their treating physician and gave written informed consent prior to the first assessment. Assessments were completed at baseline before randomization, and after 12 weeks of intervention. Stratification by age (3 categories) and sex yielded 6 strata and randomization in blocks of 6 was used to assign people to groups [23]. First assessments were completed in April 2018 and follow-ups were concluded in September 2018. All assessments were completed by a physiotherapist blinded to the intervention groups. HbA1c was measured at the Center by a lab technician who was not part of the research team.

# 2.4. Outcome
The conceptual model shown in Supplementary material Fig. S1 was used to structure the outcome measurement strategy. This model shows that the different exercise groups were expected to have a differential effect on exercise behaviours and through exercise to impact muscle function and indicators of physical capacity. Change in muscle function and change in physical capacity (balance and pulmonary function) was theorized to impact functional walking capacity and HbA1c.

The primary outcome was, therefore, glycemic control as measured by HbA1c according to the International Federation for Clinical Chemistry reference protocol [24]. HbA1c measures were taken a week prior to the first intervention session and a week post intervention (end of 12 weeks). A physiotherapist who was not a part of the study team conducted all the assessments. The secondary outcome was functional walking capacity as measured by the Six Minute Walk Test (6MWT) following the European/American Thoracic Societies protocol [25] in a 30-meter

corridor. We also included explanatory outcomes for exercise behaviour, muscle function and physical capacity.

The first set of explanatory variables were those reflecting exercise behaviours. As the outcome measures for these behaviours had overlapping items, single-item indicators were selected from the administered behavioral outcome measures.

Motivation was measured using items from two measures: (i) Physical Activity and Leisure Motivation Scale (PALMS), which consists a total of 34 items covering 11 domains [26]; of these Mastery and Enjoyment domains were represented by single items; (ii) Behavioural Regulations in Exercise Questionnaire- 2 (BREQ-2) which consist a total of 19 items covering four domains [27], of these the Intrinsic and Introjected domains were represented by one item for each. Self-efficacy was measured using one of six items from Exercise Self Efficacy Scale (ESES) [28], measured on a 0–100 visual analogue scale. Similarly, one item from the 22 items of the Exercise Adherence Scale (EARS) was used [29]. Physical activity was measured using one of seven items from the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), representing sitting time [30].

The second set of explanatory variables is theorized to arise from changes in exercise behaviours and affect muscle function and other physical capacity parameters. The variables for muscle function were muscle strength of lower extremities, measured using the maximal isokinetic test of the knees; flexibility, measured using Sit and Reach Test [31]; muscle strength of upper extremities, measured with grip strength using Jamar hand dynamometer [32]; and muscle mass and fat mass percentages, measured by Dualenergy X-ray Absorptiometry (DEXA). In addition, physical capacity outcomes were pulmonary function, measured using Forced Vital Capacity (FVC) and Forced Expiratory Volume (FEV1), and dynamic and static balance, measured using the NeuroCom Balance Master System [33].

## 2.5. Intervention

The intervention under study was an individually-tailored exercise regimen delivered through supervisor or one of the two technologies: mobile app and wearable smartwatch. The first meeting with participants involved a face-to-face session with a blind assessor to conduct assessments. Later, an individual session was scheduled set exercise program following the guidelines from the American College of Sports Medicine and the American Diabetes Association [34]. All participants filled out a questionnaire about their medications, daily activities, and exercise habits. All groups were prescribed exercise training composed of aerobic, strengthening, balance, and stretching exercises to repeat 3 times per week, starting with a 30-minute session building up to a one-hour session for a duration of 12 weeks. The programs were designed by physiotherapists as per the needs of each individual.

The Transtheoretical model [35] was applied to adjust the exercise regimens and updated every 4 weeks. This model consists of 5 stages, and it allows to determine the individual’s behaviour stage. Applying this model to exercise behavior, those who never exercise are considered stage 1; those who intend to start exercising are considered stage 2; those who are in preparation to start exercising are considered stage 3; those who have been exercising for less than 6 months are considered stage 4; and those who have been exercising for more than 6 months are considered stage 5. The individual programs were developed after determining the stages. For example, if someone is in stage 1, information, encouragement and simple exercises were selected, and the program intensity was increased as the person’s stage changed.

## Group I: Supervised exercise group

Exercise sessions were held under the supervision of a physiotherapist in the Center’s gym. Participants were asked to attend

3 sessions per week for 12 weeks for a total of 36 sessions. Each exercise session included a warm-up, aerobic exercises with treadmill or cycling, resistance exercises with elastic band and dumbbell, balance and flexibility exercises, and a cool down. The intensity of aerobic exercise was determined by the target heart rate, measured with an oximeter, according to the Karvonen method [36].

## Technology groups

For the technologies, an online platform was developed to support the delivery, tracking, and adaptation of exercise programs. The details for content development for the online platform are tabulated in Supplementary material Table S1.

## Group II: Mobile app group

A personal account was created on the DIABETEX platform for each participant in this group where they could view their exercise program and check the exercises they completed. Three one-onone training sessions were provided to the participants to orient with the app. The notification feature of the app was used to push exercise reminders, for which participants decided the frequency. The message feature of the app was used to facilitate communication between the participant and physiotherapist as needed. The smart phone had an integrated step counter which tracked realtime engagement in the aerobic component. Extra notifications were pushed when participants did not check in exercises. The progress feature of the app provided input for viewing progress daily, weekly, and monthly.

## Group III: Wearable smartwatch group

Each participant in this group received a smartwatch and the same training as the app group (Group II). Participants accessed their DIABETEX account through the smartwatch and could view their personal exercise program. The exercises were designed for optimal capture of the movement and heart rate detected by the smartwatch sensors. This permitted real-time remote tracking. Push notifications were automatic when the sensors did not detect sufficient elevation of heart rate or accelerations.

## 2.6. Statistical analysis

The three groups were characterized at baseline on all study variables using means and standard deviations or proportions according to their measurement scale. As the primary outcome, change in HbA1c, was continuous, the primary analysis employed linear regression. Given the three groups, two contrasts were made: between the supervised exercise group and the two technology groups combined; and between the two technology groups. Having only two time points (baseline and post-intervention), an analysis of change scores was calculated. Percent Change Index, described by Eduardo et al. [37], was used. The regression parameters were used to indicate change and 95% confidence intervals (CI) on the percent change between the contrasted groups. An intention-totreat analysis was carried out for those receiving at least some intervention. Missing data was dealt with in two ways, last value carried forward and hot deck imputation to eliminate the risk of bias due to missing data [38].

Additionally, an absolute reduction of 0.5% (mmol/mol) in HbA1c is considered as a Minimal Important Change (MIC) [39], Therefore, a binary outcome was created and the groups were contrasted for MIC using logistic regression with missing data excluded. The same analyses were conducted for 6MWT using the MIC 27.27 m [40].

As the explanatory outcomes (muscle function, physical capacity, and exercise behaviour) were all theoretically related within their own domains, the analysis dealt with them as a group using Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) [41] that estimated the extent to which group was associated with greater gains. Data were analysed using SAS/STAT® software version 9.4.

## 2.7. Sample size

The study was designed originally with 80% power (alpha 0.05) to detect a reduction of 0.5% in HbA1c in each of the three groups, which is the minimally important clinical value in HbA1c. Based on ½ standard deviation of HbA1c from a previous study [mean HbA1c:8.0% (SD:2.3%)] [42], a sample of size n = 25 would produce 95% confidence interval between 0.11 and 0.89 which excludes the null value of 0. We therefore targeted a total sample of 90 (30 per group) to account for uncertainty in our sample size estimation and incomplete data.

Table 2 presents, for each group, the results for HbA1c. Presented are: baseline and follow-up values as well as the difference, percent change and the proportion of people achieving a clinically meaningful difference using different imputation methods. With regards to associated explanatory outcomes, GEE analyses showed that there were no differences between groups in either contrast. For example, there were no differences between groups in behavioral outcomes (supervised vs technology; OR:0.65 (95% CI: 0.40–1.04) and mobile app vs smartwatch; OR:1.06 (95% CI: 0.05 0.58)). Changes in behavioral outcomes are also presented

in Fig. 2. The values are the number of people making a change on each of the components either in the positive direction (gains) or in the negative direction (losses). For example, for mastery, more people gained (18) than lost (5), for enjoyment the gains and losses were similar; for sitting time, the majority made gains.
