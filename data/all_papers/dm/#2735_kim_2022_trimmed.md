# Methods
## Study Design

A randomized controlled trial was performed to determine the effectiveness of an integrated diabetes self-management program using a smartphone app based on the IMB model for patients with type 2 diabetes.

## Sample and Setting

The sample comprised outpatients being treated for type 2 diabetes in the endocrinology department of a university hospital in a South Korean city. The researcher recruited participants by displaying recruitment notices and banners in the endocrinology outpatient department. The inclusion criteria were those aged 19 years and older, with HbA1c over 6.5%, taking diabetes or insulin medication for more than six months, able to walk, can communicate in and read Korean, were using the smartphone app with data input at least once a week, and those using the phone operating systems Android 4.3 or iOS 5.1 or higher.

The G-Power Analysis software program (G-power 3.1.9) was used to calculate the number of participants required for this study. In a previous study that examined the effectiveness of a self-care program among patients with type 2 diabetes (Lee et al., 2017), the effect size of self-care behavior was 0.7. Considering these findings, the present study utilized an independent t-test and calculated the number of participants based on an effect size of 0.7, a significance level of .05, and a statistical power of .80; the number of participants required for each of the control and experimental groups was 34.

Recruitment took place from March 20 to July 20, 2019. A total of 4,160 patients visited the university hospital’s outpatient endocrinology department during that time, and 2,404 of those patients met the study criteria. However, 2,332 people were excluded due to personal circumstances, with reasons such as not being motivated to participate in the research, difficulty in participating due to work, not wanting to use the app, or difficulty in completing the survey. In total, 72 patients were numbered in the order in which they participated in the study and signed the consent form. Patients were informed about consent for the two trials, but were unaware which group they would be assigned to until the study began. They were randomized into the

experimental or control groups using a random number generator by a research assistant who did not participate in the experimental treatment, data collection, and analysis. Thirty-six participants were randomly assigned to each group. The data of three participants were excluded from the analysis because they did not provide records of self-management behaviors such as diet, blood sugar, and medication in the app for longer than one week, consecutively, during the eight-week intervention period; one participant dropped out due to personal circumstances. Final analysis included data from 68 participants—32 participants in the experimental group and 36 in the control group.

## Measures

In this study, primary and secondary outcomes were measured. The primary outcome was measured by physiological indicators (i.e., HbA1c and BMI), and fasting blood sugar levels and health behaviors (i.e., daily step count and diet) as recorded with a smartphone app, “Doctor Diary.” The secondary outcome was measured by self-report questionnaires (i.e., diabetes self-management information, motivation, behavioral skills, and behaviors). Fasting blood sugar levels and health behaviors were measured only for the experimental group. These factors were not measured in the control group because they did not use smartphone apps.

## Physiological indicators

HbA1c. HbA1c was measured using the HbA1c measurement tool (CareU™ Analyzer 100, Medi-sensor, Deagu, Korea). The coefficient of variation (CV) of this machine is less than 3% and the reliability (r) is greater than 0.98.

Body mass index. The researchers utilized an automatic measurement tool to ascertain participants’ height and weight, which also automatically calculated their BMI (GL-150P, G-tech international Co., Ltd. Gyeonggi-do, Korea). The weight measurement accuracy of the machine is 1/3,000 (50g), and the height measurement accuracy is 1mm.

Fasting blood sugar levels and health behaviors recorded by the experimental group with the smartphone app

Fasting blood sugar. Fasting blood sugar refers to the value (mg/dL) recorded in the smartphone app after measuring blood sugar using a self-glucose meter (Care U SMART Blood glucose meter, medi-sensor, Daegu, Korea) in the morning before eating. The accuracy of this meter is 99.7%.

Daily step count. Daily step count refers to the number of steps per day automatically calculated and recorded in the app when the participants walked with their smartphones.

Diet. Diet refers to the total energy intake (kcal/day) and the amount of carbohydrates, proteins, and fat intake (g/day) per day. After each meal and snack, participants recorded the

amount of food they had eaten on the smartphone app. The records were saved and the researcher calculated their intake amounts utilizing CAN pro 5.0 (Computer Aided Nutritional Analysis Program).

Self-report questionnaire. All scales were used with permission from the original developers and translators of the scales.

Diabetes self-management information. Knowledge about diabetes self-management was measured using the Brief Diabetes Knowledge Test developed by the Michigan Diabetes Research Center (Fitzgerald et al., 1998) and translated by Choi (2012). It measures general knowledge (14 questions) and insulin-related knowledge (9 questions), with a total of 23 multiple-choice questions rated either 1=correct or 0=incorrect.

Diabetes self-management personal motivation. Personal motivation for diabetes self-management was measured using the third version of the Diabetes Attitude Scale (DAS-3) developed by the Michigan Diabetes Research Center (Anderson et al., 1998) and translated by Choi (2012). DAS-3 is composed of 33 questions, including on the need for special training in education (five questions), seriousness of type 2 diabetes (seven questions), the overall value of tight glucose control in diabetes care (seven questions), attitude toward patients’ autonomy (eight questions), and psychological impact of diabetes on patients (six questions). Only nine of these questions were used in this study from the following two domains: the value of tight glucose control (four questions) and patient autonomy (five questions) in diabetes selfmanagement. Answers are measured on a five-point Likert scale. In this study, the Cronbach’s αs were .65 and .86.

Diabetes self-management social motivation. Social motivation for diabetes self-management was measured using the Diabetes Family Behavior Checklist-2 developed by Glasgow and Toobert (1988) and translated by Choi and Rankin (2009). This checklist is comprised of 16 questions from the domains of positive support (nine questions) and negative support (seven questions) for diabetes selfmanagement behaviors. In this study, we measured social motivations that have a positive effect on diabetes selfmanagement behaviors. It excluded seven questions that measure negative support, such as arguing, and used five questions that measure positive support for the participant’s self-management behaviors; four questions that could not be supported by the smartphone app were also excluded. Answers are measured on a five-point Likert scale. The Cronbach’s α in this study was .86.

Diabetes self-management behavior skills. Behavior skills relating to diabetes self-management were measured using the Diabetes Self-Efficacy Scale developed in the Stanford

Center for Patient Education (Lorig et al., 2009) and translated by Byun (2016). This scale is comprised of eight questions with answers measured a yem-point Likert scale. The Cronbach’s α in this study was .84.

Diabetes self-management behaviors. Diabetes selfmanagement behaviors were measured using the Revised Summary of Diabetes Self-Care Activities Measure Scale developed by Toobert and Glasgow (1994), and verified for reliability and validity in its Korean version by Chang and Song (2009). The scale is comprised of 25 questions. This study excluded questions that were not scored in multiple responses and that related to insulin injections. Sixteen questions were ultimately utilized, related to diet (five questions), exercise (two questions), medication (two questions), blood glucose testing (two questions), and foot management (five questions). The scale measures the number of days that diabetes self-management was completed in the past week on an eight-point Likert scale. In this study, the Cronbach’s α was .84.

## Intervention

An integrated diabetes self-management program using a smartphone app based on the IMB model. The present study’s intervention was developed to increase knowledge about diabetes self-management via face-to-face education and to improve self-management motivation and behavior skills via a diabetes self-management smartphone app and phone counseling (Figure 1). After pre-test measures, the experimental group received the integrated diabetes self-management program developed in this study for eight weeks, and the control group received a book entitled Diabetes Management Guide published by the Korea Association of Diabetes Nursing Education. This book is generally provided to patients with diabetes in this clinic, and was only given to the control group.

Strategies for improving diabetes self-management knowledge. Diabetes self-management face-to-face education and education about using the diabetes self-management smartphone app were provided to this study’s participants. The preliminary education contents were determined through a literature review, and the content feasibility was verified and modified. The education content was constructed based on 40 domestic diabetes research papers and guidelines, as well as educational materials published by societies and professional organizations between 2010 and 2019. To improve the content validity of the diabetes self-management education, this study engaged in content validity testing by expert groups, each comprising five individuals: two endocrinologists, one diabetes nurse educator, one diabetes education nutritionist, and one diabetes education pharmacist. The questionnaire for validity testing included 16 questions measured on a four-point Likert scale regarding

the appropriateness of the education provision’s structure, content, effectiveness, and applicability ranging from 1=“not very valid” to 4=“very valid.” Validity verification showed that all questions scored at least 3.0 points.

The researchers formulated education content based on the objectives of face-to-face diabetes self-management education derived from a review of the literature and expert validity verification; these included orientation, blood sugar control, exercise, medication compliance, hypoglycemia management, complications and foot care, and stress management. We tried to improve participants’ understanding by actively utilizing visual data such as photos to depict diabetic diets or foods, along with pictorial representations of the nutrients in each food. To facilitate the education process, diabetes and self-management education booklets were formulated and distributed, and PowerPoint materials were formulated and used for face-to-face education with small groups of two—five participants for 60 minutes in one session.

Pilot tests were conducted to ensure that the participants understood the education content well. Four people with diabetes were each provided with one face-to-face education session using the booklet formulated by the researcher. Minor modifications were made based on the feedback from the pilot.

The participants were educated about how to use the diabetes self-management smartphone app. Education for using the smartphone app was provided for 30 minutes directly following the diabetes self-management face-to-face education. The researcher and two research assistants were paired with one or two participants, who practiced with their smartphones to record their blood sugar levels, meals, and medications (Figure 1).

Strategies for enhancing motivation and behavioral skills for diabetes self-management

Application of the diabetes self-management smartphone app. A total of 20 diabetes self-management smartphone apps were found when screening for Korean language support, availability on both Android and iOS, and free-to-use access. The app “Doctor Diary” was selected as a tool to strengthen motivation and improve behavioral skills in everyday life. In Chomutare and colleagues’ (2011) analysis of 137 diabetes self-management apps, they argued that the functions of smartphone apps that improve diabetes self-management include recording of self-management behaviors and feedback, customized recommendations and education, goal-setting, social media sharing, communication with healthcare staff, and wireless connection with blood sugar devices.

The Doctor Diary app includes most of the aforementioned functions, and the overall functionality of the app is as follows: log in using an email address; enter personal information, health information, and self-management information; add targets for pre- and post-meal blood sugar levels, HbA1c, and daily steps; self-measurement of patient’s blood

sugar levels, diet and snacks, medication or insulin, and exercise using text and photos; check all meals, exercises, and medication entered in the app on the main screen; check the blood glucose graph; check the average number of blood glucose inputs per day, along with low and high blood sugar levels; check the amount and calories of carbohydrates, fat, and sugar in the diet entered; check calories consumed by the exercise entered; analyze the blood sugar levels entered to show expected HbA1c levels; measure daily steps; communicate with medical staff or other people with diabetes using the chat window of the app; and reminders of the patient’s meal time, exercise time, blood glucose measurement time, and medication time.

The app was developed by Doctor Diary, Inc. in November 2016 to provide blood sugar management for people with diabetes. A server was developed exclusively for the management of this study’s participants.1 The data on the Doctor Diary was confidential.

The experimental group set diabetes self-management goals using Doctor Diary and engaged in self-management behaviors such as self-measuring blood sugar, taking medication, following a diabetic diet, and exercising. Participants recorded their daily self-management behaviors manually, and their daily step counts were transferred automatically to the smartphone app. Chat and community functions enabled communication between participants and researchers.

Using the management server of Doctor Diary, the researcher monitored the self-measured blood sugar levels, medications, diets, and daily step counts on a daily basis to check whether the self-management behaviors were being completed and recorded properly. The app’s chat function was used by participants to provide feedback three times per week regarding any inadequacies in app utilization, blood sugar levels, medication, diet, or daily step count (Figure 1).

Phone counseling. Phone counseling was conducted weekly, for ten minutes per session, for a total of eight sessions. The sessions were designed to resolve difficulties arising with self-management behavior and to potentially improve motivation and behavioral skills. Availability for phone calls was confirmed during pre-test interviews, and general and disease-related characteristics were recorded in interview notes, which were utilized during phone counseling. The researcher composed a manual to counsel common areas of concern such as high or low blood sugar levels, blood sugar testing, medication, diet, and exercise management.

Prior to conducting the phone counseling, the researcher reviewed the self-measured blood sugar levels daily, the diet, and the daily step count for each participant as recorded on the app, and decided in which areas to provide counseling. The researcher then called the participant at an agreed time, and applied communication skills such as asking open-ended questions, sympathizing, reflecting, and summarizing. The researcher also inquired how the participants and their

self-management behavior had been in the past week and provided counseling in accordance with the manuals (Figure 1).

## Procedures

This study took place from March 2 to September 20, 2019, and was conducted by the researcher and four research assistants. The researchers provided intervention, one research assistant made random assignments using random number generators, two assisted with data collection and smartphone app user training, and the fourth performed data coding and analysis after data collection. The two research assistants who helped in data collection were outpatient endocrinology nurses, each with two or more years of clinical experience in endocrinology. The researcher educated them about the study’s contents and objectives, the composition of the questionnaire, and the app usage. The participants’ blinds were conducted by different researchers who performed the assignment, intervention, and data analysis. Due to the nature of the intervention study, the participants’ blinds could not be performed while the intervention was being provided. To prevent cross-contamination between the experimental and control groups, the researchers conducted pre- and post-tests on different days in the hospital’s diabetes education room.

The study process was explained to participants before the questionnaire was distributed, and physiological indicators related to diabetes were measured after participants completed the questionnaires. After measuring the physiological indicators, the experimental group received face-toface education, and the control group returned home with the Diabetes Management Guide published by the Korean Diabetes Nursing Association. The booklet included information on diabetes, diagnosis and symptoms, chronic complications management, diabetes management goals, and diabetes management (meal therapy, exercise therapy, medication, low blood sugar, self-blood glucose measurement, and foot care).

The researcher organized the experimental group into groups of two to five and conducted diabetic self-management education in the diabetes education room of the hospital for 60 minutes. A PowerPoint presentation and booklets were used to facilitate the process. Immediately after the diabetes self-management education, the researcher provided a 30-minute training on how to use the diabetes self-management smartphone app. The researchers and two research assistants installed the “Doctor Diary” smartphone app on the experimental group’s smartphones and practiced setting goals, alarms, entering self-management behaviors, and using the chat function of the app.

For eight weeks after providing education, the experimental group recorded their data (blood sugar, diet, medication, and daily steps) on the smartphone app after each diabetic self-management behavior performed in real life, and the researcher checked and analyzed the data daily on the web server; additionally, the researcher provided feedback and

counseling three times a week through the app’s chat function, identified the diabetic self-management behaviors entered by the experimental group in the smartphone app for a week, and provided eight weekly telephone consultations regarding behaviors that were poorly performed.

Post-test sessions were conducted eight weeks after the pre-tests in the diabetes education room; diabetes-related physiological indicators were measured again after the questionnaire completion. After the post-test, we provided handouts on how to use the diabetic self-management smartphone app to the participants in the control group, and practiced installing the smartphone app, setting goals and alarms, entering self-management behaviors, and using the app’s chat function.

## Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the * University Institutional Ethics Committee (IRB No. 2019_06_HR). Participants were informed about the study objective and process, the management of their data, the voluntary nature of participation, and their right to withdraw. Written consent forms were submitted for participation in the study. The participants were also informed that further visits were necessary for the pre-test and post-test questionnaires. All study participants received blood sugar measurement tools, strips, blood draw needles, and alcohol wipes at the pre-test time point, and transportation costs were provided for the pre-test and posttest sessions. After the post-test sessions, the control group participants were given instructional materials for the diabetes self-management smartphone app.

## Analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS/WIN 25.0. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to test the normality of the data for the study variables of both groups; the self-management social motivation of the control group did not satisfy normal distribution, and thus non-parametric statistics were used. The experimental and control groups were tested for pre-test homogeneity using a χ ² test, Fisher’s exact test, and the Mann-Whitney U-test. To identify the effects of the intervention, an independent t-test and the Mann-Whitney U-test were used. In addition, the pre- and post-test differences in the experimental group were analyzed using paired t-tests.

# Results

(54.4%). In terms of income, 23 participants (33.8%) earned less than US$900 per month, while 22 participants (32.4%) earned more than US$2,700.
