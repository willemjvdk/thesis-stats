# Methods
## Study design

The study was a randomised controlled trial with two interventions arms: MHE and THE and one control group (C). The study was conducted in the North Western Thakurgaon district of Bangladesh, 324 km away from the capital Dhaka. According to the administrative layer, both urban and rural distributions are available in Thakurgaon district. The majority (76.4%) of the population is Muslim. This district is mainly agriculture-based and community’s distance to the capital city causes difficulties in getting sufficient technical and logistical support [21].

The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethical Commission of the Heidelberg University Hospital, Germany (s-328/2015) and the Ethical Review Committee, Bangladesh University of Health Sciences, Dhaka, Bangladesh (BUHS/ ERC/EC/15/018).

## Participants

The study took place over a period of 18 months from January 2016 to June 2017. The activities included a baseline survey, an intervention phase of 12 months and an endline survey. The study was coordinated by the Heidelberg Institute of Global Health, Heidelberg University Hospital, Germany and the Bangladesh University of Health Sciences affiliated with Diabetic Association of Bangladesh (BADAS). In this study, diabetic patients registered under Thakurgaon Swasthoseba Hospital (TSH), managed by the Thakurgaon Diabetic Association (an affiliation of BADAS), were considered as study population. Study participants were included according to the following inclusion criteria: (i) diagnosed as diabetic patient according to World Health Organization (WHO) criteria (cut off values for fasting ≥ 7.0 mmol/L and for 2 hours fasting plasma glucose >= 11.1 mmol/ L); (ii) registered in the TSH and residing in the Thakurgaon district; (iii) aged between 18 to 64 years,(iv) willingness to participate in the study, (v) informed consent form was signed. Exclusion criteria were: Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM) and mental or physical disability.

## Randomization

A total of 990 participants, 330 in each group, were recruited for the study. The sample size was calculated using the formula from Lwanga and Lemeshow [22] to detect a difference of 10% in the study outcome, given a SD of 3.75% between the MHE and THE intervention groups at the end of 12 months, with an expected 10% drop rate, with a=0.05 and b=0.20 (80% power).

At the beginning of the project 5,648 diabetic patients registered at TSH were found eligible for this study. Two upazilas (administrative subdivision of Thakurgaon district) were randomly allocated to each group (MHE, THE and C) to avoid contamination between the groups (Fig. 1). Patients were randomly selected from the patients’ registers: 165 patients were selected in each upazila using a systematic approach with an interval of six between two patients. After randomization, patients were contacted to collect their informed consent. Baseline data was collected from all the participants [23]. During the follow-up period, 86 participants were lost to follow-up: 19 died and 67 refused to continue their participation.

## Procedures

Baseline and endline information were gathered by face-to-face home-interview method, using a pre-tested and semi-structured questionnaire. The advices from health care providers, diagnosis report and blood glucose measurements were collected from

patient’s diabetic guide book. Anthropometric and clinical measurements (weight, height, waist circumferences and blood pressure) were directly made by interviewer.

Individuals from the three groups received an individual one-day educational session. The educational material entitled “Diabetes selfmanagement by patients” developed by the survey team, was written in Bengali language, based essentially on pictures. Each of the seven components of diabetes self- management was represented with one different color. Log sheet allowed patients to record their daily practices. There was a separate sheet for each month where patients could tick mark if they were adherent for each of the seven components

After the educational session, two different types of reminder and monitoring methods were applied. In the MHE group patients received one voice calling reminders per month, based on their needs, over a period of 11 months. In the THE group patients received

a home-visit by health assistants every month, over a period of 11 months.

A matrix sheet has been developed for the health assistants in both groups to track the reminders. Attempts were taken to reach patient three times within seven days. If the health assistant could not reach the patient according to the schedule, reasons were noted. Patients could decide anytime to stop their participation.

# Outcomes
The primary outcomes of this study were: (i) basic and technical knowledge on diabetes, (ii) adherence to the drug advices, (iii) adherence to the dietary advices, (iv) adherence to the physical exercise advices, (v) adherence to the follow-up visit advices, (vi) adherence to the blood glucose test advices, (vii) adherence to the

recommendation to stop smoking and (viii) adherence to the foot care advices. The health outcomes measured were: (i) Body Mass Index, (ii) Waist Circumferences, (iii) Blood Pressure, (iv) Blood Glucose. Socio-demographic characteristics, disease and therapeutic information of the participants were considered for the model adjustment.

Knowledge: To measure knowledge of the diabetic patients, scores were developed. For basic knowledge of diabetes were included: definition, types (1, 2 and GDM), normal blood glucose threshold, symptoms (excess urination, excess hunger, excess thirst etc.), general management (seven self-management components), major complications (nephropathy, retinopathy, neuropathy etc.), symptoms and management of hyper- and hypoglycemia. For technical knowledge were included in the score calculation the seven self-management components: drugs (dose, frequency, route, duration, expiry date), proper diet (importance, frequency of meals, food not recommended), regular physical exercise (frequency, duration, safety issues), benefits of attending follow-up visit and doing regular blood glucose test, importance and process of stopping tobacco consumption and advices for regular foot care. For the two scores, each correct response was assigned 1, each incorrect response was assigned 0 and for multiple answers questions the score was divided by the total numbers of correct answers. Afterwards, total score was converted into percentage of correct answers and classified into three categories. Poor knowledge corresponded to a score of (< Mean-1SD); average knowledge corresponded to a score between (Mean § 1SD); and good knowledge corresponded to a score of (> Mean +1SD) [8].

Adherence: Adherence to drug advices was defined as: drug taken according to the prescription including dose, duration, frequency and route (oral or injection form). Dietary adherence was defined as: patient taking at least five meals per day following the recommendation for diabetic patients developed by BADAS. Adherence to physical exercise was defined as weekly physical exercise of at least ≥ 150 min for walking and moderate activities, which is equivalent to 30 min, five days a week. Adherence to follow-up visit and blood glucose test (in Bangladesh, patients are advised to perform the two together) was defined as: patient attending hospital visit within a maximum of seven days after recommendation. Patients not taking any kind of tobacco products (cigarettes, jarda, shadapata, pan masala, gul, etc.) were considered as adherent. For foot care component, patients checking feet, shoes and trimming toenails at least once a week were considered as adherent.

Anthropometry and clinical information: Anthropometric and clinical measurement included patients’ height, weight, waist, hip and blood pressure which were measured at both baseline and endline.

Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kilograms)/ the square of height (meters) [2]. Cut off points for body mass index (BMI) were: < 18.5 underweight; 18.5 − 24.9 normal range; ≥ 25 overweight [24]. Waist circumference (WC) threshold for obesity was: ≥ 90cm for male and ≥ 80cm for female [25]. Threshold for high blood pressure was:>= 120mm Hg for systolic (SBP) and >= 80mm Hg for diastolic (DBP) [26]. The blood glucose (BG) threshold, two hours after breakfast was: > 7 mmol/L [27].

Intervention cost calculation: The cost of both MHE and THE based intervention were calculated considering the total expenditure to conduct the intervention. Mobile phone call charge and salary of one person over a period of 11 months was considered for the MHE based intervention. Salary of six persons including their transportation cost for household visit for a period of 11 months were considered for the THE based intervention. Total cost was divided by the total number of participants for each intervention group.

## Statistical analysis

Data were checked for quality, entered and analyzed using the STATA program (STATA 14.2, 2015; Stata Cooperation, College Station, Texas, USA). Continuous variables were presented as mean  Standard Deviation (SD) or Standard Error (SE) and categorical variables were described as percentage with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Analysis of co-variance (ANCOVA)-Bonferroni was used to compare difference of means between groups and logistic regression was used to compare groups for categorical variables. Statistically significance was considered for p value < 0.05.

# Results
990 participants (32.4% male and 67.6% female) were included in the study. There was no significant difference in the baseline characteristics of the study participants in the different groups as shown in Table 1. A total number of 904 (91.3%) participants completed the study and were included in the final analysis. The mean age of the respondents was 47.7 years; and they had on average 7.9 years of education. The majorities (63.7%) of the respondents were housewives with a mean of three children and four family members in total. The average number of years with the disease was 5.7 years and almost all patients (97.7%) were using clinical therapy for diabetes treatment (Table 1).
