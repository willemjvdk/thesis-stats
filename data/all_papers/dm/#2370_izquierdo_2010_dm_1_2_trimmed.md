# Subjects and Methods
## Participants

Subjects were Medicare beneficiaries (n ¼ 890) enrolled in the upstate New York IDEATel project from January 2001 to January 2005.23,24 Participants were randomized to intervention or usual care within clusters defined by participating primary care providers’ patient panels. Usual care was determined by the primary care providers. Each primary care provider received the American Diabetes Association Standards of Care practice guidelines published each January in Diabetes Care and received laboratory results from annual assessments (hemogloblin A1c, fasting lipid panels, and urine microalbumin:creatinine levels). Inclusion criteria were age 55 years, current Medicare beneficiaries, the diagnosis of diabetes, and domicile in a federally designated medically underserved area. Individuals were excluded if they had moderate or severe cognitive deficits, significant visual impairments, renal failure, or severe comorbid disease as previously described.23,24 The project was approved by the appropriate Institutional Review Boards for the Protection of Human Subjects, and all participants provided written informed consent.

## Intervention

Individuals randomized to the telemedicine intervention were trained to use a home telemedicine unit. A nurse case manager conducted home video visits to help the patient with diabetes self-management techniques and problem solving strategies. Nurse case managers determined the patient’s interest in receiving nutrition counseling with a dietitian within the first few video visits, and those interested were scheduled for a televisit with a dietitian. Those not interested met with the nurse case manager only, but this was rare. After an initial 1-h nutrition assessment and counseling video visit, 30-min

follow-up video visits were scheduled with the dietitian every other month. A Samsung (Seoul, Korea) document camera was used during video visits to review nutrition education materials, discuss home blood glucose patterns, and display food models and sample meal plans. Images of two- or threedimensional objects, using either top or base lighting, were transmitted to the patient’s home telemedicine units when the video source was selected.

Nutrition practice guidelines for medical nutrition therapy were used in the assessment, intervention (nutrition prescription, education, goal setting), and evaluation of outcomes. Video visits with a dietitian were conducted to facilitate behavior change and help formulate and review strategies. Medical nutrition therapy goals included improved glycemic, blood pressure, and lipid control. Weight loss was encouraged, but this was not the primary goal. Nutrition counseling was individualized, and written educational materials including sample meal patterns were either mailed or sent via the project’s secure messaging system, which is similar to e-mail, to patients requesting written information. Attainable behavior change goals (such as eat less carbohydrate, choose low fat foods, eliminate snacks) were set during each video visit to help achieve the desired goals.

## Measures

Data were collected at baseline and annual evaluation visits by personnel not involved in the technical or clinical aspects of the intervention and blinded to randomization status. Weight (platform balance scale, Detecto, Webb City, MO), height (stadiometer, Seca, Birmingham, UK), and WC (Gulick II anthropometric tape, Country Technologies, Inc., Gays Mills, WI) were assessed as previously described.24,25 The diet and exercise measure combined items from validated instruments26–28 (see Appendix). Thirteen items were summed to obtain a total score. Items were prorated to account for missing data. If a participant was missing half or more of the items, the scale score was coded as missing. The Cronbach’s a for the scale was 0.78. Diet and exercise questions were coded such that a high score indicated less self-reported knowledge and abilities to follow best practices related to diet and exercise.

## Statistical analysis

Longitudinal mixed fixed and random effects models were used to examine the direct effects of the intervention on BMI, WC, and self-reported diet and exercise practices and behavior and to examine the influence of gender on the two main outcomes. Repeated-measures mixed-model path analyses were performed to examine the indirect (mediating), longitudinal effects of the intervention on BMI and WC through diet and exercise. SAS (Cary, NC) Proc Mixed was used to adjust for clustering within primary care practices and for heterogeneity in cluster and residual variances. Random effects were used to model the design features of subjects nested within primary care practices. In addition, adjustment of unequal gender group variances was performed. Up to three waves of data (the baseline evaluation visit and 2 years of follow-up) were included in an intent-to-treat analysis, with all patients having at least one wave of data analyzed. A maximum likelihood approach was applied for estimation using mixed models; however, sensitivity analyses were

performed using baseline carried forward in order to examine the potential effects of missing data due to loss to follow-up. In the models predicting BMI and WC, diet and exercise were treated as time-varying covariates. For the analyses of gender, three-way interaction terms were constructed to examine the longitudinal effects of gender on the intervention. Some terms were centered in order to reduce colinearity in the analyses involving interaction terms. The covariance matrix was examined, and the pattern selected was based on best fit, tested using Akaike’s Information Function. The unstructured covariance structure provided the best fit. # Results
At baseline, participants (n ¼ 890) had a mean age of 71.02 years ( \mathrm { S D } = 7 . { \bar { 0 7 } } ) ,, and 57% were female. Mean BMI was 33.31 kg=m2 (SD ¼ 7.05), mean WC was 111.32 cm (SD ¼ 17.56), 91.8% of the participants had a BMI { > } 2 5 \mathrm { k g } / \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 } ., and 65.8% had a BMI 30 kg=m2 . Seventy-two percent of the women and 57% of men had a baseline BMI 30 kg=m2 . There were no significant differences in baseline characteristics between the participants randomized to receive telemedicine (n ¼ 447) or usual care (n ¼ 443).25

Table 1 shows the unadjusted mean and SD values for BMI, WC, and diet and exercise scores over 2 years and the modelbased adjusted means (adjusted for design effects or other features of the data and for covariates shown in Table 2). As shown, the intervention and usual care groups were equivalent at baseline.

In the current analyses, there were 746 respondents with at least one follow-up, and 452 participants did not complete a 2-year follow-up (218 usual care and 235 telemedicine). Of these, 104 participants refused, three families refused, 79 died, 26 were too sick, 22 reported computer problems, 75 did

not specify a reason, and 143 were unreachable for second follow-up. Those lost to the second follow-up were slightly, but significantly, younger (mean of 70.2 vs. 71.8 years) and less educated (mean of 11.4 vs. 12.1 years) and had a longer duration of diabetes (mean of 11.6 vs. 9.7 years); they were also more likely to be women and to live alone (36% vs. 30%).

In adjusted models, the longitudinal direct effect of the intervention on BMI (P ¼ 0.633) and WC (P ¼ 0.602) was not significant. (This result is not shown in a table but is reflected in the path diagrams in Fig. 1.) However, a significant direct, longitudinal effect of the intervention on diet and exercise was

observed (P ¼ 0.002). These results are summarized in the path analysis in Figure 1.

As shown in Tables 1 (adjusted means) and 2 and Figure 2, women in the telemedicine group were significantly more likely than women in the usual care group to decrease WC (P ¼ 0.02) but not BMI over time. Sensitivity analyses using different models for missing data yielded similar, although slightly attenuated, effect sizes. Figure 2 shows the magnitude of the intervention effect. Over the 2-year period, it is estimated that women in the telemedicine group decreased their WC by 1.2 cm, whereas women in the usual care group increased their WC by almost 1 cm. There were no significant

effects for men. Figure 2 examines only the effects of the intervention by gender. Inclusion of diet and exercise into the model results in a larger estimate of decreased WC for the telemedicine women (2 cm).

Path analyses (Fig. 1B) demonstrate a significant longitudinal relationship between diet and exercise and WC ( P < 0 . 0 0 1 ), such that higher diet and exercise ratings, which indicated less knowledge and poor behavior and practices regarding diet and exercise, were associated with higher WC. The main (direct) effect of gender on WC was not significant; however, as discussed earlier, the interaction effect was significant, indicating that women in the telemedicine group as contrasted with women in usual care reduced WC over time.

Results from the analysis of BMI (Fig. 1A) were similar to the analysis for WC, showing a link between the intervention and BMI via improvement in diet and exercise knowledge ( P < 0 . 0 0 1 ). Although the direct effect of the intervention on BMI was not significant, the indirect effects of the intervention through diet and exercise on BMI were significant. Formal testing of mediation effects demonstrated at least partial mediation of the telemedicine intervention through diet and exercise for both BMI (P ¼ 0.004) and WC (P ¼ 0.006).
