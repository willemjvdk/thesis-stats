# 3. Methods
## 3.1. Development of the web-based transitional care program

The web-based transitional care program was designed following a comprehensive literature review and consultation with experts (nurses who specialize in diabetes, endocrinologists, dietitians and rehabilitation therapists). The program consists of five modules: disease self-management (diet, exercise, medicine management, and blood glucose self-monitoring), health education, group interaction, remote counseling, and data collection. A brief description of the web-based transitional care program has been displayed in Table 1.

In the disease self-management module, doctors developed individualized treatment plans for patients to complete their treatment, perform blood glucose monitoring, and develop a healthy lifestyle, under the supervision of specialized nurses. The health education module provided six online courses (a combination of theory and video), including knowledge of diabetes, blood glucose monitoring, treatment, and lifestyle changes. The group interaction module allowed interaction among patients and group competitions. The platform generated a ranking, according to overall performance of patients, and promoted patient self-management through peer support and role models. The remote consultation module was a platform for specialist nurses to provide patients with a 24-hour online consultation service. The data collection module collected relevant data from patients in the intervention group. To protect patient privacy, each patient in the intervention group had an account and password to access the platform, and only relevant information was available to patients and specialized nurses.

Baseline data were collected before patient discharge from hospital, and patients in the intervention group were taught to use the platform before discharge. In the first week after discharge, specialist nurses began to send videos and materials to patients through the platform, completed the content of six health education classes (20 min/class), and supervised patient completion of test questions on the platform. Simultaneously, patients were instructed to record their daily diet (food type and amount), exercise

(time and type), drinking, smoking, and other lifestyle factors, according to their doctor’s advice, as well as their daily blood glucose level and medication use. If the patient had not recorded the above information for more than 2 days, the platform automatically sent a message to remind the patient to record the relevant information. Based on the records, the platform made comprehensive evaluations and generated scores, determined the weekly rankings, and delivered rewards according to these rankings (cake coupons, prepaid cards, etc.). Meanwhile, role models were screened based on compliance with advice and they were encouraged to share their experiences to promote active disease management among other patients.

If a patient had poor treatment compliance, the nurse sent materials about diabetes treatment to the patient through the platform, and informed the patient of the importance of diabetes treatment compliance and the possible health consequences of noncompliance. Nurses further determined and dealt with barriers to compliance. In the following week, the researchers focused on these patients’ everyday performance and provided timely feedback, modifying our health education and advice according to their individual needs while, simultaneously, acknowledging their successes.

## 3.2. Evaluation of the web-based transitional care program

## 3.2.1. Study design and settings

A randomized controlled trial study design was used (registration number: ChiCTR2000035603). A flowchart showing the research design is presented in Fig. 2. This study was conducted in a large regional hospital in Guangzhou, which currently has two nurses who specialize in diabetes and a diabetes education clinic. The hospital has an established transitional care center, which oversees the follow-up of people with chronic diseases and those with post-ostomy and maternal and infant care requirements following hospital discharge. One specialized diabetes nurse and two senior diabetes nurses in the transitional care center were selected to implement the program.

## 3.2.2. Participants

Patients admitted to the Department of Endocrinology of XX Hospital in Guangzhou from June 2017 to June 2018 were invited to participate in the study if they met the study criteria. The inclusion criteria were: (1) patients who met the World Health Organization 1999 diagnostic criteria for diabetes (i.e., fasting blood glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L or 2-h postprandial blood glucose ≥11.0 mmol/L) and had been diagnosed with type 2 diabetes; (2) clear consciousness, good communication, and no visual or auditory impairment; (3) lived in Guangzhou; and (4) volunteered to participate in the research. The exclusion criteria were: (1) patients with serious complications; (2) patients with severe physical disabilities preventing them from taking care of themselves in daily life; (3) patients with malignant tumors; and (4) patients with gestational di-

abetes. After recruitment, the subjects were randomly divided into the intervention and control groups.

## 3.2.3. Sample size estimation

Sample size was calculated based on a completely random design, using the sample size formula for the comparison of the

mean of two independent samples. The trial was designed for analysis using two-tailed tests, with type I and II error rates set at 0.05 and 0.1, respectively. We used the difference in the mean HbA1c (0.99%) between the intervention and control groups, along with the standard deviation (1.30), from a study by Chu (2013) of a transitional care program that aimed to improve glycemic control and

quality of life of participants with type 2 diabetes based on phone and home visits. The study was similar to the current trial in some respects, as both are randomized controlled trials with primary outcomes of glycemic control and quality of life. The calculations indicated that the total sample size required in each group was 31. To account for potential loss during follow-up, we assumed a need for a 50% increase, with each group therefore requiring 47 participants.

## 3.2.4. Intervention

Participants randomly divided into the intervention and control groups. The participants in intervention group received the webbased transitional care. The participants in control group received the usual care including provision of a routine discharge education and a diabetes knowledge manual diabetes knowledge manual before discharge. The manual was designed by our research team, and includes general information on diabetes and risk reduction. Patients in the intervention group also received all the information provided to the controls.

## 3.2.5. Measurement

The outcome indicators included HbA1c, treatment compliance, self-efficacy, and quality of life. Information was primarily obtained from patients and their medical records. All intervention group data were collected using the data collection module of the transitional care platform. Control group data were obtained using Questionnaire Star.

## (1) Demographics information form and HbA1c

Demographic information was provided by intervention group patients through the transitional care platform, including age, sex, religion, marital status, employment status, residence, monthly income, and method of payment of medical expenses. HbA1c levels were obtained from the electronic medical records during patient hospitalization. After discharge, HbA1c levels were measured based on vein blood by an outpatient nurse when each patient returned to the hospital for reexamination; however, for 15 patients, HbA1c levels were measured in the community and registered on the platform at home. The HbA1c target for people with diabetes to aim for is below 48 mmol/mol (6.5%). An HbA1C of 4%−6% indicates satisfactory control of blood glucose, 6%~7% indicates ideal control of blood glucose, 7%~8% indicates unsatisfactory control of blood glucose; above 8% indicates poor glycemic control.

## (2) Chronic Disease Self-Efficacy Scale

This study was based on the Chronic Disease Self-Efficacy Scale developed by Lorig and Holman (2003) and subsequently translated into Chinese by Chow and Wong (2014). The scale includes 6 items. Each item is rated from 1 to 10 and these points are summed to provide a total score. Scores of 8, 4–8, and < 4 are used to categorize self-efficacy as high, medium, and low, respectively. The scale consists of symptom management and general disease management. This scale showed good internal consistency and stability in a previous study of elderly patients with chronic diseases (Cronbach’s \alpha = 0 . 9 6 )(Chow and Wong, 2014). In this study, the wording was adjusted based on patient understanding and expert advice. The revised version was used to conduct a preliminary experiment among 20 outpatients with diabetes. All patients stated that the wording in the scale was clear.

# (3) Medical Outcomes Study 36-item Short-Form Health Survey
The concise health questionnaire was developed by the Boston Health Research Institute (Treanor and Donnelly, 2015). It consists of eight dimensions and 36 items. Two summary measures can be derived from the eight dimensions, namely, the physical component summary and mental component summary. The physical

component summary includes physical functioning, role-physical, body pain, and general health. The mental component summary includes social functioning, mental health, role-emotional, and vitality. The Chinese version of SF-36 has been widely applied in China and has good reliability and validity (Hu et al., 2010).

## (4) Treatment Adherence Scale for Patients with Diabetes

A self-designed treatment compliance scale was used in this study. The scale includes 18 items related to five aspects: medication use (4 items), diet (5 items), exercise (4 items), blood glucose monitoring (3 items), and adverse lifestyle changes (4 items). Response options are 1 (completely unable to do), 2 (can do a few times), 3 (basically able to do), 4 (can do most of the time), and 5 (completely able to do), giving a total score of 18–90, with higher scores indicating better treatment compliance. The scale was evaluated by a group of experts as having a content validity index of 0.95. The results of a preliminary experiment among 30 outpatients with diabetes showed that Cronbach’s α for the scale was 0.862. The patients who participated in the preliminary experiment stated that the language of the scale was clear and easy to understand. The psychometric examination of the scale will be elaborated on in another publication.

## 3.2.6. Data analysis

Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS (version 20.0, Chicago, IL, USA) and the SPSS module Amos 19.0. Student’s t tests and \chi ^ { 2 }tests were conducted to investigate whether the baseline demographic variables were similar between the two groups. They were also used to assess the differences in HbA1c, selfefficacy, treatment compliance, and quality of life between the two groups at baseline and 3 months. A generalized estimating equation was employed to analyze the effects of the web-based transitional care program on self-efficacy, treatment compliance, HbA1c level, and quality of life. Effect size estimates were calculated for all mean differences using Cohen’s d. Amos 19.0 was employed to conduct a path analysis to analyze the mechanism underlying how the intervention influenced glycemic control and quality of life.

## 3.2.7. Procedure and ethical considerations

The research assistants explained the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, and the confidentiality of information, prior to hospital discharge of eligible patients. If patients were willing to participate in the study, they were asked to sign informed consent documents. Number the participants according to the order in which they are recruited into the study (1–116). SPSS was used to generate random allocation sequence, which was kept in 116 opaque envelopes. A research assistant completed the grouping. The envelopes were opened in the order of entering the study, and the grouping of patients was determined according to the allocation scheme in the envelopes. The subjects were randomly divided into the intervention and control groups.

Patients in the intervention group were taught to use the “Internet + "transitional care platform. The researcher set up a personal account for each of these intervention patients on the platform. Baseline data were collected from the intervention group via the platform, while data from the control group were collected using Questionnaire Star. The time taken for patients to complete the questionnaire was 15–20 min. After discharge, the intervention group received 3 months of web-based transitional care, while the control group received only routine discharge education and a diabetes knowledge manual. Data were collected at baseline and 3 months after discharge. Ethical approval was obtained from Chi-

nese Ethics Committee of Registering Clinical Trials (approval number: ChiECRCT20200188).

# 4. Results
## 4.1. Recruitment, attrition, and adherence

Eligible participants (n 131) were identified, 116 of whom agreed to participate in the study. Participants were randomized to the intervention and control groups at a 1:1 ratio. At the end of the study, four cases in the intervention group and six in the control group had withdrawn from the study. No significant differences were found between patients who withdrew from the two groups. No adverse events occurred during the intervention.

## 4.2. Participant characteristics

Baseline characteristics of participants stratified by study group are shown in Table 2. All participants were diagnosed with type 2 diabetes. Average age was 60.85 years, and approximately half of the participants were female ( n = 5 5 , 5 1 . 8 9 \% ) ,with 92.45% married ( n = 9 8 ), and 95.28% living with their spouses or children ( n = 1 0 1 ). More than 80% of participants ( n = 9 2 )were unemployed, and more than 80% (n 91) could take care of themselves. More than 90% (n 96) of participants had medical insurance. No significant differences were observed between the intervention and control groups in terms of demographic characteristics, baseline measurements, or mediating variables.

## 4.3. Effects on glycemic control

The baseline and post-test HbA1c levels in both groups are presented in Table 3. Participants in the intervention group showed a significantly greater improvement in glycemic control compared with the control group ( \beta = 2 . 8 7 ; p < 0 . 0 0 1 ), with an effect size (Cohen’s d) for the difference in HbA1c of 0.25. The results indicate that web-based transitional care can effectively improve glycemic control.

## 4.4. Effects on quality of life

The baseline and post-test quality of life scores in both groups are presented in Table 2. Participants in the intervention group were found to have a significantly greater improvement in quality of life ( \beta = 7 . 6 9 ; p < 0 . 0 0 1 )compared with the control group. The effect size regarding quality of life was estimated to be moderate (Cohen’s d = 0 . 5 2 )). The results indicate that web-based transitional care can effectively improve the quality of life.
