## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# Methods
This RCT was conceived as part of the REgioNs of Europe WorkINg toGether for HEALTH (RENEWING HEALTH) project, an initiative co-founded by the European Commission and 21 partners from nine European regions, where TM was already standard practice for chronic patients’ care.14 RENEWING HEALTH aimed at evaluating TM services for chronic patients using a rigorous assessment approach, called Model for Assessment of Telemedicine (MAST), enrolling about 7,000 patients.15 This RCT was carried out within the Local Health Authority (LHA) of Alto Vicentino, in the Veneto Region, northern Italy, without major changes to the existing organization.

The purpose of this pragmatic, parallel, two-arm, open (unblinded) RCT was to assess whether a TM system for DM patients can improve HRQoL.16 The null hypothesis was that there was no difference between the use or no use of a TM system on DM patients’ HRQoL, as measured by the questionnaire SF 36v2. The follow-up period was 12 months. The trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov with the identifier NCT01569893. The ethics committee of Vicenza LHA approved the study.

## PATIENTS’ SELECTION AND RANDOMIZATION

Patients with a diagnosis of DM type 2 already confirmed and HbA1c > 53 mmol/mol (7.0% according to NGSP) were enrolled in the study. Exclusion criteria included: severe comorbidities with life expectancy <12 months, pregnancy, being cognitively unable to participate, and impossibility or inability to use the devices provided or to complete questionnaires in the native language. Participants were enrolled during planned specialist visits; they were informed of the nature and goals of the study. Patients accepting to be involved in the study were assigned a unique ID. Randomization was carried out through a centralized online system that associated the assigned ID to either the intervention or the control group, following the randomization table generated by PASS 200817–19 in a proportion of 2:1. The number of patients enrolled in the intervention group was the double than that in the control group, because we wanted to study TM also from an organizational viewpoint. The assumption was a moderate effect size of 0.5 on the primary outcome and a dropout rate of 20%, with power >0.95.

## INTERVENTION AND USUAL CARE

The patients randomized to the TM group received telehealth service. Patients were equipped at home with a glucometer and a gateway for data transmission to a Regional eHealth Center (ReHC). According to personalized TM plans devised by a clinician at enrolment, patients measured their blood glucose level. The ReHC processed clinical data automatically to identify possible alarm values, based on thresholds established by specialists

for each patient. ReHC’s operators detected anomalies and informed clinicians when alarms were classified as true. Following the notification by the ReHC’s operator, clinicians checked patients’ data through a Home Care portal and took appropriate action. Irrespective of the alarm, clinicians could access the Home Care portal any time. In case of an emergency, triggered by a critical glucose concentration, the operator, after confirming the alarm, contacted the patient’s relatives and/or the Emergency Department (ED). Participants in the UC group had access to usual care and shared the paper log books of glucometer tests with their general practitioner or specialist during planned visits.

In addition, both the target and the control group received a telecare system for detection of emergency situations at patients’ homes, such as falls. The telecare device allowed patients to contact the ReHC 24/7 through an emergency button. Moreover, operators periodically checked patients’ general status by phone, regardless of the diagnosis and the severity of the condition.

# OUTCOMES
The primary outcome was HRQoL, as measured by the SF-36v2 questionnaire20 at the beginning and at the end of the follow-up period (12 months). The secondary outcomes, measured at the start and at the conclusion of the follow-up period, included HbA1c concentration, outpatient and ER visits or procedures with a diabetologist, hospitalization rates (overall and due to DM), bed days of hospital care (overall and due to DM), and mental status measured through the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS).21

## DATA COLLECTION, EVALUATION, AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Normality of data was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Healthcare resources data were over-dispersed and not normally distributed, so groups were compared using the Mann–Whitney test. Differences between groups are shown as differences of medians and 95% confidence intervals. For rates, differences between groups were characterized by risk ratios with a 95% confidence interval and tested by the Exact Poisson test.

For SF36 and HADS results, a t-test or a Wilcoxon Mann– Whitney test was used to compare groups, depending on whether data were or not normally distributed. Data are displayed by means and standard deviations; however, for not normally distributed variables, data are also expressed in terms of medians and interquartile ranges. Differences between groups and a 95% confidence interval are referred to as means or medians, depending on the test used.

We analyzed data on an intention-to-treat basis. Therefore, if data were available, we included in the analysis both compliant

and noncompliant patients. However, we made no attempt to impute ‘‘missing data’’ from those participants who were lost to follow-up.22 For patients who died, data were available, but obviously the SF 36 questionnaire at the end of the follow-up could not be administered. For patients lost to follow-up, for reasons other than death, clinical data and the SF-36 questionnaire at the end of the follow-up were not available, because they had withdrawn their informed consent to participate in the study. For the intervention group, we excluded from the analysis those patients who had withdrawn before the data collection started. The analysis was carried out using software R, version 3.0.1.

## PATIENTS’ FLOW

In this study, 299 patients were enrolled (208 in the TM group and 91 in the UC group) from March to November 2012. Figure 1 describes the reasons that some patients were not enrolled and others dropped out. Two hundred forty three patients were analyzed for the primary outcome (166 patients in the TM group and 77 in the UC group).

# Results
Mean age was about 73 years, and mean HbA1c was 7.94%. There were no significant differences between the two groups in any of the baseline clinical and demographic characteristics (Tables 1 and 2). About half of the patients in both groups were treated with insulin, with a mean daily dose of 35.76 versus 38.46 IU ( p > 0.05) for the TM and the UC groups, respectively. More than half of the patients suffered from heart conditions.

The results of the study are presented in Table 3. A nonsignificant improvement of the Physical Component Summary (PCS) score was observed in the TM group (0.09; -0.11 to 2.61; p = 0.07). Contrary to expectations, the Mental Component Summary (MCS) score improved in the UC group (-4.49; -5.37 to -1.55, p < 0.001). Statistical significance was detected regarding the following subscales: bodily pain, general health, vitality, social functioning, and mental health. However, this statistical difference, together with other variations within scales and subscales, is not clinically significant.23 In addition, correlation analysis between HbA1c and both PCS and MCS, and between HbA1c and both anxiety and depression were carried out. R2 was 0.01 for the correlation between HbA1c–MCS, HbA1c–anxiety, and HbA1c–depression; R2 was 0.04 for the correlation between HbA1c and PCS.

Regarding the consumption of healthcare resources, a significant reduction in the number of outpatient visits to diabetologists (total and with procedures) ( p < 0.001) was observed in the TM group. Planned hospitalization rates were also statistically lower in the intervention group ( p = 0.02). No other statistically significant differences in the use of healthcare

resources in the two groups were observed ( p > 0.05). The risk of death was higher in patients of the TM group than in those of the UC group, but it did not reach statistical significance ( p = 0.33).
