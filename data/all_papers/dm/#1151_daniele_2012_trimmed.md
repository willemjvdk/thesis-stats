# Materials and Methods
The study recruited adult participants, who were newly diagnosed with type 2 diabetes, referred to the Building Healthy Lifestyles (BHL) program that provides education that teaches both basic knowledge about diabetes and self-care activities, as well as self-management skills and has the ability to monitor individuals’ activities and outcomes with clinicians. This program is delivered through direct dialogue with the patient in face-to-face individual or group education classes at the time of diagnosis. Subsequent interactions were through fate-to-face visits and telephone calls over a 3 to 12-month period. The educational material used in this study was based on the BHL program. All individuals who entered the BHL participated in an initial face-to-face assessment session (approximately 60 to 90 minutes) with a trained clinician. After this initial education session, potential participants were screened to ensure that they had access to a computer connected to the internet, were computer literate, had no other complicating health conditions, and were not involved in another research study.

After informed consent, participants were randomly assigned to 1 of 3 education/follow-up models (Table 1). In the following descriptions, synchronous communication refers to a mode of communication that involves a direct interaction between the 2 individuals involved such as a face-to-face or phone conversation or a electronic chat; whereas asynchronous communication refers to a mode of communication that involves a delay between the exchanges such as seen in an e-mail discussion. The control group, labelled as Controls, used synchronous interactions with providers through direct verbal communication and used an eHealth technology to support (e.g. provided Internet searching function), but not replace, their structured diabetes education and their face-toface learning task. They received paper-based educational materials and normal follow-up care through face-to-face educational meetings/seminars (media) with their provider and/or attended group educational sessions. The second group, labelled Web Static, used asynchronous communication through e-mail for interactions with providers, and used a second type of eHealth technology (additional functional characteristics such as an electronic blood glucose journal; see Table 1) to support their electronic learning task and to access electronic educational materials and tools. They received follow-up care via virtual appointments with providers using e-mail communication (i.e. asynchronous communication). The third group, labelled as Web Interactive, used both asynchronous (e-mail) and synchronous (private and public chats) communication with providers and other patients and used a third type of eHealth technology (electronic blood glucose journal and additional functional characteristics such as bulletin board) to support their electronic learning task and to access electronic

educational materials and tools. They received normal care via virtual appointments with providers using e-mail and private chats. These 2 electronic eHealth systems replaced participants’ structured diabetes education programs. Further, a system was developed to support clinicians’ interaction with their patients (e.g. e-mail, chats, responding to bulletin board questions, posting links to vetted websites, and access to individual patient’s blood glucose journal; see Table 1).

All participants received face-to-face training on the eHealth system they were randomly assigned and were provided with a training manual that matched their group assignment. The research assistants who worked on this project and interacted with subjects were diabetes educators who already worked at the Diabetes Education Center and were responsible for administrating the BHL program. They used standardized scripts during their education sessions with participants and the same educators were responsible for the follow-up of the patients in all 3 groups. At the beginning of the individualized technology training sessions, participants were assigned a unique user name and password for accessing their assigned eHealth system via a web portal and they completed various lab and medication questions. Training sessions were 30 to 60 minutes in duration. In addition, each participant received a study business card and 4 laboratory requisition forms for monitoring biomedical outcomes. Administration of study questionnaires was done through the web portal. Measurements of glycated hemoglobin (A1C) and administration of the questionnaires were done at baseline, 3, 6, 9 and 12 months.

All A1C were measured in provincial laboratories using 6.1% as the upper limit of normal for individuals without diabetes. Diabetes knowledge was measured with the 23-item multiple choice questionnaire developed by Fitzgerald et al (9). Fourteen of the items are for all patients with diabetes and an additional 9 items are for individuals treated with insulin. Scores were reported as percentage of correct answers for each individual. This tool has been found to be reliable with Cronbach’s a>0.7 and valid (9). Selfefficacy was measured with the tool validated by Littlefield et al

(10). A 10-point Likert scale was used for each of the 7 items with lower scores indicating lower sense of self-efficacy. The internal consistency in our sample was 0.75. Self-care behaviours were measured through the diabetes self-care activities tool evaluated by Toobert et al (11). A 5-point Likert scale (1 never, 5 always) was used for each item. For the total score, to be able to correlate with published data, each item was converted to percentage value as follows: 1 0, 2 25, 3 50, 4 75 and 5 100. Satisfaction with the website was assessed with computer satisfaction measure developed and validated by Doll and Torkzadeh (12,13). This is a 12-item instrument measuring 5 areas of end-user satisfaction with computer applications: component, accuracy, format, ease of use and timeliness. Satisfaction with diabetes care was measured by a 25-item (13) service satisfaction scale (14,15). This scale has a reported internal consistency coefficient of 0.89. Diabetes quality of life was assessed by the Diabetes Control and Complications Trial’s Diabetes Quality of Life questionnaire (16). This instrument has been well validated and tested with reported high degrees of internal consistency (Cronbach’s r 0.66e0.92) and excellent testeretest reliability (r¼0.78e0.92). Quality of Health was assessed with a single question on current health rating developed by the authors. Usage of the website was measured by the number of hits for each participant over the 12 months of the study.

This study was approved by the Human Subject Research Committee at the University of Lethbridge and by the Chinook Health Region’s ethic board.

## Data analysis

Descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation and frequency were used to summarize patient characteristics. Initial comparisons between groups were done by analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables, and chi-square test of association ( \chi ^ { 2 } )for categorical variables, with significance levels set at p<0.05. The remaining analyses were conducted using repeated measures ANOVA with an intent-to-treat approach. Group, gender and time (i.e. first study visit, final study visit) were used as factors for each repeated measures ANOVA.

# Results
After informed consent, a total of 79 clients were recruited and randomly assigned to 1 of 3 eHealth technologies. The attrition rate for this longitudinal experiment was 14%, with 11 participants withdrawing from the study. There was no significant difference for age, gender, A1C and living area between those who completed the study and those who dropped out. There tended to be more dropouts in the Web Static group than the other 2 groups (25% for the Web Static group vs. 16% for controls and 3.3% for Web Interactive; \chi ^ { 2 } [ 2 , \mathrm { n } { = } 7 9 ] { = } 5 . 3 5 , \mathrm { p } { = } 0 . 0 6 9 )). Our final sample included 21 participants in the control group, 18 participants to the Web Static treatment group, and 29 participants to the Web Interactive treatment group. Of the 68 participants who volunteered for the experiment, 48 lived in an urban centre, 32 were male and 36 were female. Their ages ranged between 35 to 79 years of age (average age 54.2 years, standard deviation [SD] 9.1). The baseline A1C values were not statistically different across the 3 experimental groups (A1C mean 7.11, SD 1.8; F[2, 65] 0.307, p 0.737). More detail on group characteristics are shown in Table 2. There was also no significant difference between groups for age of patient (F[2, 65] 1.04, p 0.358), or gender of the patient ( \chi ^ { 2 } [ 2 ] { = } 0 . 9 0 , \mathsf { p { = } } 0 . 6 3 8 )at baseline. There was, however, a significant association between group and location patient lived in ( \chi ^ { 2 } ~ [ 2 ] { = } 7 . 0 9 , ~ \mathsf { p { = } } 0 . 0 2 9 ), with 94.4% of the Web Static group living in urban areas, but only 58.6% of the Web Interactive group and 66.7% of the control group living in urban areas.

Results using an intent-to-treat approach showed no overall significant differences between the 3 groups in A1C at the end of the study (Fig. 1). However, a trend for a group by time by gender interaction for change in A1C from baseline to the final visit was found (F[2, 62]¼2.76, p¼0.071). Males in the Web Interactive group and the Web Static group tended to drop in A1C by the final visit, but males in the control group showed a slight increase in A1C from the first to final visit (F[2, 29] 3.05, p 0.063). For females only, no significant effects were found (see Table 3).

As shown in Figure 2, all groups showed a significant increase in knowledge scores: (F[1, 62]¼8.68, p¼0.005) with a trend for group differences (F[2, 62]¼2.62, p¼0.081) with a higher knowledge score in the Web Static group than control group; but no significant interactions or gender differences. Better total diabetes self-efficacy (F[1, 62]¼5.76, p¼0.019), and better self care (F[1, 62]¼8.16, p¼0.006) was also observed over time for the overall sample. No significant group or gender differences and no significant interactions emerged for these 2 last variables. Quality of life did not change significantly over time, and no significant group or gender differences, and no significant interactions emerged (Table 3).

As expected by our study design, total computer website use was zero at baseline for all groups, but by the final visit, there was a significant group difference with the control group having significantly lower total use than the Web Interactive group (Web Interactive group mean 843.7 hits, SD¼918.9; Web Static group mean¼598.5 hits, SD¼960.8; control group mean¼99.2 hits, SD¼122.6; F[2, 62]¼5.37, p¼0.007). No significant gender effects or interactions emerged for total use. However, a significant group by time by gender interaction was found for total satisfaction with the website (F[2, 62]¼3.71, p¼0.030). Males in the Web Static group and the control group both tended to show increases in satisfaction with the web site, but the Web Interactive group tended to show a decrease over time (F[2, 29]¼2.65, p¼0.088). For females, no significant group or time differences emerged. In contrast, when examining overall satisfaction with diabetes care services, there was a significant group by time by gender interaction (F(2, 62) 3.36, p¼0.041). Males showed no significant effects. Females in the Web Interactive group tended to show an increase in satisfaction by the last visit, whereas females in the other 2 groups showed a slight drop in satisfaction by the last visit (F[2, 33] 2.54, p 0.095. When examining correlation between website usage and our 3 main outcomes, significant correlations were found. A higher total use was associated with a higher diabetes knowledge score (r 0.265, p¼0.029), a higher total diabetes self-efficacy by final study visit (r 0.317, p 0.008), and a lower A1C by final study visit (r 0.271, p¼0.026) by the last visit, regardless of which group subjects were randomized to.
