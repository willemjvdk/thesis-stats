## a b s t r a c t

Aims: Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) can substantially decrease quality of life (QOL). This study examined the effects on QOL-relevant psychosocial measures of a widely available commercial weight loss program enhanced for individuals with T2DM.

Methods: A year-long multi-site randomized clinical trial compared the Weight Watchers (WW) approach, supplemented with phone and email counseling with a certified diabetes educator (CDE), to brief standard diabetes nutrition counseling and education (Standard Care; SC). Participants were 400 women and 163 men (N = 279 WW; 284 SC) with T2DM [mean (±SD) HbA1c 8.32 ± 1%; BMI = 37.1 ± 5.7 kg/m2 ; age = 55.1 ± 9.1 years]. Psychosocial outcomes were assessed at baseline, month 6, and month 12 using a diabetes specific psychosocial measure (Diabetes Distress Scale [DDS]), Impact of Weight on Quality of Life-Lite scale (IWQOL), a generic QOL measure (SF-36), and a depression screen (PHQ-9).

Results: WW participants showed significantly greater improvements than did SC participants on all DDS subscales and total score and on IWQOL total score and physical function, sex life and work domains (all ps b .05). There was no significant treatment effect on SF-36 scores or PHQ-9.

Conclusions: WW enhanced for individuals with T2DM was superior to SC in improving psychosocial outcomes most specific to T2DM and obesity. Available commercial WL programs, combined with scalable complementary program-specific diabetes counseling, may have benefits that extend to diabetes-related distress and weight-relevant QOL.

# 2. Subjects, Materials, and Methods
## 2.1. Research Design

Details of the clinical trial have been described elsewhere.25 Briefly, the trial was a 12-month prospective, randomized, parallelgroups clinical investigation conducted at 16 sites across 13 states. Each site had institutional review board approval. Participants were recruited from a variety of sources (e.g., community, primary care) by site investigators and screened over a 13-month period. All participants were randomized to either a modified WW condition, which was adapted for individuals with T2DM by adding telephone and email consultation with a certified diabetes educator (CDE), or SC group, which consisted of one session of face-to-face T2DM nutritional counseling by a registered dietitian as well as follow-up written information. Participants were randomized in a 1:1 fashion and stratified by study site, gender, and { \mathrm { H b } } { \mathrm { A } } _ { 1 { \mathrm { c } } } .Randomization took place at the week 0 visit. Treatment assignment was not revealed to either study staff or participants until that time. As reported in the main paper,25 analyses conducted to test for failure of randomization yielded no meaningful differences on baseline key clinical measurements between randomized groups. Participants were monitored at 3, 6, 9, and 12-month follow-up visits for primary outcomes (HbA1c, weight change). Psychosocial outcomes (i.e., diabetes distress, health related quality of life, depression) were examined at baseline and at the 6- and 12-month follow-up visits.

## 2.2. Inclusion/Exclusion Criteria

Major inclusion criteria were: participant-reported diagnosis of T2DM; age = 18–70 years; BMI = 27–50 kg/m2 ; HbA1c = 7%–11%; fasting blood glucose b240 mg/dl [13.3 mmol/L]; current diabetes management by a non-study physician; stable regimen of all medications for at least 3 months; and willingness to attend weekly WW meetings in the community and to use WW online tools (if randomized to WW condition). Major exclusion criteria were: type 1 DM; active cardiovascular or coronary heart disease; blood pressure N 160/110 mmHg; weight loss of N5 kg over previous 3 months; severe depression within the previous year based on DSM-IV-TR criteria for a major depressive episode or PHQ-9 Total Score N 15 at screening visit; use of prescription or over-the-counter weight loss medication within four weeks prior to screening; participation in a weight-control program within the prior 3 months; and previous weight loss surgery. A complete list of all inclusion and exclusion criteria has been reported elsewhere.25

## 2.3. Description of Interventions

Participants in the modified WW condition were provided with access to the regular ongoing weekly in-person Weight Watchers meetings in their communities and to standard Weight Watchers online tools. Weight Watchers meeting staff were blind to participants' T2DM status or enrollment in the clinical trial. In two scheduled telephone consultations, an unblinded certified diabetes educator (CDE) spoke with the participant about how to adapt the standard Weight Watchers program to be compatible with a T2DM diet and to enhance glycemic control. Participants also received

weekly e-mails that discussed how that week's meeting topic related to T2DM. Participants had access to unlimited additional phone and email CDE consultations throughout the study.

The SC condition was intended to be representative of the amount of diabetes education that individuals with T2DM commonly receive in the general population.26,27 Participants in the SC condition received one session of in-person T2DM nutritional counseling with a registered dietitian at the baseline visit; additional written materials were provided to them at follow-up visits. They were also given instructions to consume a hypocaloric diet (estimated 500 kcal/day deficit) and nutritional guidance for T2DM control. SC participants who completed the study were given a 1-year post-study membership in the standard Weight Watchers program as well as an initial CDE telephone consultation.

## 2.4. Study Measures

At the baseline, month 6 and month 12 visits, participants completed the following questionnaires using tablets provided at the sites.

## 2.4.1. Diabetes Distress Scale ( D D S ) ^ { 7 }

The DDS is a measure of diabetes-related emotional distress. It consists of 17 items scored on a 1–6 scale, with higher scores indicating higher distress over the past month. The DDS comprises four subscales (emotional burden, physician-related distress, regimen-related distress, and interpersonal distress) and a total score. The DDS has shown good reliability and internal validity across independent samples.28

## 2.4.2. Impact of Weight on Quality of Life-Lite (IWQOL-Lite)29

The IWQOL-Lite is a 31-item, self-report measure of obesity-specific quality of life. The scale consists of five domains (physical functioning, self-esteem, sexual life, public distress and work) and a total score. Domain scores are transformed to a 0–100 scale, in which higher scores indicate higher quality of life. The scale has shown good internal consistency,29,30 test–retest reliability,31 and variation with weight loss32 and degree of obesity.33

## 2.4.3. Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9)34

The PHQ-9 is a short, well-validated, 9-item measure of depressive symptoms over the preceding two weeks based on DSM-IV criteria for major depressive disorder. Scores of 0–4 indicate no depression, 5–9 indicate minimal to mild depression, 10–14 indicate moderate depression, 15–19 indicate moderately severe depression and N20 indicate severe depression. Anyone with a score greater than 15 at baseline assessment were excluded from the study.

# 2.4.4. Medical Outcomes Study-Short Form ( S F  – 3 6 ) ^ { 3 5 }
The SF-36 is a well-validated measure of health status and health related quality of life that measures functioning and well-being in eight basic dimensions (physical functioning, role limitations due to physical factors, role limitations due to emotional factors, vitality, emotional wellbeing, social functioning, pain, and general health) and also provides two norm-based composite T-scores (physical and emotional component summaries). Higher scores on the SF-36 indicate more favorable outcomes (i.e., higher quality of life).

## 2.5. Statistical Analyses

Power analyses for 90% indicated that for power to detect a difference in the primary outcome measure \left( { \mathrm { H b } } { \mathrm { A _ { 1 c } } } \right)with a 2-tailed (0.05) significance level, a between-pairs correlations of p = . 8 ,, 25% attrition and an uncertainty allowance of 10%, a sample of 560 (280 per group) was required. Treatment effects were tested using hierarchical linear modeling, using all randomized participants.

Treatment condition was entered as a level 2 fixed effect and the interaction between treatment condition and time was used for hypothesis testing. Baseline { \mathrm { H b } } { \mathsf { A } } _ { 1 \mathrm { c } } ,, BMI, and gender were entered into the model as random effects. To investigate treatment-associated variations in the relation of \mathrm { H b } \mathsf { A } _ { 1 \mathrm { c } }and weight loss on psychosocial outcomes, HLM models regressed 12-month \mathrm { H B A } _ { 1 \mathrm { c } }change and percent weight loss (% WL) on 12-month changes in total scale scores for the DDS and IWQOL.

## 3.3.2. SF-36

WW participants did not differ significantly from SC participants on changes in SF-36 scores. However, significant time effects indicated improvements for the combined sample on the Physical Functioning, Role Limitations (physical), General Health, and Vitality scale scores and Physical Component Summary score, and a temporary decline at six months on the Emotional Well-being subscale (Table 3).

## 3.3.3. PHQ-9 Total Score

There was no observed treatment effect on PHQ-9 total score ( p = . 7 1 ; \mathrm { T a b l e } \ 3 ). For the combined sample there was a significant effect of time, reflecting a small increase in scores especially at the 6-month follow-up, although all mean scores remained in the non-depressed range.

## 3.3.4. IWQOL-Lite

Overall, participants in the WW group showed significantly greater increases in weight-related quality of life compared to participants in the SC group as measured by IWQOL Total Score \left( p < . 0 1 \right)and the Physical Function \left( p < . 0 0 1 \right), Sex Life \left( p < . 0 0 1 \right)and Work \left( p < . 0 5 \right)subscales. A treatment effect was not observed for the Self-esteem and Public Distress domains (Table 4). Change in IWQOL Total Score is presented in Fig. 1b. As shown in Fig. 1b, mean IWQOL Total scores increased over 12 months by 10.85 (95% CI = 8.65–13.05) in the WW group and by 5.05 (95% CI = 2.85–7.25) in the SC group.

# 3.4. Relations of H b A _ { 1 c }Change and % Weight Loss to Changes in Psychosocial Outcomes
In the overall sample, correlations of \mathrm { H b } \mathsf { A } _ { 1 \mathrm { c } }change with change in DDS total score and IWQOL Total Score were modest but significant ( p s < . 0 5 ; r s = . 1 8and .10, respectively), as were correlations of %WL with change in DDS total score change and IWQOL Total Score ( p < . 0 0 1 ; \ r s = . 2 7and .22, respectively). In HLM analyses, after accounting for the effects of treatment condition, change in \mathrm { H b } \mathsf { A } _ { 1 \mathrm { c } }accounted for 3.2% of the variance in changes in DDS total score, while %WL accounted for 2.1% of the variance ( p s < . 0 0 1 )). For changes in IWQOL Total Score, \mathrm { H b } \mathsf { A } _ { 1 \mathrm { c } }change accounted for 4.2% of the variance while %WL accounted for 3.2% of the variance ( p s < . 0 0 1 ). The interactions of treatment condition with HbA1c change and with %WL did not account for additional variance in either DDS total score or IWQOL Total Score.
