# Methods
## Study design and participants

We undertook a randomized, controlled trial. Patients with type 2 diabetes, aged 60 years or older with a glycated

hemoglobin (HbA1c) level of 7.0–10.5 % (53.0–91.3 mmol/mol) were recruited from the outpatient clinic of the Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (SNUBH) from December, 2013 to January, 2014. Because the use of a mobile device and the Internet use were essential in this study, patients who were unable to use text messages or to access the internet for any reason were excluded in the enrollment.

A total of 121 patients were screened for the study, and 21 were excluded: 15 for exclusion criteria and six who refused to participate in the study after being informed of the study protocol. Finally, 100 patients were enrolled (50 in the u-healthcare and 50 in the SMBG groups). Enrolled patients were randomly assigned in one of the two groups using block randomization: routine care with self-monitored blood glucose (SMBG) group or the u-healthcare system. The SNUBH institutional review board approved the study (B-0807-059-017), and all patients gave their written, informed consent.

## Procedures

For both groups, pertinent diabetes education including a therapeutic lifestyle change program was provided before the random assignment, to standardize each patient’s information level and diabetes management according to the recommendations of the American Diabetes Association (ADA) and the Korean Diabetes Association (kDa) [17, 18].

The diet and exercise counselling was conducted for 1 h at the baseline, 3- and 6-month visits. Individual assessment of dietary habits over 3 days (two weekdays and 1 day on a weekend) was performed, and nutrition education was given to each participant by a dietician. Education about the importance of exercise and how to exercise was given to all participants by an exercise specialist. The participants were encouraged at every visit, without obligation, to flow the diet and exercise recommendations.

Patients in the SMBG group were recommended to measure their blood glucose level at least eight times a week (three or more times fasting, three or more times postprandially, and twice or more at bedtime). The u-healthcare group was educated to use a public switched telephone network (PSTN)-connected glucometer to measure their blood glucose level at the same frequency as the SMBG group. With this device, their glucose levels were transferred to the main server in the u-healthcare center (Fig. 1). Tailored messages automatically generated from the CDSS rule engine were transmitted to their mobile phone immediately. The design of the PSTN-connected glucometer which uses Bluetooth Health Device Profile (HDP) wireless connection is highly user-friendly; for example, (1) the operation buttons on the glucometer are easy to access, (2) the liquid crystal display screen and the font

size are twice the size of that of other commercial glucometers, and (3) its distinctive audio guidance function enables older patients to use it easily. The same glucometers without u-healthcare system were given to the patients in the SMBG group.

For assessing physical activity, a physical activity monitor linked with the u-healthcare system was applied to the u-healthcare participants. This device was invented to measure physical activity by sensing body movements greater than the preset threshold. Participants were educated to carry this device at their waist level all day long except when sleeping at night. Daily physical activity data that were acquired while the patient was carrying the device were automatically transmitted to the main server through the Bluetooth and PSTN network. The activity information from each participant was evaluated based on his or her recommended activity level set by the exercise physiologist, and a tailored message was transmitted to the mobile phone of the participant. The same device without u-healthcare system was given to the patients in the SMBG group.

For dietary feedback, patients were educated and trained to click what they ate on a specific day in the u-healthcare website. In the website, each participant’s favorite food list had been selected by a dietician to help patients easily click on what and how much they ate. The data input by the participants were automatically converted into total caloric intake and carbohydrate, protein, and fat components by CAN Pro 3.0. The results from the CAN Pro software were displayed automatically on the Web site so the participants could find detailed information about their diet pattern

Body composition factors such as whole body fat and android fat, and muscle mass were measured using dualenergy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) (Lunar, GE Medical Systems, Madison, WI, USA) [19].

The primary endpoint of the present study was the proportion of patients achieving the target of \mathrm { H b A } _ { 1 \mathrm { c } } < 7% without hypoglycemia at 6 months. For both groups, the frequency of SMBG was checked to ensure compliance of self-testing. All participants visited the u-healthcare center every 3 months after randomization. During the study period, the dose of sulfonylurea or insulin could be adjusted for safety reasons at the physician’s discretion.

## Devices

## Glucometer and physical activity monitor

A specially designed glucometer for u-healthcare was used in this study (GlucoDr PlusTM, AGM-3000B-Bluetooth HDP version meter and AGM-4000 Strip, Allmedicus,

Seoul, Korea). It was evaluated according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines together with another six glucometers [20]. The within-run, between-run, and between-day coefficients of variation were 0.99–4.98 %. Satisfactory linearity was found for glucose concentrations in the range of 32.5–786.5 mg/dl ( r ^ { 2 } = 0 . 9 9 ). A comparison with the reference laboratory method showed close concordance over the entire range of concentrations evaluated ( r ^ { 2 } = 0 . 9 8 ). No significant effects were noted from added interfering agents, hematocrit, or \mathrm { P a O } _ { 2 }.

The physical activity monitor used in this study was especially designed to monitor activity levels while perceiving the user’s motion and exercise strength. These data are converted automatically into calories (H3 System Co. Ltd., Daejeon, Korea; http://www.h3system.co.kr). This device was validated with 47 healthy young volunteers using a treadmill and connected to a Vmax 229 gas analyzer (SensorMedics, Yorba Linda, CA, USA). Details of the volunteers and the validation results are shown in tables S1 and S2. The activity level is measured in real time based on a three-axis accelerometer, and data are transmitted wirelessly to a home gateway and then to the main server in the u-healthcare center.

## Home gateway system

The home gateway device was specially designed to transfer data from the glucometer and activity monitor to a remote server. When a patient uses the glucometer or physical activity monitor, the tested data were automatically transmitted to the main server at the u-healthcare center through the Bluetooth and the PSTN (Fig. 1). The glucose data were then evaluated by the CDSS rule engine, which was created on the basis of each participant’s hospital information, including the most recent HbA1c level and medication. After the information was processed by the CDSS, tailored messages were generated and sent to each patient’s mobile phone within 1 min.

## CDSS-based U-healthcare service

The u-healthcare service used in this study was based on a CDSS upgraded from a previous system [8]. Participants’ anthropometric parameters, blood pressures, biochemical data, and current medication information were automatically uploaded from the hospital’s electronic medical record (EMR) to the main server in the u-healthcare center (Fig. 1 and figure S1).

In this study, individual information, including glucose value, diet pattern, and daily physical activity, was transferred to the main server to provide an appropriately tailored service. In regard to glucose control, immediately

after data from the patient’s glucometer were sent to the server via PSTN, a specific message containing an interpretation and instructions for each patient was generated automatically by the CDSS rule engine and sent back to the patient. The frequency of messages depended on the number of glucose measurements for each individual. The message was mainly based on the clinical practice recommendations of the ADA and the kDa [17, 18]. For example, if the patient’s fasting glucose level reached 140 mg/dl (7.8 mmol/l), a message was sent to the patient advising an increase in exercise. If a participant’s glucose concentration fell below 50 mg/dl (2.8 mmol/l), a warning message suggesting prompt action to eat or drink a glucose-containing beverage or food was sent to both the participant and a guardian or family member registered at the u-healthcare center. A patent for this CDSS rule engine has been obtained from the Korean Intellectual Property Office (registration number: 1011451420000). Algorithms and actual messages in response to each patient’s daily glucose input are shown in figure S2A.

For long-term control, the CDSS rule engine generated evaluation messages for the average weekly and monthly glucose levels for each patient (figure S2B, C): Messages about weekly average glucose levels were sent at 10 a.m. every Monday, and those for monthly average glucose levels were sent at 11 a.m. on the last day of every month. To ensure good compliance with frequent glucose testing, reminder messages about the total number of glucose measurements during the previous week were also sent at 10 a.m. every Tuesday (figure S3A, B).

The daily amount of exercise was assessed, and the average caloric consumption by exercise for all participants was estimated at baseline. All subjects were instructed to maintain or increase daily activity to more than 30 min at least three times a week. The physical activity data were evaluated by the CDSS based on each participant’s previous activity and their preset recommended exercise level, and were used to generate tailored messages. Daily reports of patients’ physical activity levels including time and calorie expenditure were sent to each patient’s mobile phone at 10 pm every day. To ensure good compliance with the exercise recommendations, reminder messages about the number of data inputs for exercise during the previous week were also sent at 10 a.m. every Wednesday.

## Definition of hypoglycemia

All patients were educated on the symptoms of hypoglycemia in detail. They were advised to perform glucose testing immediately if they felt any hypoglycemic symptoms. In this study, hypoglycemia and severe hypoglycemia were defined as a blood glucose concentration \72 mg/dl (\4 mmol/l) with hypoglycemic symptoms and

\50 mg/dl (\2.78 mmol/l), requiring the assistance of another party, respectively. Nocturnal hypoglycemia was defined as a hypoglycemic event occurring while the patient was asleep.

## The U-healthcare website

For our study, we created a dedicated Web site containing a glucose control section, diet control section, physical activity section, and an integrated widget. Detailed information is shown in figure S4A–4C.

## Statistical analysis

All results are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to confirm the normal distribution of variables. The number of study subjects was decided based on a previous study [8]. Both intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses were carried out for the efficacy analysis and safety profile. Paired t tests were used to compare changes in parameters before and after intervention. Student’s t test and \chi ^ { 2 }test were used to compare differences between groups. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows version 18.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). For all tests, P \ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

# Results
## Baseline characteristics and follow-up of study participants

There were no significant differences in biochemical parameters, including fasting glucose or HbA1c levels, or prescriptions of antidiabetic agents between the groups except height and muscle mass (Table 1). Among 50 patients in each group, 43 (86.0 %) and 42 (84.0 %) were taking C2 antidiabetic medications and 32 (64.0 %) and 33 (66.0 %) were taking C2 antihypertensive medications.

Of those enrolled, 43 (86 %) and 42 (84 %) patients in the u-healthcare and SMBG groups completed the study, respectively. The dropout rates were not different between groups: Two patients in the u-healthcare group and four in the SMBG group withdrew because of the inconvenience of the study; five in the u-healthcare group and four in the SMBG group were withdrawn because their frequency of self-testing for their blood glucose level was less than 80 % than recommended. There was no change in the medications except five patients in the u-healthcare group and one patient in the SMBG group, who were recommended to reduce their sulfonylurea or insulin dose to prevent hypoglycemia.

## Effects of multifactorial intervention

Fasting and postprandial glucose concentrations were significantly decreased in the u-healthcare group, whereas no significant changes were observed in the SMBG group (Table 2). In addition, the body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference measurements were significantly reduced in the u-healthcare group compared with the SMBG group. Lipid profiles, blood pressure, liver function, and microalbuminuria improved in the u-healthcare group but not in the SMBG group. In regard to antidiabetic medication, 11.6 % in the u-healthcare group reduced their dose of oral antidiabetic drug or insulin, whereas there was no change in antidiabetic medication in the SMBG group.

## Achievement of the primary target goal

The mean HbA1c level of the u-healthcare group had decreased significantly at 3-month follow-up (P \ 0.05) but that of the SMBG group did not change (Fig. 2a). The decreased level of HbA1c in the u-healthcare group was maintained for 6 months. The number of patients reaching the target HbA1c level after 6 months of follow-up was significantly higher in the u-healthcare group than the SMBG group in intention-to-treat analysis: 34 versus 16 % (P \ 0.05) (39.5 vs. 19.0 % in per-protocol analysis, P \ 0.05). The proportion of patients who achieved the target HbA1c level \7.0 % (53.0 mmol/mol) without hypoglycemia, the primary endpoint of this study, was significantly higher in the u-healthcare group (26 %) than in the SMBG group (12 %; P \ 0.05; Fig. 2b). Importantly, the proportion of patients whose HbA1c level decreased more than 1 % after 6 months of follow-up was significantly higher in the u-healthcare group than the SMBG group: 58 versus 18 % (P \ 0.05, Fig. 2c) (67.4 vs. 21.4 % in per-protocol analysis, P \ 0.05).

## Hypoglycemia

Before enrollment, the numbers of hypoglycemic events that the patients had experienced were not different between the two groups. During the 6 months of the study, the numbers of patients experiencing hypoglycemia were not different, but those experiencing severe or nocturnal hypoglycemia was more frequent in the SMBG group than in the u-healthcare group (Fig. 2d).

## Obesity index

Over the study period, the BMI and waist circumference decreased significantly in the u-healthcare group, but no significant changes were found in the SMBG group

(Fig. 2e, f). Android fat mass also decreased in the u-healthcare group but not in the SMBG group (Table 2).

## Caloric intake and exercise

The mean caloric intake significantly decreased in both groups, but the decrease was greater in the u-healthcare group than the SMBG group (8.5 vs. 2.4 %; P \ 0.05) (Fig. 2g). The number of exercise episodes per week increased in the u-healthcare group, whereas there was no change in the SMBG group (Fig. 2h).
