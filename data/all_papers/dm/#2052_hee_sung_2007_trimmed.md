# Methods
## Participants

Men and women diagnosed with type 2 diabetes were recruited from the endocrinology outpatient department of a tertiary care hospital located in an urban city of South Korea. Diabetes was diagnosed according to the ADA criteria (The Expert Committee on the Diagnosis and Classification of Diabetes Mellitus 1997). The hospital is a university-affiliated medical center with 800 beds. The data were collected from January 2003 to January 2004. The selection criteria required that participants should be able to perform blood glucose self-testing and -injection of medication, and input to the website (http://www.biodang.com). All enrolled participants in this study had Internet access in their homes and had their own cellular phone. Patients were excluded if they had a clinical history of an important illness, or renal insufficiency with a creatinine level >1Æ5 mg/dl, diabetic complication and had have been using insulin pumps.

A total of 60 patients met the above criteria and agreed to participate. They were randomized by random-permutated block design using a random number table and assigned to one of two groups, either intervention (n ¼ 30) or control (n ¼ 30). Intervention and control groups were then

separated into two groups by baseline HbA1c, a baseline HbA1c ‡7Æ0% and a baseline HbA1c <7Æ0%. Four groups had 15 subjects, respectively. Only 51 subjects completed the entire study, 24 baseline HbA1c <7Æ0% group and 27 baseline HbA1c ‡7Æ0% group. Five subjects did not record their glucose levels for more than four weeks on the web site (http://www.biodang.com). Four subjects were lost before completing the post-test.

## Intervention

The goal of the intervention was to maintain blood glucose level at a normal range. Before the intervention, each patient was instructed about the input of the website for 30 minutes by the researcher. Patients in the intervention group contacted our website and logged in whenever it was convenient for them. The participants could contact our website at any place where Internet access was possible by cellular phone or wire Internet. They then sent their self-monitored blood glucose levels and drug information, including the kinds and dosage of insulin and oral antidiabetic medication that they used for diabetes control. These data were transported to our Internet server system, and displayed on the individual electronic chart in our homepage automatically. Patients were able to see the recommendations from the researcher as well as the laboratory data.

The researcher could view the information on each patient, including the blood glucose levels, medication and details of some events, provided by the patient. In addition to this information, a nurse could also view basic personal history, including historical data, family history, smoking habits, body mass index, blood pressure and baseline laboratory data. After integrating the above information, the nurse sends recommendations back to each patient by short message service of cellular phone and wire Internet weekly. For example, the recommendations included ‘Please, decrease the long-acting insulin by two units’, ‘Please add one tablet of sulfonylurea in evening’, ‘Please check the amount of diet’, ‘Lack of exercise may be the cause of the aggravated glucose level’ or ‘Your glucose control seems to be good’. The researcher provided the intervention for 12 weeks. The 12 weeks of intervention consisted of continuous education and reinforcement of diet, exercise and medication adjustment, as well as frequent self-monitoring of blood glucose levels. The researcher could adjust medications after reviewing the blood glucose log and discussing glucose values with the patients. All medication adjustments were communicated to the subjects’ diabetes doctors. If a patient had not forwarded a blood glucose level for more than one week, a

reminder message was sent. If, despite the reminder messages, individuals had not recorded glucose levels for more than four consecutive weeks, they would have been withdrawn from the intervention group.

Participants in the control group were unaware of any knowledge regarding this web-based nurse’s education system. They met the endocrinologist specialist once or twice during the 12 weeks. As customary in our outpatient clinic for diabetic patients, when the patients in the control group visited our center, they were provided with recommendations about medication, medication dosage and life style modification and so on from the endocrinologist specialist. When the doctor chose to consult for special education or if the patient wished, the nurse or dietitian came to aid with more individualized and detailed information for lifestyle modification.

## Procedure

Before the intervention, demographic variables, HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and two-hour plasma glucose (2-h PG) were measured as pretest data in four groups. HbA1c, FPG and 2-h PG were measured again 12 weeks after baseline in four groups.

Glycosylated haemoglobin was determined by a highperformance liquid chromatography technique using the Variant II (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). An FPG and 2-h PG were analysed by the glucose oxidase method using a Hitachi 7600 (Hitachi, Japan).

## Data analysis

The data were analysed by the SAS (version 8.12, SAS institute, Cary, NC, USA) program. The unpaired t-test was used for comparison of differences between the two groups.

# Results
## Characteristics of sample

The characteristics of the two groups are shown in Table 1. In the pretest, there were no significant differences in the age, gender, body mass index, duration of diabetes, treatment method, HbA1c, FPG, and 2-h PG in the HbA1c ‡7Æ0% and the HbA1c <7Æ0% groups.

## Glycosylated haemoglobin

There was a significant percentage change in a baseline HbA1c <7Æ0% for the control group, with a mean

percentage change of 0Æ43 (6Æ71% pretest to 7Æ14% post-test) (  p = 0 { \cdot } 0 3 4 ). The percentage change for the intervention group was, however, no significant; this time the mean percentage change was -0Æ21 (6Æ92% pretest to 6Æ71% posttest).

There was a significant percentage change in a baseline HbA1c ‡7Æ0% for the intervention group (p ¼ 0Æ007), with a mean percentage change of -2Æ15 (9Æ35% pretest to 7Æ20% post-test). The percentage change in the control group was, however, no significant; this time the mean percentage change was -0Æ22 (8Æ24% pretest to 8Æ02% posttest) (Table 2).

## Fasting plasma glucose

Patients with a baseline HbA1c <7Æ0% in the intervention group had a decrease of -13Æ4 (150Æ7 mg/dl pretest to 137Æ3 mg/dl post-test) in FPG, but no significant (Table 3).

## Two-hour plasma glucose

Patients with a baseline HbA1c <7Æ0% in the intervention group had a decrease of -56Æ0 (215Æ8 mg/dl pretest to 159Æ8 mg/dl post-test) in 2-h PG (p ¼ 0Æ019), but those in control group showed no significant change.

Patients with a baseline HbA1c ‡7Æ0% in the intervention and the control groups had no significant mean change after 16 weeks (Table 4).
