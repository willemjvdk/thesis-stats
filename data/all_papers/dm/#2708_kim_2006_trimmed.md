# 2. Methods
## 2.1. Research design and participants

A control group, pre-/post-test, design was used in this study. Participants were recruited from the endocrinology outpatient department of a tertiary care hospital that was located in an urban city of South Korea. The hospital is a university-affiliated medical centre that has 800 beds. The data were collected from January 2003 to August 2004. Diabetes was diagnosed according to the ADA criteria (The Expert Committee on the Diagnosis and Classification of Diabetes Mellitus, 1997). The selection criteria required that participants should be able to perform blood glucose selftesting and self-injection of medication, access websites and have their own cellular phone. Patients were excluded if they had a clinical history of a severe illness, renal insufficiency with a creatinine level 40.08 mmol/l or had been using insulin pumps. For repeated measures analysis of variance (for an effect size of 0.60, at a power of 0.80 and at an alpha level of 0.05), 25 subjects in each group were required in order to ensure an adequate trial for 1% reduction of \mathrm { H b A _ { 1 } c }levels at post-test compared with pre-test (Machin et al., 1997). A total of 60 patients met the above criteria and agreed to participate. They were randomized by random, permuted block design using a random number table, and assigned to one of two groups: either intervention ( n = 3 0 )or control ( n = 3 0 ). Only 51 subjects completed the entire study: 25 intervention patients and 26 control patients. Five subjects did not record their glucose levels for more than 4 weeks on the website (www.biodang.com) in the intervention group. In addition, four subjects were lost before completing the post-test in the control group: one

moved to another city and three decided to opt out of the programme before completing the post-test.

For ethical considerations, the research protocol was approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of the university hospital. Written consent was obtained from those patients who agreed to participate in the study. Anonymity and confidentiality were guaranteed to participants.

## 2.2. Intervention

The goal of the intervention was to maintain blood glucose levels within a normal range. Before the intervention, each patient was instructed, for 30 min by a researcher, about inputting data into the website. Patients in the group contacted our website and logged in whenever it was convenient for them. Participants in our study could contact our website at any place where Internet access was possible by cellular phone or wired Internet. They then sent their self-monitored blood glucose levels and drug information, including the kinds and dosages of insulin and oral anti-diabetic medication that they used for diabetes control. These data were transported to our Internet server system, and automatically displayed on the individual electronic chart on our homepage. Patients were able to see the recommendations from their nurses and physicians, as well as the laboratory data, on our website.

The researcher could view the information on each patient, including the blood glucose levels, medication and details of some events that were provided by the patient. In addition to this information, a nurse could also view basic personal history, including historical data, family history, smoking habits, body mass index (BMI), blood pressure and baseline laboratory data. After integrating the above information, the nurse sent optimal recommendations back to each patient, weekly, by an SMS by cellular phone or wired Internet. For example, recommendations included: ‘‘Please, decrease the long-acting insulin by two units’’; ‘‘Please add one tablet of sulfonylurea in the evening’’; ‘‘Please check the amount that you eat’’; ‘‘Lack of exercise may be the cause of the aggravated glucose level’’ or ‘‘Your glucose control seems to be good’’. The researcher provided the intervention for 12 weeks. The researcher was a professor at a nursing college. The 12 weeks of intervention consisted of continuous education and reinforcement of diet, exercise, medication adjustment, as well as frequent self-monitoring of blood glucose levels. The researcher could adjust medications after reviewing the blood glucose log and discussing glucose values with the patients. All medication adjustments were communicated to the subjects’ diabetes doctors. If a patient had not forwarded a blood glucose level for more than 1 week, a warning message was sent. If, despite warning messages, individuals had not recorded

glucose levels for more than four consecutive weeks, they were withdrawn from the intervention group.

Participants in the control group met the endocrinologist specialist once or twice during the 12 weeks. As customary in our outpatient clinic for patients with diabetes, when the patients in the control group visited our centre, they were provided with recommendations about medication, medication dosage and lifestyle modification by the endocrinologist specialist. When the doctor chose to consult with the patient to disclose particular information, or if the patient wished, the nurse or dietitian came to aid with more individualized and detailed information relating to lifestyle modification.

## 2.3. Measures

The primary outcomes were { \mathrm { H b A } } _ { 1 } { \mathrm { c } } ,FPG and 2HPMG levels. Before the intervention, demographic variables, { \mathrm { H b A } } _ { 1 } { \mathrm { c } } , FPG and 2HPMG were measured as pre-test data. The { \mathrm { H b A } } _ { 1 } \mathbf { c } ., FPG and 2HPMG levels were measured again 12 weeks later. Patients’ blood was drawn from veins for { \mathrm { H b A } } _ { 1 } { \mathrm { c } } ,FPG and 2HPMG measurements to be obtained. { \mathrm { H b A } } _ { 1 } { \mathrm { c } } _ { }, FPG and 2HPMG levels were measured in a laboratory at the university-affiliated medical centre. \mathrm { H b A _ { 1 } c }levels were determined using a high-performance liquid chromatography technique using Variant II (Bio-Rad, Montreal, Que., Canada). FPG and 2HPMG levels were analysed by the glucose oxidase method using a Hitachi 7600 (Hitachi, Hitachi, Japan).

## 2.4. Data analysis

The data were analysed using the SAS (version 8; SAS Institute, Cary, NC) programme. Chi-square test, t-test and Fisher’s exact test were used to test for the homogeneity of demographic and clinical characteristics between the intervention and control groups. Repeatedmeasures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to determine the difference between groups and over time. When a significant difference was found by repeatedmeasures ANOVA, paired t-tests using the Bonferroni correction were applied to identify specific differences.

# 3. Results
## 3.1. Participants’ characteristics

The characteristics of the intervention and control group are shown in Table 1. The mean age of the control group was 47.5 years and that of the intervention group was 46.8 years. The mean BMI of the control group was 2 3 . 4 \mathrm { k g } / \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 }and that of the intervention group was 2 4 . 5 \mathrm { k g } / \mathrm { m } ^ { 2 }. The mean duration of diabetes was 8.0 years in the control group and 5.2 years in the intervention group. There was no significant difference in age,

gender, BMI, duration of diabetes, treatment method or blood glucose levels between the two groups.

## 3.2. Intervention effects on blood glucose levels

At the pre-test, no significant difference was found in \mathrm { H b A _ { 1 } c }levels between the groups, as shown in Table 1. \mathrm { H b A _ { 1 } c }levels did not differ significantly between the two groups (intervention group versus control group) ( p = 0 . 6 4 4 ), but did differ over time (pre-test versus post-test) ( p = 0 . 0 0 8 ). There was also an interaction between group and time ( p = 0 . 0 0 5 ), as shown in Table 2. There was a significant percentage change in \mathrm { H b A _ { 1 } c }levels for the intervention group ( p < 0 . 0 5 ), with a mean percentage change of -1.15 (8.09% pre-test to 6.94% post-test). The percentage change in the control group was, however, not significant; the mean percentage change was +0.07 (7.59% pre-test to 7.66% post-test).

FPG levels did not differ significantly between the two groups, or over time, and there was no interaction between group and time.

2HPMG levels did not differ significantly between the two groups ( p = 0 . 1 3 5 )or over time ( p = 0 . 0 7 6 ), but there was an interaction between group and time ( p = 0 . 0 0 7 ). There was a significant mean change in 2HPMG levels for the intervention group ( p < 0 . 0 5 ), with a mean change of -4.7 (14.2 mmol/l pre-test to 9.5 mmol/l post-test). The mean change in the control group was not significant; the mean percentage change was +0.8 (12.9 mmol/l pre-test to 13.7 mmol/l post-test).
