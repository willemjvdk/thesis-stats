## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# 2. Materials and methods
## 2.1. Study design and sample size

This study is a three-arm randomized controlled trial. 150 patients with diabetes type 2 were recruited through an outpatient diabetes clinic of the Endocrinology and Metabolism Research Institute affiliated to Tehran University of Medical Sciences. Patients referred to the clinic were screened for eligibility (Fig. 1). The inclusion criteria were as follows: owning a mobile phone, consenting to receive text messages, and ability to read text messages. Patients were excluded if they had a positive clinical history of a serious disease such as renal insufficiency with a creatinine level >1.5 mg/dl, hepatic insufficiency, severe visual impairment due to diabetes complication or psychiatric diseases.

Based on the results of a previous study [21], we expect effect size of at least 0.4 in the HbA1C level between tailored SMS group and the control group. A sample size of n = 48 in each study group would reveal 80% power to detect this difference based on calculations performed with G-Power software and the significance level of the test was targeted at 0.05. Thus, we recruited a total of 50 study participants in each group.

A total of 150 subjects were randomly assigned to the study groups, the randomization was performed by means of permuted block randomization method with a block size of three

including: individually tailored SMS group, non-tailored SMS group, and the control group. The participants were blinded to the other groups, but the investigator was not blinded to the study groups.

Since one of our study goals was to improve patients selfcare and self-efficacy based on social cognitive theory and that according to this theory, planning for behavior change must be individualized [22]. Therefore, one group received messages individually tailored specifically on barriers obtained from diabetes self-care barriers assessment scale for older adults. We hypothesised that patients who receive tailored educational messages are more likely to improve self care skills.

As for ethical considerations, the research protocol was approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of the Tehran University of Medical Sciences. In addition to written informed consent which was obtained from the patients prior to the commencement of the project, a notification SMS was sent to them at the beginning of the project requesting another confirmation.

## 2.2. Measures

Prior to the intervention, the patients’ demographic characteristics were recorded, HbA1c, FBS, Lipid profile assays were carried out and the results were recorded as baseline levels.

Subsequently, Self Care Inventory (SCI) and Diabetes Management Self-Efficacy Scale (DMSES) were used to assess patients’ adherence to diabetes regimens.

It is noteworthy that evidence shows that diabetic patients tend to fail to adhere to their diabetes self-care tasks due to their complexity, being time consuming and involving all aspects of life [23,24]. So in order to identify barriers to diabetes adherence and to minimize their adverse effects on self care programs, Diabetes Self-Care Barriers assessment scale for Older Adults (DSCB-OA) was integrated into the intervention. DSCB-OA is a 15-item scale assessing obstacles to diabetes self-care (diet, exercise, blood glucose monitoring, medications). DSCB-OA scores were computed by summing individual item responses (0 = strongly disagree, 3 = strongly agree). Higher scores reflected greater perceived barriers to self-care [25]. For example, 4 of 15 items were considered about common problems with the use of glucose meter (e.g. checking my blood sugar with glucose meter at home is difficult and annoying for me). If a patient chose strongly agree option, so the messages focused on this barrier. Other items of selfcare barriers scale including problems and concerns related to diet, physical activity and medications were assessed in the same way. In the tailored SMS group, each person received 75% of their messages tailored to the top two barriers to adherence they had reported in their scale. The remaining

25% of messages were randomly selected from the rest of the message pool. In contrast, in the non-tailored SMS group, random messages were sent to each person regardless to the barriers. Both groups were sent an average number of 7 messages per week for 3 months. During these 12 weeks, various aspects of patient-centered educational intervention were reinforcement through the messages, including: diet, exercise, medication adjustment, and frequent self monitoring of blood glucose levels.

## 2.3. Designing the content of the messages

Educational messages were built according to a detailed set of instructions based on the four main areas of diabetes self-care tasks (diet, exercise, blood glucose monitoring, taking medications).

In the field of nutrition, the main focus of the messages were on increasing patients’ awareness of importance of a healthy diet. They included the following: choice of healthier food, food substitution list, recommendations for healthy cooking methods, advantages of each food groups, recommendations for preparations of meals and snacks, eating out, tips for eating healthy at a party, holiday meal planning, healthy fast food meals for diabetics, different ways to eat more vegetable and fruits, healthy eating plate, foods to avoid with diabetes, refraining from overeating and cravings, and as well as several practical suggestions for adherence to a healthy diet. Draft were initially prepared by a nutritionist, and then, they were analyzed and revised several times by all members of the team (general practitioner, diabetes nurse educator, nutritionist, endocrinologist).

Regarding exercise, messages were about increasing patients awareness of health benefits of regular physical activity. These messages included different aspects of diabetes and exercise such as: types of physical activity for diabetes management (aerobic exercise and strength training), seeking physician’s advise before beginning an exercise program, recommended exercise duration and frequency in a day and week, techniques of proper walking and its benefits, foot care tips during exercise, and suggestions for overcoming barriers to physical activity and its integration into daily life. Again, initial draft prepared by a exercise specialist were reviewed by all team members. In regards to blood glucose monitoring, messages included the following information: benefits of using glucose meters and adverse outcomes of not using it, a comprehensive guide to blood glucose meters (choosing, using and caring), frequency and desirable timing for test, tips on efficient use of glucometers and increasing accuracy of measurements.

Messages related to medications were focused on raising patient awareness of medications commonly used for type 2 diabetes, dosing, mechanisms of action, side effects, hypoglycemia they may cause, its symptoms and treatment options, insulin therapy in diabetes type 2, taking medicine based on prescription, adherence to treatment, importance of regular use of the medication, and risks of starting and stopping medications without medical supervision. All team members contributed to preparation of the messages.

It is noteworthy that the content of all messages were encouraging and supportive and intended to persuade

patients to adhere to the prescribed diet and medications, as well as increasing their physical activity level. The messages’ content also provided some practical solutions to specific problems of each individual patient in the tailored SMS group.

# 2.4. Outcome measures
Outcome measurements were clinical measures (HgA1C levels, FBS, Lipid profile and BMI), diabetes self-care via the Self-Care Inventory (SCI) and patients’ self-efficacy via the Diabetes Management Self-Efficacy Scale (DMSES).

The clinical measures were carried out and the all questionnaires were filled once again 12 weeks later.

Self-Care Inventory questionnaire was consisted of 15 items with the main objective of investigating how patients had followed their diabetes treatment plan in the previous 1–2 months. Responses were rated on a five-point scale from “Never = 1 to “Always = 5. Subsequently, results were summed to produce a single score for self-care activities where higher scores indicating superior self-care [26].

Self-efficacy questionnaire was comprised of 20 items that assessed the extent to which respondents were confident in certain fields (e.g., maintain blood glucose level at the acceptable range, medications, diet, and level of physical activity). Responses were ranked on a six-point scale of agreement, with “1” indicating strongly agree to “6” demonstrating strongly disagree”. Responses were summed to create a score for selfefficacy, with lower scores indicating superior self-efficacy [27].

All questionnaires were filled by subjects under supervision of a researcher in order to check whether all the questionnaires are properly filled. Ultimately, total scores of each questionnaire (SCI, DSCB-OA, DMSES) were rescaled to a value of 0 to 100 for all participants.

All the questionnaires were first translated into Farsi by two independent translators, and then, subsequently, were translated back into English (linguistic validity). After ratification of translation disparities, experts’ opinion were sought for acknowledgement of the validity of the questionnaires. A test-retest reliability evaluation was performed over a 14 day period on a sample of 10 patients. Pearson correlation coefficient was 0.92 and the Cronbach - reliability of this study sample was demonstrated to be 0.88.

## 2.5. Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed by means of SPSS version 21. The normality of outcome variables was also checked and we used natural-log transformation to improve normality when needed. In order to compare baseline characteristics between the three groups, variance analysis and Chi-Squared test were used and differences between pre and post intervention measurements were tested by paired t-test. An analysis of covariance was used to assess between groups differences adjusted for baseline measure and significance level was set at 0.05 and effect size was calculated using partial eta squared.

# 3. Results
150 diabetic patients were recruited for the study (50 patients in each group) with a mean age of 52.5 10.19 (range 18–79 years). 54% of the participants were male and 46% were female. There were no significantly differences between the three groups in terms of sex, age, family history of diabetes, duration of diabetes and occupation. However, there was a significant difference in level of education between three groups and more highly educated people were in the intervention groups (Table 1).

While tailored and non-tailored SMS groups experienced significant decline in mean BMI during the study (27.71 vs 27.14 for tailored with P < 0.001, and 27.40 vs 26.90 for non-tailored with P = 0.002), the mean BMI of the control group increased marginally (27.92 vs 28.91 with P = 0.045). The reduction of fasting blood sugar levels (FBS) was significant in the tailored SMS group (P = 0.003) and non-tailored SMS Group (P = 0.026) in comparison with the control group. Although triglyceride (TG) levels reduced after the intervention in tailored SMS group, this reduction was not significant (P = 0.176). Moreover, HbA1c and other lipid profiles were not significantly affected by the intervention (Table 2).

Using the pre-intervention measures as covariates in ANCOVA, it was demonstrated that after three months, there were significant differences between the three groups in terms of BMI, FBS, and triglyceride which were followed by pairwise comparisons. All pairwise comparisons of the three groups were significant for triglyceride levels and BMI (all P values <0.05), indicating that both these two indicators differed significantly between each two groups in comparison with the baseline levels. As for FBS, significantly lower FBS levels were observed after the intervention in the tailored SMS group in comparison with the other two groups (P < 0.001 and P = 0.018). However, there were no significant differences between three groups in terms of the other factors after three months when they were compared with the baseline levels (all P values >0.05).

No differences were observed in SCI-R and DMSES mean scores between the three groups at the beginning of the study with P values of 0.890 and 0.091, respectively; however, the mean score of DSCB-OA was significantly different between

non-tailored SMS Group and control group (P = 0.005 for pairwise comparison). After the intervention, mean SCI-R score significantly increased in the tailored and non-tailored SMS groups (P < 0.001), while it significantly decreased in the control group (P < 0.001). Moreover, a significant reduction was observed in the mean DSCB and DMSES scores (P < 0.001) in tailored and non-tailored SMS groups, and they significantly increased in the control group in comparison to the levels at the beginning of the study (P < 0.001) (Table 3).

At the end of the study, SCI-R, DSCB, and DMSES scores differed significantly (all P values <0.001) between three groups in comparison with the baseline values. With post hoc tests, it was demonstrated that SCI-R score was significantly higher in both two SMS groups in comparison with the control group. DSCB and DMSES scores were significantly smaller in both two SMS groups in comparison with control’s one (Table 3).
