# Methods
## Study design and participants

This study was a 3-month randomized controlled trial (RCT) designed to evaluate the use of a tablet-based, chronic disease self-monitoring system for the home (i.e., intervention group) relative to the use of conventional self-monitoring (i.e., control group) in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and/or hypertension. The study was conducted in the patients’ homes at baseline and 1, 2, and 3 months after randomization. The trial protocol was approved by the institutional review board of the University of Hong Kong/Hospital Authority Hong Kong West Cluster.

The participants were recruited from three health service centers of a nonprofit health service network that provided health care and services to the community. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) age of 18 years or above with a diagnosis of type 2 diabetes mellitus and/or hypertension of at least 3 months’ duration, (ii) a prescription for oral hypoglycemic and/or antihypertensive medications, (iii) normal (or corrected-to-normal) vision, (iv) no cognitive or physical impairment, (v) the ability to learn and perform computer-based

disease self-monitoring, and (vi) the ability to understand written and spoken Chinese. Patients with unstable or lifethreatening conditions were excluded. The study was performed between February and July 2014.

The case managers of the health service centers contacted the potential patient participants and invited them to participate in the study. The authors of this article introduced the study to the potential participants who indicated a willingness to participate and screened them for eligibility at the centers. A home visit was later made to each eligible patient. During the home visit, the patients gave written informed consent and were randomized to either the intervention or the control group, stratified by disease type. Randomization was done using permuted blocks of size 4 and 6 (with sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes) presented in random order [14]. Given the nature of the study and its intervention, the patients and outcome assessors were not blinded.

Patients who were assigned to the intervention group received a tablet computer-based disease self-monitoring system. It was an interactive system that was developed on the basis of human factors approaches and feedback from patients with chronic diseases [2, 13]. The system was equipped with a 10-inch tablet computer and a 2-in-1 blood glucose and blood pressure monitor that enabled patients to measure, automatically record, and monitor their blood glucose, blood pressure, and pulse. The system would indicate whether the measured vital signs of patients deviated from the normal value ranges. Abnormal measurement values were displayed in red, and values within the normal ranges were displayed in green. Structured, easy-to-read graphs and tables containing the vital signs records allowed the patients and anyone else, e.g., their families, to keep track of their health conditions. The system also had video-based educational materials that allowed patients to learn how to self-manage their chronic conditions. They included information regarding how to measure blood glucose and blood pressure, what diet to follow, and what exercises to perform. Patients who were assigned to the control group received a 2-in-1 blood glucose and blood pressure monitor for self-monitoring and a log book for recording the vital signs measured and the dates and times of the measurements. The participants in both groups were encouraged to use the devices to measure their vital signs and perform other selfmanagement activities as recommended by their health care providers. All of the self-monitoring devices and materials were provided free of charge. All participants continued with their usual care during the study. Technical support was available for all participants throughout the study.

# Outcome measures
The data on the outcomes reported here were collected at baseline and at all follow-up visits unless otherwise specified. The participants’ baseline characteristics included age, gender,

ethnicity, education level, living status, type and duration of diagnosed chronic diseases, health literacy, and self-reported experience in computer use. Their health literacy was assessed by the three health literacy items illustrated in Chew et al. [15]. We assessed the glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) level and the fasting blood glucose level in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and blood pressure in patients with hypertension. The HbA1c level was assessed at baseline and at the 3- month follow-up visit with a Bayer A1cNow self-check system. The fasting blood glucose level and blood pressure were measured with the 2-in-1 blood glucose and blood pressure monitors used in this study. The participants’ knowledge about diabetes and hypertension is also presented. A modified Michigan Diabetes Knowledge Scale that consisted of 18 true/ false statements was administered to assess diabetes knowledge [16]. Hypertension knowledge was assessed with a validated questionnaire with 19 true/false statements [17]. The percentage of correct responses to the items was calculated to indicate the participants’ knowledge about the chronic diseases. For the participants in the intervention group, the data on the frequency of self-monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG) and self-monitoring of blood pressure (SMBP) that were captured by the tablet self-monitoring system were extracted to assess their self-monitoring frequency. For control patients, self-monitoring frequency was assessed based on the records in the log book.

## Statistical analysis

Differences between the two study groups in terms of baseline demographics and clinical characteristics were assessed with a chi-square test for categorical variables and an unpaired t-test for continuous variables. A linear mixed model was used to compare continuous outcome variables at baseline and at 1, 2, and 3 months between the intervention and control groups in an intention-to-treat manner without imputation for missing data. Two sensitivity analyses were performed, one adjusted for age, gender, the type of disease, and corresponding baseline variables, and the other to consider the effects of missing data by imputing missing values using the last observation carried forward approach [18]. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the differences in the frequency of SMBP and SMBG between groups because of the skewed nature of the data. The analyses were performed with SPSS version 21.

## Systolic and diastolic blood pressures

Both groups experienced declines in their systolic and diastolic blood pressures. Significant declines in systolic blood pressure were seen in the intervention group from baseline to 1 month (−16.7 mm Hg; 95 % confidence

interval [CI], −22.8 to −10.7), 2 months (−10.3 mm Hg; 95 % CI, −16.4 to −4.3), and 3 months (−13.0 mm Hg; 95 % CI, −19.1 to −6.9), and non-significant declines were seen in the control group (−2.1 mm Hg; 95 % CI, −8.6 to 4.4 at 1 month; 6.2 mm Hg; 95 % CI, −12.8 to 0.3 at 2 months; and −5.4 mm Hg; 95 % CI, −12.0 to 1.1 at 3 months) (see Fig. 2a). The decreases in systolic blood pressure were significantly greater in the intervention group than in the control group after 1 (p < 0.001) and 3 months (p = 0.043). Similarly, significant declines in diastolic blood pressure were seen in the intervention group from baseline to the follow-up visits (−8.0 mm Hg; 95 % CI, −11.5 to −4.5 at 1 month; −6.6 mm Hg; 95 % CI, −10.1 to −3.1 at 2 months; and −5.7 mm Hg; 95 % CI, −9.3 to −2.2 at 3 months), and non-significant declines were seen in the control group (−0.3 mm Hg; 95 % CI, −4.1 to 3.4 at 1 month; −1.9 mm Hg; 95 % CI, −5.7 to 1.9 at 2 months; and −2.0 mm Hg; 95 % CI, −5.7 to 1.8 at 3 months) (see Fig. 2b). The declines in the diastolic

blood pressure were significantly greater in the intervention group than the control group after 1 (p < 0.001) and 2 months (p = 0.028).

## Fasting blood glucose

After 3 months, a non-significant decrease in fasting blood glucose was seen in the intervention group (−1.0 mmol/dL; 95 % CI, −2.7 to 0.7), and a nonsignificant increase was seen in the control group (0.4 mmol/dL; 95 % CI, −1.5 to 2.2) (Fig. 2c). The pattern of the trend over time was not significantly different between the two groups (p = 0.407).

## HbA1c

Both groups yielded a similarly slight decrease in HbA1c after 3 months (−0.2; 95 % CI, −0.5 to 0.2 in the intervention group; −0.2; 95 % CI, −0.6 to 0.1 in

the control group) (Fig. 2d), and no between-group or within-group differences were seen.

## Diabetes and hypertension knowledge

Improvements in diabetes knowledge and hypertension knowledge were seen for both groups, except for the hypertension knowledge of the patients in the intervention group 2 months after baseline. None of the improvements was significant, except for the diabetes knowledge of the patients in the intervention group 2 months after baseline.

## Frequency of self-monitoring

During the 3-month study period, intervention and control patients had similar SMBP (p = 0.460) and SMBG (p = 0.087) (see Table 3). In general, on average, patients with comorbid diabetes mellitus and hypertension

performed SMBP daily and SMBG three to five times per week; patients with hypertension took their blood pressure measurements around eight to nine times per week; and patients with diabetes mellitus measured their blood glucose level around four to five times per week. Figure 3 shows the weekly frequency of SMBP and SMBG by study group and the type of disease.

## Use of the tablet-based self-monitoring system

Thirty patients in the intervention group (91 %; excluding one who withdrew from the study and two who did not use the self-monitoring application throughout the study) regularly used the self-monitoring system during the 3-month study period. System usage was high in the first month and decreased in the second and third months for both types of patients (see Fig. 4). Twentyeight of the 30 patients (93 %) uploaded measurements at least 3 days per week during the first month. The

percentage decreased over time, to 67 % during the second month and 73 % during the third month.

## Technical problems

Nine patients in the intervention group encountered one or more problems with the self-monitoring system during the study. The most frequent problem reported was a failure to upload measurements to the system due to malfunctions of the device-cable connection (four patients) and difficulties in measuring the blood glucose level with the blood glucose monitor (four patients). Other technical problems included difficulties with the cable connection between the measurement device and tablet (one patient), system failure (one patient), and the need to replace the tablet’s charger (one patient). There were patients in the control group encountered difficulty taking blood glucose measurements (one patient) or blood pressure measurements (one patient) with the measurement device. No adverse events regarding the use of the system and measurement devices were reported.
