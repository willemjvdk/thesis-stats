## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# 2. Methods
## 2.1. Study design

A three-arm randomized controlled trial was conducted from June to October 2021. This study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the School of Nursing, Jilin University (No. 2020101501). It was registered and approved by the China Clinical Trials Centre (ChiCTR2100045772), and the protocol was published (Gao et al., 2022b).

## 2.2. Participants

Participants were recruited from inpatients in the endocrine and metabolic unit of a university-affiliated hospital in Changchun. Inclusion criteria: Patients with a clinical diagnosis of type 2 diabetes for more than six months according to WHO criteria; age ≥ 18 years; hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) >7%; had access to a smartphone and voluntary participation. Exclusion criteria: patients who were pregnant or breastfeeding; comorbid malignancy; physical limitations in diabetes self-care; hearing or visual impairment; limited physical activity due to other medical conditions; significant cognitive or mental impairment; had no internet facility; had not recently participated in other research projects.

Experienced clinical nurses carefully assessed patients and referred those who met the criteria to the research staff. Then the trained researchers presented participants with detailed information, and if the participant agreed to participate, an informed consent form was signed.

## 2.3. Sample size

The sample size was calculated using the statistical software PASS. An effect size of 0.4 (Grady et al., 2011; Park et al., 2020) was calculated for 66 participants to achieve 80% power, 0.05 significance level, and equal proportional sample allocation using analysis of variance (ANOVA). Considering a 20% dropout rate, the total sample size was 84, equally divided into 28 participants per group.

## 2.4. Randomization

A randomization list was pre-generated by researchers who were not involved in the recruitment using STATA software. Following the baseline assessment, participants were randomly assigned to groups based on their level of activation. The generated random sequence was stored in a sealed envelope until the random allocation process was completed.

## 2.5. Intervention description

The information on self-management of type 2 diabetes was collected from the guideline for the prevention and management of type 2 diabetes mellitus in China (2017, 2020 Edition) (Chinese Diabetes Society, 2018; Chinese Diabetes Society, 2021), Chinese dietary guidelines for type 2 diabetes (Chinese Nutrition Society, 2017), Chinese guidelines for exercise therapy in diabetes mellitus (Chinese Diabetes Society, 2013), Physical Activity/Exercise and Diabetes (Colberg et al., 2016), Clinical application guideline for blood glucose monitoring in China (2015 Edition) (Chinese Diabetes Society, 2015), Chinese guideline on prevention and management of diabetes-related foot (2019 edition) (Chinese Diabetes Society et al., 2019), and American Diabetes Association website. The intervention covered a definition of type 2 diabetes, appropriate diet, exercise, weight management, prevention of hypoglycemia, blood glucose monitoring skills, foot care, smoking cessation, and medication in a total of 9 modules and 30 topics. An outline of the intervention content was presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Delivery of the information as videos, including animation, verbal narrative, and text content, increased the acceptability (Talati et al., 2022), and each video was 40–90s long. Video messages were sent via the communication software WeChat. Objective content-equivalent diabetes self-management skills were highlighted by presenting the outcomes in different message framing (Park et al., 2020; Cao and Wang, 2019). The control group received 30 videos with basic information about diabetes self-care without message framing. The two message framing groups also received 30 videos that included the basic and framing information. Participants in the gain-framed group received videos with basic information and emphasizing desirable outcomes from effective diabetes self-care. Participants in the loss-framed group received videos with basic information and highlighting the undesirable consequences of ineffective diabetes self-care. As an example of foot care, the gain-framed group stated that wearing appropriate shoes and socks would help to protect the skin and reduce the risk of complications such as vascular damage. Whereas the loss-framed message indicated that wearing unsuitable footwear might cause foot friction, increasing the risk of skin breakage and even complications from blood vessel damage. The control group received a video about selecting appropriate shoes and socks without message framing. Supplementary Table 2 presents examples of intervention contents.

A printed intervention outline was distributed to participants before the intervention. The intervention lasted 12 weeks, with each participant receiving video messages three times a week for the first six weeks and twice a week for the following six weeks. Researchers delivered video messages to each participant via WeChat. A specific question about the video content was sent to participants to answer, ensuring that they watched the message carefully and

understood it. For those who answered correctly, a reward of one Chinese Yuan was given to participants via WeChat to increase engagement.

## 2.6. Data collection

The trained researchers completed the outcome assessment. Sociodemographic and disease-related data were assessed at baseline. All outcomes were measured at baseline and after the intervention. The primary goal was to assess self-management behavior using the Summary of Diabetes Self-care Activities, with higher scores indicating greater self-care behaviors, which have been shown with good validity and reliability in both the original and Chinese versions, with Cronbach's alpha ranging from 0.62 to 0.98 (Toobert et al., 2000; Tang et al., 2021). Secondary outcomes included self-efficacy for diabetes management self-efficacy, patient activation, diabetes-related knowledge, attitude, quality of life, and physical indicators (waist circumference, BMI, blood glucose, and blood pressure). Self-efficacy for diabetes management was measured by Stanford Self-efficacy for Diabetes Management scale, with higher scores indicating higher selfefficacy (Lorig et al., 2009), and has been shown with good validity and reliability, with Cronbach's alpha of 0.75 in the Chinese version (Sun et al., 2011). Patient activation was measured by Patient Activation Measurement (Hibbard et al., 2004), with higher scores indicating higher patient activation, and has been shown with good validity and reliability, with Cronbach's alpha of 0.82 in the Chinese version (Chen et al., 2020). Knowledge and attitude were measured by the knowledge and attitude subscale of the knowledge, attitude, and behavior assessment scale for self-management of diabetes, with higher scores indicating higher knowledge and attitude, and has been shown with good validity and reliability, with Cronbach's alpha ranging from 0.86 to 0.78 (Wu et al., 2016). Quality of life was measured using the Chinese Diabetes Specific Quality of Life Scale, with lower scores indicating higher quality of life, with Cronbach's alpha of 0.84 (Fang, 2000). Physical indicators were obtained from patients' electronic medical records and follow-up records.

Engagement, satisfaction, understanding, and manipulation check were assessed following the intervention. The engagement was assessed by several responses to questions following each message intervention, with high engagement being defined as more than 75% of responses. Satisfaction was measured using a 5-point Likert scale, with 0 being extreme dissatisfaction and 4 being highly satisfied (Janke et al., 2011). The understanding was assessed on a 5-point Likert scale, with 0 indicating a failure to understand and 4 indicating a complete understanding. Checking for manipulation was measured by the question, “Overall, to what extent do you think the video messages focus on the benefits you gain from self-management or the disadvantages of not engagement in self-manage?” A score of 1 indicates an emphasis on the benefits, while a score of 7 indicates a focus on the disadvantages (Li et al., 2017b; Hirschey et al., 2016).

## 2.7. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0. One-way ANOVA, non-parametric rank-sum test or chi-square test was used to compare baseline differences between the three groups. Paired t-test was used to analyze within-group comparisons. ANCOVA with baseline data as covariates was used to assess differences in outcomes between the three groups. To identify the potential moderating effects of patient activation on message framing, a two-way ANCOVA was performed with baseline self-management as the covariate to further investigate the interaction between message framing and patient activation on self-management behavior (Park et al., 2020). If there was an interaction effect, a simple main effect was analyzed to explore the impact of different message framing on self-management behavior in the low or high-activation group. Moreover, to evaluate the influence of missing

data on the results, a comparison was made between participants who did not complete the intervention and those who did to verify the sensitivity of this trial. The statistical analysis followed the intent-to-treat principle, with missing data values filled in with the latest assessment results (Jakobsen et al., 2017). A two-sided P < 0 . 0 5was considered statistically different, and the test levels were corrected for multiple comparisons by the Bonferroni method.

# 3. Results
## 3.1. Participant flow and baseline characteristics

A total of eight dropouts after a 12-week intervention, with data from 84 participants were analyzed based on the intent-to-treat principle (Fig. 1). The mean (± standard deviation, SD) age of participants was 56.17 (9.35) years, 45.2% were female, the mean disease duration was 8.89 (6.73) years, and the HbA1c mean was 9.18% (1.67). The baseline data of participants in the three groups were balanced, as shown in Table 1.

# 3.2. Effects of message framing interventions on the primary outcome
According to the within-group comparison analysis, participants who received gain- or loss-framed message interventions had significantly higher self-management scores after the intervention than baseline ( P < 0 . 0 5 )). However, there was no significant difference in the control group (Supplementary Table 3).

According to the findings of the inter-group comparison, there was a statistically significant difference in the post-intervention selfmanagement scores of participants in the three groups after adjusting for the baseline self-management scores ( F = 6 . 9 1 9 , P = 0 . 0 0 2 ). Table 2 presents detailed results, and Supplementary Table 4 presents the adjusted scores outcome variables. Further, both message frames increased self-management scores compared to the control group. The adjusted

post-intervention self-management level in the gain-framed message group was, on average, 2.19 points higher than in the control group, with a statistically significant difference (95% CI: 0.069–4.314, P = 0.041). The adjusted post-intervention self-management level in the loss-framed message group was, on average, 3.16 points higher than in the control group, with a statistically significant difference (95% CI: 1.033–5.290, P = 0.001). No significant difference was observed in the two message framing groups. Table 3 depicts the detailed results.

# 3.3. Effects of message framing interventions on secondary outcomes
According to the within-group comparison analysis, participants who received gain- or loss-framed message interventions had significantly higher scores of self-efficacy, patient activation, diabetesrelated knowledge, and attitude, whereas significantly lower scores of quality of life. Significantly increased physical indicators (waist circumference, BMI, fasting blood glucose, and systolic blood pressure) after the intervention were compared to baseline (P < 0.05). Participants in the control group reported a significant increase in knowledge and patient activation scores after the intervention compared to baseline (P < 0.05), but no significant difference was observed in the remaining outcomes (Supplementary Table 3).

According to the findings of the inter-group comparison, there was a statistically significant difference in the post-intervention self-efficacy, patient activation, diabetes-related knowledge, attitude, and quality of

life scores of participants in the three groups after adjusting for each variable baseline scores. However, the three groups observed no statistically significant differences in the post-intervention physical indicators. Table 2 presents detailed results of the analysis.

Further analysis showed that after the intervention, only lossframed messages increased scores of self-efficacy, patient activation, diabetes-related knowledge, and attitude compared to the control group. And both message frames were found to decrease the quality of life scores compared to the control group. Detailed results are presented in Table 3. Supplementary Table 4 presents the adjusted scores outcome variable.

## 3.4. Analysis of the interaction between message framing and patient activation

After adjusting for baseline self-management scores, there was an interaction between patient activation and message frame (F(2, 77) = 6.786, P = 0.002), as shown in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 5. The self-management behavior scores after stratification by patient activation are shown in Supplementary Table 6.

In both the high and low patient activation groups, the simple main effect of message framing was statistically significant (Supplementary Table 7). In the low activation group, adjusted post-intervention selfmanagement behaviors scores were higher in the loss framing group than in the gain framing group (95% CI: 1.022–6.422, P = 0.003) and

control group (95% CI: 1.652–6.964, P < 0.001). In the high activation group, adjusted post-intervention self-management behaviors scores were higher in the gain framing group than in the control group (95% CI: 1.166–7.096, P = 0.003). The results indicated that gain and loss message framing interventions are more effective in increasing selfmanagement behaviors scores in participants with higher and lower activation levels, respectively. Supplementary Table 8 presents the results of the pairwise comparison of self-management after stratification of patient activation level.

# 3.5. Analysis of additional outcome indicators
The three groups had no statistically significant differences regarding engagement, satisfaction, or understanding. Analysis between groups showed statistically significant differences in the evaluation manipulation check (Supplementary Tables 9 and 10).

## 3.6. Sensitivity analysis

We also compared participants who completed the intervention and those who dropped out. The two sets of participants showed similar demographic characteristics but lower self-efficacy and attitudes scores and higher fasting blood glucose among dropouts (Supplementary Table 11).
