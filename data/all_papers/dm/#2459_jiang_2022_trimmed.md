# 2.6.1  |  Outcome measures
The primary outcome of self-efficacy was assessed by the generalized self-efficacy scale (GSE) (Schwarzer & Jerusalem, 2010). It is a 10-item scale assessing the strength of a person's belief about their ability to cope with challenges and setbacks in a variety of life situations. Each item was rated on a 4-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating better self-efficacy.

Secondary outcomes included diabetes self-care as measured by the revised Summary of Diabetes Self-Care Activities (SDSCA), HRQoL as measured by the Audit of Diabetes-Dependent Quality of Life (DDQoL) and number of acute diabetes complications and unplanned medical consultation. The unplanned medical consultation included hospital admissions due to diabetic complications, emergency department visits and outpatient medical consultations.

The SDSCA is a 11-item scale measuring levels of selfmanagement across different domains of diabetes self-care, including general and specific diet, physical exercise, blood glucose monitoring, foot care and smoking. For each domain, except smoking, the score indicated the average number of days per week that the individuals participated in specific diabetes self-care activities (Toobert et al., 2000).

The DDQoL is a disease-specific instrument measuring the impact of diabetes on a person's quality of life. It comprises of 19-domain specific items measuring the impact of diabetes on specific domains of life and the importance of these domains of life on quality of life (QoL). It also has two overview items measuring the ‘generic present QoL’ and diabetes-specific ‘impact of diabetes on QoL’. The overall average weight impact (AWI) score reflected the overall impact of diabetes on the important domains of an individual's quality of life, ranging from −9 (negative impact) to 3 (positive impact) of diabetes (Soon et al., 2010). For the two overview items, the ‘generic present QoL’ ranged from −3 to 3, with higher scores indicating better generic QoL. The diabetes-specific ‘impact of diabetes on QoL’ ranged from −3 to 1, with −3 representing the greatest negative impact of diabetes on QoL and 1 representing the greatest positive impact (Soon et al., 2010).

The clinical outcomes included HbA1c which was retrieved from participant's blood test reports at the diabetes clinic and the number of participants who had acute diabetes complications at 3-month and 6-month follow-ups. The unplanned medical consultation including hospital admissions due to diabetic complications, emergency department visits and outpatient medical consultations was also assessed at 6-month follow-up.

The instruments used in this study are valid and reliable as shown in previous studies. The generalized self-efficacy scale (GSE) has

good internal consistency as shown by a Cronbach's alpha of 0.92 and a test–retest reliability with intraclass correlation coefficients of 0.75–0.94 (Schwarzer & Jerusalem, 2010). The revised Summary of Diabetes Self-Care Activities (SDSCA) and the Audit of Diabetes-Dependent Quality of Life (DDQoL) are self-report scales widely validated with good psychometric properties (Soon et al., 2010; Toobert et al., 2000).

## 2.6.2  |  Recruitment

Recruitment was initiated at the diabetes clinic where potential participants were screened by diabetes APN or her delegated staff and referred to the research nurse. The research nurse then conducted an interview in person to confirm the participant's eligibility, obtain their informed consent, and collect the sociodemographic and baseline data prior to randomization. After the intervention, the research nurse would arrange appointments with the participants to collect post-test data at 3 months (post-test 1) and 6 months (post-test 2) post-baseline assessment. The appointment on average took 20 mins for completion of questionnaires.

## 2.7  |  Ethical consideration

Ethics approval was obtained from the National Healthcare Group-Domain-Specific Review Board (NHG-DSRB: 2013/00727) prior to study commencement. All participants were informed of the nature and objective of the study, the possible risks and benefits, and the extent of their participation. They were also informed of their right to study withdrawal at any time without compromising their treatment or care. The confidentiality of participants' data was assured.

## 2.8  |  Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS 24.0. Descriptive statistics were used to present the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. Intention-to-treat analysis was used to manage missing data, where missing values in the continues variables were imputed by means. Independent sample t test or chi-square analysis were used to determine sample equivalence at baseline. Repeated measures ANOVA was used to test the interaction effect (groups × time) of each continuous outcome if it was normally distributed. Variables for baseline difference were controlled for as covariates in the analysis. Otherwise, nonparametric test was employed to determine the difference in the outcome between groups at each time point. The chi-square test of Fisher's exact test was used to determine the difference between the two groups in the numbers of smokers and the numbers of participants who developed acute diabetes complications during the past time interval. All significant levels were set at p .05.

## 3  |  FINDINGS

Participants were recruited from June 2018 to September 2020. A total of 146 people with poorly controlled type 2 diabetes from the study hospital were approached and screened, of which 114 (78%) eligible participants were recruited in the study. We did not reach the determined sample size of 128 participants due to an increase in community-transmitted cases of COVID-19 in Singapore in 2020, which led to the cessation of most of research activities in the study hospital.

Over the course of 6 months, nine (16%) participants from the control group and seven (12%) participants from the intervention group dropped out for reasons listed in Figure 1. The overall attrition rate was 14%, 98 participants completed all the data collections. There was a considerable amount of missing data on alcohol consumption that prevented meaningful analyses. As this result was not part of our study objectives or research hypotheses, it was not analysed. In addition, a cost-effectiveness analysis (which was the objective 3 in our published protocol) was not carried out. While some information on use of health resource was collected, it was not used to undertake a cost-effectiveness analysis and is therefore not reported in this manuscript. It is a deviation from the protocol.

Table 1 compares the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the participants between the two groups. In both groups, majority of participants were male (66.1% vs. 63.8%), married (71.4% vs. 69%), had a highest education level below secondary school (71.4% vs. 60.4%), worked full-time/part-time (64.3% vs. 72.4%), and lived with family (87.5% vs. 89.7%). On average, there were significantly younger participants in the intervention group (49.6 years old) compared with control group (56.0 years old, p = .003). There were also significantly more former or current smokers in the intervention group than in the control group (p < .05). However, the number of people with heart disease was significantly higher in the control group (32.1%) than in the intervention group (15.8%, p = .042).

Repeated measures ANOVA was performed on GSE, SDSCA_ General diet, SDSCA_ Specific diet, SDSCA_ Exercise, SDSCA_ Blood glucose testing, SDSCA_ Foot care, DDQoL_ Generic present QoL, DDQoL_ Diabetes-specific QoL, DDQoL_AWI and HbA1c. Baseline confounding variables including age, ex-smoker, current smoker, history of heart disease and baseline GSE scores were controlled as covariates in the analysis.

Table 2 summarized the interaction effects of these outcomes and the mean differences between intervention group and control group at each follow-up time point after adjusting for the covariates. There were insignificant interactions between group and time for all the outcomes except for SDSCA_ Blood glucose testing. Specifically, participants in the control group showed a gradual and consistent increase in glucose testing activity from baseline to 6-month follow-up, whereas participants in the intervention group showed improvement in glucose testing activity at 3-month follow-up but a decrease in glucose testing activity at 6-month follow-up (Figure 2). Figure 3 shows the trend of HbA1c values in the two groups over the course of the study. Both groups had reduced HbA1c values.

Although not statistically significant, the intervention group had a lower HbA1c than the control group at the post-intervention followups (Figure 3).

None of the participants had acute diabetes compilations at 3-month or 6-month follow-up. Table 3 showed the comparisons of unplanned medical consultation use at 6-month follow-up.
