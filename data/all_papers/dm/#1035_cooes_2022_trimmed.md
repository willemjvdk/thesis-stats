## EXTRACTED SECTIONS

# METHODS
## Study Design

A two-group (1:1 allocation), parallel, randomized controlled design was used. Participants provided written informed consent and the study was approved by the University’s Medical Research Ethics Committee and registered with the Australia and New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ACTRN12617001488392).

## Inclusion Criteria

Community dwelling, diagnosis of T2D, age over 20 yr, not meeting PA guidelines, access to a smart phone, and willing/able to access the study site. The diagnosis of T2D needed to be made by the person’s doctor.

## Exclusion Criteria

Myocardial infarction within 6 months, unstable angina, coronary artery disease, severe hypertension (systolic BP > 200 mm Hg and/or diastolic BP > 110 mm Hg), uncontrolled cardiac arrhythmias, severe hemodynamically significant valvular disease, heart failure, acute pulmonary embolus, pulmonary infarction or deep venous thrombosis, acute myocarditis or pericarditis, suspected or known aneurysm, using ß-blockers, any changes in diabetes medication in the last 3 months, participating in another clinical intervention or exercise study, regularly taking part in endurance exercise, weight loss surgery in the previous year, planned operations during the study period, drug or alcohol abuse; current or expected pregnancy, significant cognitive impairment, limited life expectancy (<6 months), or any other condition preventing an ability to perform PA.

## Recruitment, Screening, and Consent

Recruitment was via advertising in University publications, community newspapers, Facebook, Google ads, local diabetes associations, and clinical links. Screening was initially conducted via an online questionnaire or phone call by a member of the research team. If a person was deemed potentially eligible they were emailed the Patient Information and Consent Form and an assessment time was arranged. This occurred at least 24 h before their first assessment visit to the research laboratory (testing session 1). At this visit, the person was asked if they have any questions about the study before being asked to sign the Patient Information and Consent Form. If a person was deemed ineligible after either of the screening visits (testing sessions 1 and 2), their data were destroyed.

## Randomization, Stratification, and Blinding

When a participant was deemed eligible for inclusion (after testing sessions 1 and 2), their details were sent to a person independent of the study team who applied a computer-generated permuted block random allocation sequence. Stratification for age (above/below 50 yr of age) and sex (male/female) was used

to keep these variables balanced between groups. Allocation details for each participant were then sent to the project coordinator.

## Testing Sessions

Two testing sessions were conducted before (baseline) and after (posttrial) the 12-wk trial in University’s Clinical Exercise Physiology Laboratories. For 24 h before the testing sessions participants were asked not to consume any coffee, tea, or alcohol or perform any intense exercise. They were required to complete an overnight fast with sessions starting at ~8:00 AM). Participants were instructed to take medications as usual and drink water before coming, so as to not feel dehydrated. Participants were provided with a meal replacement beverage (Sustagen™; Nestlé Health Science, Murarrie, Australia) at the end of the sessions. If they preferred, they were given the opportunity to bring their choice of snack to eat at the end of the sessions. Upon arriving they were asked about their adherence to these pretest requirements and what medications they have taken in the last 24 h. Nonadherence with pretest requirements led to rescheduling.

Testing session 1 (baseline). At this session the following measures were conducted:

Glycemic control: measured using blood samples collected from an antecubital vein after fasting and during a 2-h oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). The OGTT was conducted only in participants not taking insulin. HbA1c, plasma glucose, and insulin were assessed using manufacturer supplied kits in clinical auto analyzers. The RX Daytona+ (Randox, Kearneysville, WV) was used for HbA1c and glucose and a Cobas E411 (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) for insulin. The homeostasis assessment model 2 (HOMA2) was used to estimate overall insulin sensitivity (%S), beta-cell function (%B), and insulin resistance (IR) (11). Indices from the OGTT included the plasma glucose value at 120 min, the area under the glucose curve over 120 min, and the Matsuda, Insulinogenic, and Disposition indexes. The Matsuda Index is a measure of glucose disposal with values comparable to the rate of glucose disappearance measured by the hyperinsulinemic/euglycemic clamp approach (13). The Insulinogenic Index is a marker of beta cell function (14) and the Disposition Index assesses the combination of beta cell function and the ability of the body to dispose of the glucose load (15).

Lipid profile: serum lipid profile (total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL], high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglycerides), was assessed using manufacturer supplied assay kits in the RX Daytona+ (Randox).

Physical measures: body mass, height, and waist circumference. Surveys: to obtain sociodemographic data (ethnicity, education level, employment status), health history, smoking history, current symptoms of disease, comorbidities, medication use, current exercise habits, and health-related quality of life (Short Form [SF]-36).

Additional screening: information collected during testing session 1 was reviewed by the Study Medical Doctor who

made a clinical judgment regarding whether the participant was able to participate in the maximal exercise test (testing session 2) or whether additional information from their doctor was needed.

Accelerometer and PA diary: participants approved to progress to testing session 2 were provided with an accelerometer \mathrm { ( G T 3 X ^ { + } ; }ActiGraph, Pensacola, FL) and asked to wear this for the next 7 d on their nondominant wrist for 24 h·d−1 . They were also provided with a diary to record any PA they completed over the 7 d.

Testing session 2 (baseline). This was held at least 8 d after testing session 1. At this session, the following measures were conducted:

Physical activity: The accelerometers were returned, and data were downloaded using a software package (ActiLife; Actigraph). Raw acceleration was extracted and processed using R-package GGIR (16). This included a postcalibration process using local gravity (17), nonwear detection (18), and calculation of the vector magnitude of activity-related acceleration using the Euclidian Norm minus 1g (ENMO = \sqrt { \mathit { x } 2 + \mathit { y } 2 + z 2 - 1 ) }) (16). To be entered into analyses, devices needed to record at least 1 6 \mathrm { { h } } { \cdot } \mathrm { { d } } ^ { - 1 }over 4+ d, including at least one weekend day. Acceleration in milligravity units (mg) aggregated in 5-s epochs was used to estimate time spent in activities at different intensities using the intensity thresholds proposed by Hildebrand et al. (19) (Light intensity: acceleration 50–100 mg; moderate intensity: acceleration 100–400 mg; vigorous intensity: acceleration higher than 400 mg). Sleep time was estimated using the algorithm proposed by van Hees et al. (20).

Blood pressure: measured at the upper arm with a sphygmomanometer after a seated 5-min rest.

Body composition: total and regional fat mass and fat-free mass was assessed using Dual Energy X-ray absorptiometry (Horizon A; Hologic, Bedford, MA) in accordance with manufacturer specifications. Participants were asked to void their bladder within 30 min of the scan. Scans were analyzed by a researcher blinded to group allocation using manufacturersupplied software \boldsymbol { ( \mathrm { A P E X } ^ { \mathbb { R } } }version 5.6.0.5), with reference to the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey population database, which has a coefficient of variation of 0.52% for fat free mass and 0.78% for fat mass.

Cardiopulmonary Exercise Test (CPET): was supervised by the Study Medical Doctor and determined exercise capacity (time on test), cardiorespiratory fitness ( \dot { \mathrm { V } } \mathrm { O } _ { 2 \mathrm { p e a k } } ) ., and peak heart rate. The test began with a 3-min standing rest period followed by a warm-up at 4 \mathrm { k m } { \cdot } \mathrm { h } ^ { - 1 }at a 0% gradient for 4 min and then 4 min at a 4% gradient at the same speed. After the warm-up, participants exercised in continuous 1-min stages at either a walking or jogging pace that increased in speed and/or grade, until volitional exhaustion. Standardized verbal encouragement was given throughout the tests. Pulmonary gas exchange was measured from the beginning of the rest period until cessation of the test using

the Parvo metabolic system (Parvo Medics, Sandy, UT). Simultaneous electrocardiogram recordings were made during the tests. The rating of perceived exertion and heart rate were monitored before the test, during warm-up, every minute during the exercise test, at cessation and at 1, 2, and 3 min postexercise. Blood pressure was also assessed before, during, and at 1 and 5 min posttest

Testing Sessions 3 and 4 (Posttrial): Twelve weeks after testing session 2, all participants were asked to attend testing sessions 3 and 4. Procedures and requirements during these were the same as for testing sessions 1 and 2 (excluding the survey items about sociodemographic data, health history, smoking history, and comorbidities).

## PAI e-Health Program

Participants randomized to the PAI Group were asked to attend 4  2 h·wk−1 small group sessions at the University. At the first session, participants were given a wrist-worn monitor (Lynk2, Accuro, Oakbrook Terrace, IL) to keep, and downloaded the PAI Health app (PAI Health, Vancouver, Canada). The band uses photoplethysmography to measure heart rate and sends data to the app on their smartphone. The app syncs with the PAI Health database to determine, and provide feedback on PAI scores.

Each of the four sessions consisted of activities that included; PAI-learning/interpretive assistance, exercise tasters (PAI-playtime), and behavioral counseling.

PAI-learning/interpretive assistance was designed to help participants understand the PAI concept and how to (i) use the PAI app on their smartphone, (ii) activate heart rate recording and (iii) troubleshoot problems. Interpretive Assistance involved answering questions and solving any issues that participants may have about the monitor and app. PAI-playtime was where an accredited exercise physiologist guided participants through different types of exercise (for short bouts of 5–10 min) at different intensities to experience different ways PAI can be obtained. Exercises were mixed and included some in a gym to provide some exposure to equipment-based activities, some using TheraBands (given to participants to keep) and some using activities that could be done at home (e.g., stair climbing, hill walking). Participants were also encouraged to explore other types of exercise outside of sessions (e.g., gardening, bicycling). Behavioral counseling. Group counseling was led by a registered psychologist with endorsements in clinical and health psychology. Sessions targeted exercise-related knowledge and values (e.g., links between exercise, diabetes management, and other personally meaningful outcomes), intentions, capability beliefs, behavioral regulation, and negative emotions. Behavior change techniques were tailored to respond to emerging issues and included salience of consequences, behavioral review, problem solving, feedback, informational and emotional support, and behavioral experiments with PAI.

At the end of the four sessions, it was expected that participants were able to: a) use the PAI app; connect app to the

wrist monitor, sync data, and know where to get help if they experienced any technical problems throughout the study period and b) know how to mix and match exercises of varying modality and intensity to reach 100 PAI points per week. After the first session, participants were asked to work toward a PAI score ≥100 points by the beginning of week 2 (as it takes 1 wk to generate a PAI score that reflects the last 7 d), and for the remainder of the trial.

## PA Attention Control Group

Participants randomized to the control group continued to receive usual care from their GPs and/or other health care providers. In accordance with the current guidelines on exercise for people with T2D, they received a written pamphlet explaining the role of exercise in diabetes management, including recommendations to accumulate 210 min·wk−1 of moderate or 125 min·wk−1 of vigorous exercise (21). For PA attention control, they were required to attend 4  1 h·wk−1 exercise sessions that focused on improving balance and flexibility. During the trial, they were not supplied with any information regarding PAI. At the completion of posttrial testing, they were provided with the activity monitor to keep and information on how to use the device as a sign of gratitude for participating in the study.

Feasibility: of the PAI e-Health Program was assessed by i) attendance at the weekly intervention group sessions and ii) the ability of participants to achieve and maintain the PAI target, which was assessed as the number of days that their PAI score was ≥100. This was determined from the stored data on the PAI Health server (see Data Analysis).

Efficacy: was assessed as between group differences in glycemic control, cardiorespiratory fitness, exercise capacity (time-on-test), body composition, PA assessed by accelerometry, sleep time, and health-related quality of life.

Acceptability: of the PAI eHealth Program in the participants randomized to receive the intervention was assessed via a brief individual semi-structured interview after testing session 4. This was led by the project coordinator. Questions focused on participants’ experience of the Program, intention to use PAI in the future, and ease of PAI use.

Safety: was assessed by monitoring adverse events. Participants were asked to record any physical symptoms (e.g., extreme breathlessness, chest pain), musculoskeletal injuries, hypoglycemic events, falls, acute illnesses, and changes in health status/medications in a diary provided to them. They were asked to contact project staff if they had any issues/ anything to report. During week 6 (half way during the trial), participants were contacted by phone to check on their adverse event reporting. They were asked again about adverse events after Testing Session 4. A Medical Doctor was the adjudicator for adverse events.

## Data Analysis

The following PAI metrics were generated for each individual from daily data stored on the PAI Health server over weeks

2 to 12 (i.e., it takes 1 wk to generate a PAI score that reflects the last 7 d); 1) average PAI score (reflecting PA accumulated over the previous 7 d), 2) average daily PAI accrued from low-, moderate-, and high-intensity PA, and 3) proportion of days that an individual maintained 25, 50, 75, or 100 PAI. This latter metric was calculated as progressive health benefits may be observed with lower values of PAI (8).

As a feasibility study no a priori sample size calculation was performed. The participant number of 30 was chosen based on our previous experience with the PAI approach that indicated this number of people would provide the necessary data to determine feasibility. For efficacy measures, between group differences posttrial were assessed using an ANCOVA, adjusting for the baseline measure. Confidence intervals (CI) were adjusted using a Bonferroni approach. Model residuals were checked to ensure the assumptions of the model were satisfied. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 26 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL).

# RESULTS
Figure 1 shows participant flow through the study with reasons for exclusions. The recruitment target of 30 individuals was achieved, and Table 1 provides a summary of their baseline characteristics. During the trial (January 2019 to November 2019), two participants in the intervention group and three in control withdrew. There were no changes in the prescription of medications.

## Feasibility

Attendance at the four PAI e-Health Program group sessions was 98% for the 12 participants who were included in the analysis (one participant missed one session). Table 2 provides PAI data during the trial from the participants randomized to the PAI e-Health Program. On average, the PAI score was 119.7 ± 60.6 and participants met the goal of maintaining >100 PAI on 56.4% of the days. The majority of PAI per day

was earned from moderate-intensity activity (7.6 ± 3.2), with 4.9 ± 3.7 PAI per day coming from high-intensity exercise.

## Efficacy

Cardiorespiratory fitness and exercise capacity. Table 3 and Figure 2A show a significant between-group difference in exercise capacity (time-on-test) after the trial. The PAI Program group exercised for 63 s (95% CI, 17.9–108.0 s) longer during the CPET than the control group. The adjusted mean difference for \dot { \mathrm { V O } } _ { 2 \mathrm { p e a k } }relative to body mass indicated the PAI Program group had a nonsignificant increase in \dot { \mathrm { V O } } _ { 2 \mathrm { p e a k } }of 2.35 mL·kg−1 ·min−1 (−2.11 to 6.80 mL·kg−1 ·min−1 ) relative to control.

PA, sedentary time, and sleep. There were no significant between-group differences in accelerometry derived moderatevigorous PA or sedentary time (Table 3). There was a significant between-group difference in sleep time after the trial (Table 3 and Fig. 2B). The PAI Program group slept for 67.2 min·d−1 longer than the control group (95% CI, 7.2–127.1 min).

Body composition. Table 3 and Figure 2C show a significant between-group difference in the loss of total percent body fat. Compared with the control group, the PAI Program group lost 1.3% more body fat (95% CI, −2.6% to −0.1%). Table 3 and Figure 2D indicate a significant between-group difference in the loss of percent gynoid fat with the PAI Program group losing 1.5% (2.6 to −0.5%) more than controls. Both of these percent changes in fat appear to be due to decreases in fat mass and/or increases in lean mass (Table 3).

Glycemic control. There were no significant betweengroup differences in any of the glycemic control measures (HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose or insulin, HOMA2-IR, HOMA2-%B, HOMA2-%S, and OGTT measures). The adjusted mean difference for HbA1c indicated a between group decrease of 0.2% (−1.1% to 0.7%) in the PAI group relative to control.

Blood pressure and blood lipids. There were no significant between-group differences in blood pressure or blood lipids (Table 3).

Health-related quality of life (SF-36). The PAI Program group demonstrated a significant improvement in role limitations due to emotional problems, with an adjusted between-group difference of 21.6 (0.3–42.9). A number of other aspects of health-related quality of life demonstrated suggested improvements in the PAI Program group relative to control (physical functioning, role limitations due to physical health, and social functioning), although these were not significant.

## Acceptability

All participants from the PAI Program (n = 14) were invited to participate in a semi-structured interview at the end of the trial, with 10 accepting the offer. Eight of the 10 participants indicated that they enjoyed the Program and intended to continue to use PAI monitoring. Six participants expressed concerns with the technical issues, including connectivity between the monitor and their smartphone. The two participants who stated that they did not enjoy the Program said it was because of the syncing problems they continually experienced.

## Safety

There was one adverse event with a PAI Program participant experiencing a mild hypoglycemic episode (blood glucose 3.6 mmol·L−1 ) at the end of a group session - this resolved quickly; after eating a fast-acting carbohydrate.
