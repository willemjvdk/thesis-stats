# Methods
## Site

Service régional de soins à domicile (SRSAD) offers specialized home care services to adults suffering from severe chronic lung disease without assisted ventilation and living in the Greater Montreal area, Canada. Founded in 1968, SRSAD grew out a desire to offer home care and services to clients who were consuming a considerable amount of hospital resources. In 1983, this specialized service for patients with chronic lung disease was regionalized; responsibility for its administration was given to the Medicine Program at the Maisonneuve-Rosemont Hospital (MRH), and its operations began to be managed in partnership with local communitybased service centres. In 2011—2012, SRSAD was following over 4100 registered patients in their homes.

SRSAD provides patients with curative, palliative, preventive and rehabilitative care, with a goal of improving their quality of life from an overall health perspective. Its patients present severe lung problems and are subject to multiple decompensations. Most of them have specialized equipment in the home, such as oxygen concentrators and non-invasive ventilation, and require close monitoring and frequent assessments of their cardio-respiratory condition. In order to serve a growing number of patients, SRSAD has a team of managers and over 25 nurse clinicians and 30 respiratory therapists.

In the 3 years preceding the telehomecare program examined in this study, SRSAD had experienced an approximately 11% increase in its client base. This growth was among the most severe cases, since patients with less demanding needs were being directly managed by local, community-based service centres. SRSAD’s capacity to handle the case load

had reached a limit (with an average of 55 patients per nurse and an average of 70 patients by respiratory therapist). The organization was having increasing difficulty recruiting nurse clinicians and respiratory therapists, and the problem was exacerbated by professionals departing for retirement, on maternity leaves or on long-term disability leaves and not being replaced.

Many work reorganization projects had recently been undertaken (hiring a physical rehabilitation therapist, a respiratory therapy technician and medical technologists) to compensate the shortage of resources, but the situation remained very precarious. It became necessary to look for different ways of providing health services that would maintain their quality. This optimization objective led SRSAD managers to consider the potential for a contribution by information technologies. It was in this spirit that, in 2010—2011, SRSAD carried out the home telemonitoring project examined in this assessment as part of a reorganization of its care services offering.

## Description of the intervention

The technological solution included providing patients with a touch screen with an integrated modem (TELUSTM). A custom follow-up protocol was programmed into the device to monitor various parameters, and patients were taught how to follow the protocol during their initial meeting with one of the two nurse case managers. Then the patients had to send the clinical data over the Internet each time they were collected. More specifically, every day the patients had to complete a data entry table documenting symptoms and medication consumed. They also had to read specific learning capsules on COPD adapted from the self-management program called ‘‘Living well with COPD’’. The tool was used in order for the patient to understand the relationships between his or her condition, environment, life habits and medication management, thereby empowering the patient to better self-manage the disease by following a therapeutic action plan.

The information sent by the Internet was securely stored on a server where the case managers could consult the data every day, remotely monitoring patient health status and adherence to the prescribed therapy. In addition, the

device automatically analyzed the data as it was entered, and automatically issued a warning when the data strayed from predefined parameters. Like a push technology, the computer system generated warnings to both the patient and the nurse case manager whenever it detected one of the following situations:

In such situations, the system immediately intervened, sending preprogrammed advice on the appropriate required action. The advice was based on the best clinical practices from the Canadian Thoracic Society COPD guidelines and the aforementioned COPD self-management program. At the same time, the nurse case manager was automatically notified of the problems and the suggestions made to the patient. The nurse case manager could therefore remotely monitor the patient’s responses and, if necessary, directly intervene by phoning the patient or informing the attending physician, who determined an appropriate response.

## Study design

The research design consisted of a retrospective and prospective randomized controlled trial (RCT). Patients were selected based on a strict series of inclusion and exclusion criteria. First, the evaluation project was focused on active patients with very serious cases of COPD requiring frequent home visits. The patients considered for this study had a forced volume vital capacity (FEV1) less than 45% of the predicted value, were hospitalized at least once in the preceding year, showed a willingness to manage their health status (with or without an informal caregiver), were able to communicate in English or French, and had an operational telephone line in the home. Patients were excluded from the study if they suffered from psychological or psychiatric problems, had a cognitive deficit that would make them unable to participate in their own treatment or suffered from a visual or motor deficit that would make them unable to use the remote telemonitoring technology unless

an informal caregiver agreed to shoulder this responsibility for them.

To reduce the differences between the two groups, patients were recruited in pairs and the matching criteria were age, sex, and the severity of their illness. The members of each pair were randomly and blindly assigned to either the control group or the experimental group. A total of 140 patients were recruited from September 2010 to March 2011. The sample size was established on the basis of pragmatic constraints of time and resources. As shown in Table 3, 20 of these patients (10 in each group) had to be withdrawn from the study for various reasons. In view of the somewhat high attrition rate (14%), it was necessary to determine how similar members of the ‘‘drop out’’ group were to those who remained in the sample. As recommended by Miller and Hollist [19], we conducted a logistical regression analysis that included demographic data (age, gender), clinical data (primary diagnosis), and cost variables associated with the pre-period (nurse home visits, ER visits and hospitalizations). No statistically significant coefficient was found, indicating that there is no evidence of attrition bias.

Table 4 presents a profile of the 120 participants at the start of the study. Most of the patients were French-speaking (85%) with an average age of 68 years (minimum = 55 and maximum = 86). There were more women than men (women represented 68% of the sample). A total of 60% of the participants were dependent on oxygen, and their average forced expired volume in one second (FEV1) was 28.5%. Based on these clinical indicators, the population consisted of severe COPD cases. The comparative analyses of the experimental and control groups showed that the matching strategy was effective. Indeed, none of the comparison variables was statistically significant.

The average observation period in the pre-phase (the period preceding deployment of the technological device) was 365 days, while the average length of the per-phase (the period during which the technology was used) was 178 days (117—241 days) and that of the post-phase (the period following withdrawal of the technology) was 105 days (12—191 days). It should be mentioned that not all the patients were recruited at the same time; rather, they were recruited

sequentially, so the beginning of the pre-phase ran from September 27, 2010 to March 10, 2011. Finally, the postperiod ended on October 4, 2011 for all participants.

The main indicators selected for this analysis are associated with patient consumption of health services, in particular home visits (nurses and respiratory therapists) as well as ER visits and hospitalizations associated with the primary diagnosis. Using these indicators, we conducted a detailed economic analysis to compare the costs associated with managing a chronically ill person using the traditional home care approach compared with the telehomecare approach. The following section describes the specific methodology used to make this analysis. The data on consumption of health services were extracted from the administrative systems of hospitals in the Greater Montreal Area. As a final note, the study design was approved by the ethics authorities.

# Results
Before we present the results of the economic analysis, we will describe the effects of the telehomecare program on the various indices of health service consumption. We will examine the statistics on ER visits, hospitalizations (including average hospital stay) and home visits by clinicians (nurses and respiratory therapists). We will then present the rate of patient satisfaction with the technological device used in this trial. It is important to note that in order to properly assess the impact of home telemonitoring on the consumption of health care services and the costs associated with these services, the data for the post-period was adjusted from 105 to 187 days. This made the number of days before (pre) system deployment and the number of days after (per + post) system deployment equal and, hence, allowed comparisons on an annual basis.

## Consumption of health care services

First, the results presented in Table 5 show that the number of patients in the experimental group who visited an ER at

least once declined from 44 (the pre-period) to 18 (the per period) to 13 (the adjusted post-period). It is important to mention that we only compiled ER visits related to the primary diagnosis. Importantly, the total number of ER visits fell from 121 (pre) to 35 (per) and then went up slightly, to 43 (adjusted post). This suggests a 36% decrease in ER visits following deployment of the technological device (from 121 to 78) among these patients. A paired comparative pre- and post-analysis reveals that this reduction is statistically significant (t = 2.4; P = 0.02). On the other hand, the number of patients in the control group who visited the ER at least once declined from 49 (pre) to 18 (per) and then went up to 23 (post). We observed a 13% decrease in the total number of ER visits (from 127 to 111) following system deployment among these patients (t = 0.7; P = 0.47). While the difference between the two groups was not statistically significant during the initial (pre) period (t = 0.23; P = 0.82) and the decrease was larger in the intervention group than in the control group (P = 0.02 vs. 0.47), a paired comparative intergroup test indicates that the mean difference between the two groups in terms of ER visits is not statistically significant (t = 0.9; P = 0.34).

The second health care consumption index concerns hospital admissions. As in the case of ER visits, our analyses only took into account hospitalizations associated with the main diagnosis. The data in Table 5 reveal that the number of patients in the intervention group who had stayed in hospital declined from 47 (pre) to 17 (per) to 10 (adjusted post), and the total number of hospital stays in this group fell from 88 (pre) to 25 (per) to 23 (adjusted post). This represents a 45% decrease in the number of hospitalizations following system deployment (from 88 to 48), a statistically

significant result (t = 2.8; P = 0.006). Given that the average number of days of hospitalization fell from 8.1 (pre) to 7.4 (per) to 5.7 (adjusted post), the total number of hospitalization days declined from 843 (pre) to 419 (per and adjusted post). This represents a 50% decrease (t = 3.2; P < 0.001).

Turning our attention to the patients in the control group, the data in Table 5 show that the number of patients who had been hospitalized at least once declined from 55 (pre) to 15 (per) and then remained relative stable at 16 (adjusted post). The total number of hospital admissions in this group fell from 104 (pre) to 28 (per) and then went up to 37 (adjusted post). We therefore observe a 38% decrease in the number of hospitalizations following system deployment (from 104 to 65), a statistically significant result (t = 2.5; P = 0.014). For its part, the average number of days of hospitalization in this group went from 10.3 (pre) to 15.5 (per) to 11.3 (adjusted post). As a result, the total number of hospitalization days declined from 908 (pre) to 648 (per and adjusted post), representing a 28% decrease (t = 1.2; P = 0.241).

In sum, the decrease in terms of total number of hospital days is superior in the experimental group compared to the control group, and the mean differences between the two groups are statistically significant (t = 3.0; P = 0.004).

The third index is related to home visits by nurses. As shown in Table 6, in the experimental group the total number of visits by nurses fell from 559 (pre) to 180 (per), then climbed back up to 459 (adjusted post). Combining the visits in the per- and post-periods and comparing the total number of visits associated with the pre-period, the

overall increase was 14% (t = −1.7; P = 0.096). In the control group, the total number of visits by nurses fell from 583 (pre) to 295 (per) and then climbed back up to 418 (adjusted post). Performing the same calculation, we arrive at a greater increase than that found in the experimental group: 22% (t = −2.7; P = 0.010). As expected, the difference between these two increases is not statistically significant (t = 0.7; P = 0.466). However, the difference in terms of the numbers of nurses’ visits during the intervention period (per) is statistically significant in favor of the experimental group (t = 4.1; P < 0.001).

The large increase in the number of nurses’ visits in the third period (for both groups) may be due to the fact that the care unit has a policy of re-assigning its nurses to different geographical areas each spring, which corresponds to the adjusted post-period of our study. The service area changes assign the nurses to new patient cohorts, and this change inevitably provokes a large increase in the number of home visits during the transition period. In addition, patients in the intervention group were reassigned to the caseload of home visiting nurses at the end of the 6-month intervention period. Considering the severity of the patients’ respiratory condition, home nurses may have decided to prioritize these patients and, hence, increase the frequency of home visits for them.

The total length of nurses’ visits in the experimental group rose from 25,925 minutes (pre) to 30,784 minutes (per + adjusted post), a statistically significant increase (t = −2.0; P = 0.046). The total length of these visits for the control group increased from 27,205 minutes to 32,231 minutes. In statistical terms, this was a greater increase than that observed in the experimental group (t = −2.2; P = 0.028). The difference between the two increases is not statistically significant (t = 0.5; P = 0.960). However, as in the number of nurses’ visits, we found a significant difference between the two groups in terms of the time spent by nurses in patient homes during the intervention period (t = 4.0; P < 0.001). Here again, this difference favors the experimental group, which consumes close to 5200 fewer minutes during this period.

The fourth and last index is related to home visits by respiratory therapists (Table 6). Our results show a 33% increase in the number of visits following deployment of the technological device in the control group (t = −2.3; P = 0.025) compared to an increase of 14% in the experimental group (t = 1.3; P = 0.192). The difference between the increases observed in these two groups is not statistically significant (t = 1.0; P = 0.339). Finally, once the technology was deployed in the control group the length

of visits by respiratory therapists increased 30% (t = −2.3; P = 0.026) compared to only an 18% increase in the experimental group (t = 1.5; P = 132). Once again, the difference between these two increases was not statistically significant.

## Patients’ satisfaction with the technological device

We asked the 60 participants in the experimental arm to indicate their satisfaction with the technological device at the end of the intervention. As shown in Table 7, the results indicate very high satisfaction among the vast majority of respondents, with an overall average of 4.0 on a scale of 5 (where 1 = not at all and 5 = enormously). Furthermore, in response to the question ‘‘If you could keep the automated telemonitoring system in your home, would you continue to use it in the future?’’, 86% said that they would.

## Cost-minimization analysis

The economic analysis strategy adopted for this study was one of cost-minimization [20—22]. This strategy can be

used to compare the costs of different interventions whose clinical results are considered similar in order to determine which costs the least. It provides a comparative basis that, beyond calculations of costs and benefits, must be able to indicate whether the new program appears to be better than the usual model. The purpose of the analysis performed for this assessment was to confirm the extent to which telehomecare leads to cost savings in comparison with usual care. In this regard, it is a ‘‘pre/post’’ comparison, where the ‘‘pre’’ situation serves as a benchmark for assessing the intervention’s effectiveness. As mentioned above, the economic analysis was focused on assessing the costs associated with healthcare service consumption before, during and after the intervention and comparing them to the costs in the control group.

This economic analysis was performed from a health system point of view. This means that costs incurred by patients were not considered. It is our view that such costs should not change the results of the economic assessment, since the costs associated with the technology used were assumed by the health facility and both intervention types (usual home care and telehomecare) took place in the home, so that the

expenses incurred by the patient should be similar. Finally, it should be noted that the calculations were made in Canadian dollars.

The cost incurred for nursing was calculated by multiplying the average hourly rate under the nurses’ collective agreement ($35.72/hour + 15% employee benefits) by the total time spent in home visits (time spent with patients). Similarly, the cost associated with respiratory therapist home visits was the product of the average hourly rate ($28.13 + 15% employee benefits) and the total time spent with patients. The travel cost takes into account the actual time spent by nurses on the road (average of 20 minutes per visit) and reimbursement of the mileage traveled (average of 12 km per visit) at the rate specified in the collective agreement: 43¢ per kilometer. The cost of hospitalizations and ER visits was estimated separately for each of the 60 patients. To this end, the average daily cost to hospitalize COPD patients in 2010—2011 was $714, and the average cost of a visit to the ER in 2010 was $1001.18.1 In order to have comparative data, we adjusted all the costs associated with the post-period to a 187-day period.

The cost of operating the telehomecare program (nursing costs) came to $25,263, representing $421 per patient. This is based on the fact that a total of 615 hours were spent

during the 6-month intervention program monitoring patients, making telephone calls as well as coordinating and following up on interventions in the field with road nurses and other health professionals. Finally, the costs associated with the technology were estimated using the prices negotiated with the application’s supplier amortized over a 3-year period. These costs include the costs associated with purchasing and installing equipment in the patients’ homes; purchasing, hosting and maintaining a regional server; purchasing and installing the user application; purchasing and installing the application server used by the nurse case managers; preparing clinical protocols; and providing professional services (including the training of nurses and technical services and support). The total estimate of these costs is $26,520, representing $442 per patient. Table 8 provides detailed information on the costs calculated for each of the three periods.

The above economic analysis provided positive results. First and foremost, the cumulative cost of the per and adjusted post-periods for the intervention group is $470,312, or $289,109 less than the pre-period. This represents savings of 38%, or $4818 per patient (t = 2.5; P = 0.014). For the control group, the difference between the preperiod and the following two periods represents savings of 24%, or $3205 per patient (t = 1.0; P = 0.300). As a result, we observe a net benefit of 14% in favor of the home telemonitoring program compared to the usual care services.

The main source of savings under telehomecare stemmed from drastic reductions to the number of hospitalizations and the average length of stay. Indeed, the annual savings associated with hospitalizations represented $438,990 in the intervention group (t = 2.9; P = 0.006) compared to $185,626 in the control group (t = 1.5; P = 0.146). It is also interesting to note that during the intervention period (per), the cost of hospitalizations represented 59% of the total costs for patients in the intervention group compared to 83% for those in the control group.

Although smaller in scale, additional savings were realized through fewer ER visits. Indeed, the cost savings associated with these visits totaled $43,309 (t = 2.4; P = 0.018) in the intervention group compared to $16,000 in the control group (t = 0.7; P = 0.472). As mentioned above, the number of home visits by both nurses and respiratory therapists slightly increased after system deployment (especially during the post-period) and, hence, no cost savings were realized at this level. However, the cost increase was not statistically significant for the intervention group (nurses: t = 1.7, P = 0.096; respiratory therapists: t = 1.3, P = 0.192), while it was significant for the control group (nurses: t = −2.7, P = 0.010; respiratory therapists: t = −2.3, P = 0.025).

Lastly, as expected, the savings achieved through the telehomecare program were partly reduced by the costs associated with using the technology and running the home telemonitoring program. These costs totaled $51,783, representing 21% of the costs associated with the per period (intervention group). Considering that patients participated in the home telemonitoring program for 178 days on average, the daily cost of the intervention per patient was $4.85.
